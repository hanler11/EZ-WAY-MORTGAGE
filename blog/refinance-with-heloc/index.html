Refinance With a HELOC: Options, Costs & Risks

Find out how to refinance with a HELOC, compare your options, understand costs, and learn what to consider before changing your home equity line.

A rising HELOC payment can catch you off guard, especially if your line is moving from the draw period into repayment. Refinancing may help you change the way you pay down your balance, seek a more predictable rate, or keep access to credit. Some homeowners refinance with a HELOC that replaces their current line, while others consider a fixed-rate home equity loan or cash-out refinance. Each option works differently and can affect your costs, first mortgage, and available equity. Learn how to compare the choices, understand qualification factors, and decide whether a refinance fits your household budget and plans.

What Does It Mean to Refinance a HELOC?

Refinancing a home equity line of credit means replacing your current HELOC with a new loan or credit line, or changing its repayment terms if your lender allows it. The best option depends on your balance, interest rate, payment schedule, and plans for your home equity. First, make sure you understand how your current HELOC works.

Use home equity through a revolving line of credit

A home equity line of credit, or HELOC, lets you borrow against the equity in your home. Unlike a home equity loan, which usually provides a lump sum, a HELOC is revolving credit: You can borrow, repay, and borrow again up to your limit while funds are available. Your home secures the debt, so falling behind on payments can put your property at risk.

Many HELOCs have a draw period followed by a repayment period. The length and terms vary, but some have a 10-year draw period and a 20-year repayment period, as Chase’s HELOC overview explains. Check your loan documents for your specific terms.

Understand how draw and repayment periods affect access and payments

During the draw period, you may borrow up to your available credit limit. Depending on your agreement, you may need to pay interest only or make payments toward both principal and interest. When the draw period ends, you typically can no longer access funds, and you begin repaying the balance under the terms of your HELOC.

The change can raise your monthly payment, particularly if you have been paying mostly interest. Yahoo Finance describes how the transition to repayment can affect borrowers’ budgets. If you refinance, compare the new payment schedule with your current one. A smaller payment may mean a longer payoff timeline, so consider both what you can afford each month and the total time you’ll need to repay the debt.

See how variable rates, interest-only payments, and rate caps work

Many HELOCs have variable interest rates that can change over time. As the rate changes, so may your payment. If your HELOC allows interest-only payments during the draw period, paying only the required amount may leave your principal balance unchanged.

Some agreements set rate caps, which limit how much the interest rate can rise at once or over the life of the line. A cap can help you understand your potential maximum rate, but it does not guarantee that payments will stay low. Review your loan documents for the rate index, lender’s margin, adjustment schedule, and applicable caps. Compare refinancing a HELOC with using one to pay off other debt

Refinancing a HELOC changes the terms of your current balance or replaces the existing line of credit. Using a HELOC to pay off other debt is different: You borrow against your home and use the funds to pay debts such as credit cards or personal loans. Consolidating balances may simplify payments or reduce interest, but it also secures that debt with your home.

Before consolidating, compare interest rates, fees, repayment terms, and the total amount you expect to pay. A lower monthly payment may reflect a longer repayment period rather than a lower overall cost. Consider whether you have a plan to avoid building up new debt after paying off existing balances. Why Refinance a HELOC?

Refinancing a home equity line of credit may help you adjust its rate, payment structure, or repayment timeline. Whether it makes sense depends on your current balance and terms, your financial goals, and the costs of getting a new loan or line of credit. Start by identifying what you want to change, then compare offers based on their full costs, not just the monthly payment.

Seek a lower rate or more predictable payments

A lower interest rate may reduce what you pay over time, particularly if you carry a sizable balance. Your finances or home equity may have changed since you opened the HELOC, which could affect the terms available to you. Another lender may also offer a structure that better fits your needs. Approval and rates depend on factors such as your credit, income, home value, and current debt.

If your HELOC has a variable rate, your payment can change as the rate changes. Refinancing may give you a more predictable payment, but fees and repayment terms can affect whether it’s worthwhile. Review the factors that can shape a HELOC refinance and compare the total cost with your current arrangement.

Convert a variable rate to fixed payments

Many HELOCs have variable rates, so payments may rise or fall over time. If you want more certainty in your budget, ask your current lender whether you can convert some or all of your balance to a fixed rate. You could also compare a fixed-rate home equity loan. Either option may make payments easier to plan for, but a fixed rate is not always lower than a variable rate.

Check whether a fixed-rate conversion limits access to the remaining credit line, and review any fees or minimum balance requirements. Compare the rate, monthly payment, and payoff timeline before choosing. Chase outlines common reasons to refinance a HELOC, including moving from a variable rate to fixed payments. Ask lenders to explain how the options apply to your balance.

Change the credit limit, draw period, or repayment schedule

Your borrowing needs may have changed since you opened your HELOC. You may want a lower credit limit to help manage spending, or you may need a larger line for a planned expense. A new HELOC could also change how long you can draw funds and when you must repay the balance. These terms affect both your access to credit and the payment you’ll need to make later.

A longer draw period may preserve access to funds, but it can delay principal repayment. A shorter repayment period may help you pay down the balance sooner, while raising monthly payments. Review the schedule and ask about application, appraisal, title, and early-termination fees. A refinance may change a HELOC’s draw and repayment periods, so check that the new terms fit your budget and payoff plan.

Consolidate higher-rate debt or access available equity

You might consider using a new HELOC or home equity loan to pay off higher-rate debt, such as credit card balances. Combining debts can simplify payments and may lower interest costs if the new financing has favorable terms. But this changes unsecured debt into debt secured by your home. Be sure the payment is manageable, and plan how you’ll avoid adding new balances after paying off existing ones.

A refinance may also let you borrow against available equity for a planned expense, subject to lender requirements and your overall debt. A cash-out refinance is another option, but it replaces your first mortgage as well as paying off the HELOC. Compare the new mortgage rate and fees with the cost of keeping your current mortgage and refinancing only the HELOC.

Match your HELOC to your budget, goals, or payoff plan

A refinance may make sense when your current HELOC no longer fits your plans. You may want a set payoff date, steadier payments, or continued access to funds for a specific project. First, decide how quickly you want to repay the balance and what monthly payment fits your budget. Then compare options by interest rate, fees, payment structure, and total interest over the loan term.

Also consider how long you expect to stay in your home. Closing costs may take time to offset, while a longer repayment term may reduce monthly payments but increase total interest. What Are Your HELOC Refinancing Options?

Refinancing a home equity line of credit (HELOC) means replacing it with a new loan or changing how you repay the balance. Depending on your lender, finances, available home equity, and goals, you may be able to open a new HELOC, convert some or all of your balance to a fixed rate, or use a home equity loan or cash-out refinance to pay it off. You can also keep your current line if a new loan would cost more than it saves.

Start by reviewing your HELOC’s balance, interest rate, monthly payment, and remaining draw or repayment period. Then decide what you want to change. You may prioritize predictable payments, a lower rate, continued access to credit, or a clear payoff date. Some options can help with one goal while adding fees, extending repayment, or changing the terms of your first mortgage.

Compare written offers based on the full cost, not just the advertised rate or initial payment. Ask about closing costs, repayment terms, rate changes, and whether you can borrow again. Lenders may have different eligibility rules, and approval generally depends on factors such as your credit, income, debts, and home equity. Review your current HELOC terms before comparing options, so you know what you’d gain and what you might give up.

Replace your HELOC with a new HELOC

You may be able to replace your current line of credit with a new HELOC. The new line can pay off the existing balance, and its terms may offer a different rate, credit limit, draw period, or repayment schedule. This may suit you if you still want flexible access to credit but your current terms no longer fit your needs.

Compare the rate structure, fees, draw and repayment periods, and any minimum withdrawal requirements. An introductory rate may change later, and a new draw period could give you more time to borrow while extending the time it takes to repay the debt. You’ll also need to qualify for the new line, which may involve a credit review and an assessment of your home’s value and available equity. Learn how replacing a HELOC works and compare the new agreement with your current one.

Ask whether you can convert your balance to a fixed rate

Some HELOC lenders let borrowers convert part or all of an outstanding balance to a fixed rate. This can make payments more predictable and help you set a clearer payoff plan. Your lender may call this feature a fixed-rate option or rate lock, but availability and terms vary.

Ask whether the conversion applies to your full balance or only a portion, how long the fixed rate lasts, and whether fees or minimum balance requirements apply. New amounts you borrow may still have a variable rate, even if you’ve fixed the rate on an existing balance. If your lender doesn’t offer this feature, compare other options before making a change. Refinancing can involve paperwork, but having your loan and financial documents ready may help make the process more manageable, as Newrez explains in its guide to refinancing.

Replace your HELOC balance with a fixed-rate home equity loan

A home equity loan provides a lump sum you can use to pay off your HELOC. It typically has a fixed interest rate and scheduled payments over a set term. This may be a good fit if you don’t need continued access to a revolving credit line and want a consistent payment and payoff timeline.

Before choosing this route, compare the interest rate, fees, loan term, monthly payment, and total amount you’ll repay. A longer term may lower the monthly payment but increase the total interest paid. The loan is usually secured by your home, so make sure the payment fits your budget even if your income or expenses change. See how a fixed-rate home equity loan compares with a HELOC to assess the trade-offs in payment certainty and access to funds.

Use a cash-out refinance to pay off your HELOC and first mortgage

A cash-out refinance replaces your first mortgage with a new, larger mortgage. The new loan pays off your existing mortgage and HELOC, and you may receive remaining funds after the debts and closing costs are paid. This can combine the balances into one mortgage, but it also changes the rate and terms on your first home loan.

Compare the new mortgage rate with your current first-mortgage rate, along with closing costs, loan term, monthly payment, and total interest. If your current mortgage has a lower rate, replacing it could increase your borrowing costs, even if you end up with one payment. Ask for a written estimate that shows the new loan amount, any cash you’d receive, and the projected payoff timeline. Compare a cash-out refinance with a HELOC before deciding whether to combine the loans.

Keep your HELOC and compare the cost of making no change

Refinancing isn’t always the best choice. If your current rate, payment, and repayment schedule work for you, keeping the HELOC may cost less than opening a new loan. You can continue paying down the balance without taking on new fees or extending the repayment period.

For a useful comparison, check your current rate and whether it can change, your outstanding balance, and how much time remains in the draw period. Estimate the total cost of keeping the line and compare it with each refinancing option, including fees and future interest. Also consider whether you need continued access to credit and how soon you plan to pay off the balance. What Costs and Terms Should You Compare?

When you refinance a HELOC, compare more than the interest rate or the first payment shown in an offer. Look at the full cost of borrowing, how long you’ll make payments, whether the rate can change, and whether you’ll still be able to draw funds. A loan with a lower payment may cost more over time if it extends repayment or starts a new draw period.

Start by reviewing your current HELOC statement and loan documents. Note your balance, rate, monthly payment, remaining draw period, and any fees for closing the account early. Then compare those details with written estimates for each option you’re considering. Ask lenders to explain fees, payment changes, and payoff timelines in plain English.

Your priorities matter, too. You may want stable payments, access to funds for planned expenses, or a shorter path to paying off the balance. There isn’t one right structure for every homeowner. Compare rates, payments, flexibility, and access to funds

Compare the interest rate alongside the payment structure and features of each offer. Find out whether payments can change, whether you can continue drawing from the line, and how much credit will remain available. A new HELOC may provide another draw period, while a home equity loan typically provides a set amount with scheduled payments.

Think about how you plan to use the funds. If you expect major repairs, continued access to credit could be important. If your main goal is to pay down your existing balance, a structure with predictable payments may be a better fit. Refinancing may help you seek a lower rate, fixed payments, or access to equity, but the right choice depends on the terms offered. Review closing costs, appraisal fees, lender charges, and early-termination fees

Request an itemized list of costs before you commit. Refinancing may involve an appraisal, lender or origination fees, title work, credit reporting, and recording fees. Your current HELOC may also charge an early-termination fee if you close the account within a set period. These costs can reduce the savings from a lower rate.

Fees vary by lender, loan, and property. One estimate puts HELOC refinancing costs at about 2% to 6% of the loan amount, but your own written estimate will give you a more useful figure. Ask which charges are due upfront, which can be added to the loan, and whether fees are refundable if the loan does not close. Compare the total costs of each offer, not just the advertised rate.

Check introductory rates, rate changes, and rate caps

An introductory rate may last only for a limited time. Ask when it ends, what rate applies afterward, and how often that rate can change. Many HELOCs have variable rates tied to an index such as the prime rate, so your interest rate and payment may rise or fall as the index changes.

Ask how the lender calculates your rate, including the margin added to the index, how often adjustments occur, and whether the loan has a rate cap. A cap limits how high the rate can rise, but the rules vary by loan. Request payment examples at the current rate and at higher rates. That can help you judge whether the payment would still fit your budget if borrowing costs increase.

Compare total interest and payoff timelines, not just monthly payments

A smaller monthly payment does not necessarily mean a lower overall cost. Extending repayment can reduce what you owe each month while adding more time for interest to accrue. A new draw period can also make it easier to borrow again, which may delay paying off the balance.

Compare the estimated total interest and payoff date for each option. Include closing costs, any fee for ending your current HELOC, and the balance you expect to carry. Ask lenders to use the same balance and repayment assumptions so you can compare offers fairly. A full cost and payoff comparison can show whether a lower payment comes with a longer repayment timeline or higher total interest.

Consider how a new HELOC affects your first mortgage and lien position

A HELOC is secured by your home, and its lien position can affect a refinance of your first mortgage. If you refinance the first mortgage but keep the HELOC, the HELOC lender may need to agree to stay in second position behind the new mortgage. This approval is called subordination, and it is not automatic.

Ask your mortgage professional and both lenders whether a proposed refinance could affect your existing liens, costs, or closing timeline. If the HELOC lender does not approve subordination, you may need to consider paying off the HELOC as part of the first-mortgage refinance or reviewing another option. Raise the question early so you know whether the loans can work together before proceeding.

Ask a tax professional whether you can deduct interest for your intended use

Do not assume the interest on a HELOC is tax-deductible. Whether it qualifies depends on your circumstances, how you use the funds, and current tax rules. In some cases, interest may be deductible when funds are used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home securing the loan, subject to applicable limits and requirements.

Keep records of how you use the money and ask a qualified tax professional how the rules apply to you. A mortgage lender or broker can explain loan features and costs, but cannot determine your tax treatment. Do You Qualify to Refinance a HELOC?

Qualifying to refinance a home equity line of credit (HELOC) depends on your overall financial picture, not one number alone. Lenders typically review your credit, income, existing debts, home value, and the amount you owe. They’ll also consider the new loan’s terms and whether your property meets their requirements. Because lenders and loan programs set different standards, general benchmarks can help you prepare, but they can’t guarantee approval.

Start by gathering your latest HELOC statement, mortgage statement, income records, and an estimate of your home’s value. Then look at how a new payment could fit your budget, especially if your current line is nearing the end of its draw period. If you’re comparing a new HELOC with a home equity loan or cash-out refinance, ask how each option affects your monthly payment, interest rate, and total repayment.

Check your credit score, history, and payment record

Your credit score and payment history can influence whether you qualify and what rate a lender offers. Review your home value, available equity, and combined loan-to-value ratio

Lenders compare your home’s estimated value with the debts secured by it. This includes your first mortgage and the HELOC you plan to refinance. The combined loan-to-value ratio, or CLTV, shows how much you owe against the home’s value. A lower CLTV generally means more equity remains after the refinance, though each lender sets its own limits.

Some lenders may require homeowners to retain 15% to 20% equity, but that is not a universal rule. Point’s guide to refinancing a HELOC explains how available equity can affect eligibility. Recent comparable home sales can provide a rough estimate of value, but the lender will determine the value used for underwriting. If your estimate is close to a lender’s maximum CLTV, ask how an appraisal could affect your application.

Assess your debt-to-income ratio, income stability, and existing obligations

Your debt-to-income ratio, or DTI, compares your monthly debt payments with your gross monthly income. Lenders may prefer a DTI of 40% or less, though acceptable limits vary. Some guidance suggests keeping debt below 43% of gross income may improve approval prospects. These figures are benchmarks, not guarantees, since lenders consider other parts of your application too. Point discusses DTI and other qualification factors.

Include your mortgage, car payments, student loans, credit card minimums, and the expected new HELOC payment when estimating your DTI. Lenders also assess whether your income is stable and likely to continue. If your income has changed, or you receive commissions or seasonal pay, ask how the lender will calculate it. A clear picture of your income and obligations helps you assess whether the proposed payment fits your budget.

Consider your HELOC balance and whether you’re in the draw or repayment period

Check your current HELOC balance, rate, minimum payment, remaining draw period, and repayment schedule. During the draw period, your agreement may allow you to borrow from the line again. When that period ends, you may no longer have access to additional funds, and payments can rise as you begin repaying principal. Yahoo Finance explains why the transition to repayment can surprise borrowers.

A lender will review how much you owe and whether the new financing can pay off the existing HELOC. Gather a recent statement and your original loan documents so you can compare the current terms with a refinance offer. Pay close attention to whether the new loan restarts a draw period or changes your repayment timeline. A lower initial payment may not mean a lower total cost if repayment takes longer.

Prepare income and asset documents, including if you’re self-employed

Lenders verify your income, assets, debts, and property information as part of the application. They may ask for recent pay stubs, W-2 forms, tax returns, bank statements, identification, homeowners insurance details, and information about outstanding debts. Document lists vary, so request a checklist from the lender before applying. Having records ready can make it easier to answer questions and avoid delays.

If you’re self-employed, be prepared to provide tax returns and additional records that show your income and business activity. A lender may review income over time rather than relying on a single month’s deposits. If your earnings vary, ask which documents will help explain the pattern. Check property type, location, and lender requirements

A lender may consider where your home is located, how you use it, and whether its property type meets the loan program’s rules. What Are the Risks of Refinancing a HELOC?

Refinancing a home equity line of credit can change your rate, payment structure, or access to funds, but it does not guarantee lower costs. A new loan may include fees, extend your repayment timeline, or expose you to rate changes that affect your budget. Compare the full cost of the new option with your current HELOC, including how long you expect to carry the balance.

Remember, a HELOC is secured by your home. If you fall behind on payments, your property could be at risk. Before making a decision, review the terms and consider how they fit your household budget and payoff plan. A mortgage professional can explain the trade-offs and help you compare available loan programs.

A refinance may not lower your payment or total borrowing cost

A lower advertised rate does not always mean a less expensive loan. The new HELOC or loan may include closing costs, a longer repayment term, or a rate structure that increases the interest you pay over time. Even if your monthly payment goes down, extending the time you carry a balance may raise your total cost.

Compare the new offer with your current HELOC using the same assumptions: your balance, expected payment, fees, rate changes, and payoff timeline. Ask the lender to explain both the monthly payment and estimated total cost. Amerisave’s guide to refinancing a HELOC describes common reasons homeowners refinance, but whether it makes sense depends on the terms you’re offered and how you plan to repay.

Restarting the draw period can extend borrowing and increase long-term interest

A replacement HELOC may come with a new draw period, which lets you borrow from the line again. That flexibility can help with planned expenses, but it may also make it easier to borrow more before paying down your existing balance. If refinancing restarts the timeline, you could carry debt for longer and pay more interest.

Check when the draw period ends, whether payments are interest-only during that time, and when principal repayment begins. Then make a realistic plan to reduce the balance instead of treating available credit as extra income. A longer draw period may keep payments lower at first, but it can also delay the point when you start making meaningful progress on the principal.

Longer repayment terms can lower monthly payments but increase total interest

Spreading a balance over more months may reduce your required payment, which can offer some room in your monthly budget. However, a longer term also gives interest more time to accrue. You could end up paying more overall, even if the new payment feels more manageable.

Compare the monthly payment and the estimated total interest through payoff before you choose a term. If the new loan runs longer, ask whether you can make extra principal payments without a penalty. Consider whether you can afford to pay more than the minimum, especially if your goal is to clear the debt sooner. PNC’s overview of home equity borrowing options notes that a longer term or higher rate can increase the interest paid over time.

Variable rates can change payments, while fixed-rate options may cost more upfront

Many HELOCs have variable rates, so your payment can rise or fall as the rate changes. A fixed-rate option can make payments more predictable, but it may come with a higher initial rate or different fees. The best fit depends on your budget, tolerance for payment changes, and expected payoff timeline.

Review the rate index, margin, adjustment schedule, and any rate caps in the loan documents. Ask how a rate increase would change your payment, rather than focusing only on the amount due today. PNC explains common differences between HELOCs and fixed-rate borrowing, but your lender’s specific terms will determine how the loan works.

Fees may outweigh savings, especially if you plan to sell soon

Refinancing may involve application, appraisal, title, and other closing costs. If the new terms reduce your borrowing costs, you may recoup those expenses over time. But if you plan to sell your home soon, you may move before the savings make up for the fees. A lower monthly payment by itself does not show whether refinancing is worthwhile.

Ask for a written estimate of upfront and ongoing costs, then calculate how long it would take for expected savings to cover them. Check for early-termination fees on your current HELOC, too. Point’s guide to refinancing a HELOC recommends comparing total costs, not just monthly payments. If you’re unsure how to assess an offer, ask a mortgage professional to walk through the numbers with you.

Unpaid debt secured by your home can put the property at risk

A refinanced HELOC is generally still secured by your home. If you cannot make the required payments, the lender may take steps that could ultimately lead to foreclosure. This makes it important to borrow only what you can reasonably repay, even if you qualify for a larger credit line.

Before signing, review the payment terms and consider how they would fit your budget if your income changed or expenses increased. Leave room for emergencies, and contact your lender promptly if you expect difficulty making payments. Home equity can be a useful source of financing, but it differs from unsecured debt because your property serves as collateral for the balance.

Debt consolidation does not prevent new balances from accumulating

Using a HELOC refinance to pay off credit cards or other higher-rate debt may simplify payments or reduce interest costs. But paying off those accounts does not address the expenses or habits that led to the balances. If you keep using the cards without a repayment plan, you could accumulate new debt while still owing money on a HELOC secured by your home.

Before consolidating, make a monthly budget and decide how you’ll manage the accounts you pay off. You might pause new card spending, adjust credit limits, or set automatic payments for purchases you continue to make. Treat consolidation as one part of a broader payoff plan, and confirm that the new loan’s fees, rate, and repayment schedule support your goals.

How Can You Decide Whether to Refinance a HELOC?

A HELOC refinance should fit your finances now and your plan for paying off the balance. Compare your current line with the proposed loan, and look beyond the advertised rate or monthly payment. These steps can help you weigh the trade-offs and ask informed questions before you decide.

Compare the new loan’s full cost and terms with your current HELOC

Write down your current balance, interest rate, monthly payment, remaining draw period, and repayment schedule. Then compare those details with the proposed loan’s written terms. Include closing costs, appraisal and lender fees, any early-termination charge, and the interest you may pay over the time you expect to carry the debt.

A lower monthly payment does not always mean a lower overall cost. It may come from extending the repayment period, which can mean paying interest for longer. As Point explains, refinancing may make payments more manageable or predictable, but fees and a longer term can increase the total cost. Ask the lender to show how the monthly payment and estimated payoff cost compare with your current HELOC.

Match the payment structure to your budget and payoff plan

Think about the payment you can manage comfortably and how quickly you want to pay off the balance. A fixed-rate home equity loan usually provides a lump sum with predictable payments. A HELOC may preserve access to funds, but its rate and payment can change according to the loan terms. The better fit depends on whether payment certainty or borrowing flexibility matters more to you.

Review your income, regular expenses, and other debt payments before choosing. If you plan to pay down the balance faster, ask whether you can make extra payments without a penalty. If you need a lower required payment, find out whether a longer repayment period would increase total interest. A comparison of HELOCs and home equity loans can help you understand how each option supports a different repayment plan.

Consider how long you expect to keep your home

Refinancing comes with costs, so estimate how long it could take for potential savings to offset them. If the new loan would lower your monthly payment, compare the expected savings over the time you plan to stay in the home with the fees you would pay to refinance. If you expect to move soon, you may not have enough time for the savings to make up for those costs.

This is an estimate, not a guarantee, since plans and loan costs can change. Consider possible changes such as a new job, a growing family, or a move to another part of New Jersey. AmeriSave’s HELOC refinancing guide also recommends considering how long you expect to stay in your home when evaluating a refinance.

Weigh flexible access against predictable payments

A HELOC lets you borrow from an available credit line over time, which may help if you expect upcoming expenses and want to draw funds as needed. Refinancing into a home equity loan usually means receiving a lump sum instead. That can make repayment more predictable, but it may not provide the same ability to borrow again as you repay the balance.

Consider how you expect to use the funds before choosing. If you want continued access to credit, ask about the new HELOC’s limit, draw period, rate, and repayment schedule. If your priority is paying off a known balance with steady payments, compare fixed-rate options. PNC’s overview of HELOCs and home equity loans explains differences in payment structure, flexibility, and costs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I refinance a HELOC without refinancing my first mortgage?
Yes. You may be able to replace your HELOC with a new HELOC or a home equity loan while keeping your first mortgage. A cash-out refinance is different because it replaces your first mortgage as well as paying off the HELOC. Compare rates, fees, and payment terms before choosing.

Does refinancing a HELOC always lower my monthly payment?
No. Your payment depends on the new interest rate, balance, and repayment schedule. A longer term may lower the required monthly payment but increase the total interest you pay. Review the full payoff estimate, not just the monthly amount.

What information should I gather before applying?
Have your latest HELOC and mortgage statements, loan documents, income records, and details about your other debts ready. Lenders may also ask for bank statements and information about your property. Requirements vary, so request a document checklist from the lender.

Can I refinance a HELOC if my credit or income has changed?
You can apply, but approval and terms depend on the lender’s review of your credit, income, debts, home value, and available equity. If your income varies or you’re self-employed, ask what records the lender needs to assess it.
