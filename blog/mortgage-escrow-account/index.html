Mortgage Escrow Account: Taxes, Insurance, and Payment Changes

Learn how a mortgage escrow account collects property taxes and insurance, why payments change, and what to check in your annual escrow statement.

A mortgage escrow account can make it easier to handle large property-tax and insurance bills by collecting part of those costs with your monthly mortgage payment. If you own a home in New Jersey, understanding the account can help you make sense of payment changes and spot questions in your annual statement.

What is a mortgage escrow account?

A mortgage escrow account is an account managed by a mortgage servicer to hold money collected from you for certain homeownership bills. The servicer generally pays property taxes and homeowners insurance from that account when they are due. Depending on the loan and property, other items such as mortgage insurance or flood insurance may also be included.

Escrow is separate from the amount that pays down your loan balance and interest. It is a way to set aside funds throughout the year for bills that may otherwise arrive as larger, less frequent payments. Your loan documents and servicer statement identify the items included in your particular account.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains federal rules for escrow accounts, including how servicers handle funds for taxes and insurance. See its escrow account regulation for details.

How does escrow collect and pay the bills?

The servicer estimates the bills it expects to pay during the coming year. It divides that amount into installments and adds an escrow portion to your monthly payment. The servicer holds those funds, then sends payments to the tax authority or insurance provider according to their billing schedules.

  1. Estimate annual bills. The servicer uses available tax and insurance information to project upcoming costs.
  2. Collect monthly installments. A portion of each payment goes into the escrow account.
  3. Pay bills when due. The servicer sends the funds to the relevant billing organizations.
  4. Review and adjust. The servicer compares the estimate with actual bills and recalculates the amount needed for the next period.

Why can a mortgage payment change?

Your total payment may change even when the principal-and-interest portion stays the same. The escrow portion is based on projected tax and insurance bills, which can change over time. In New Jersey, property-tax bills are determined by local taxing authorities, while insurance premiums depend on the policy and insurer. A change in either can affect the servicer's estimate.

Other reasons for a different escrow amount include a prior estimate that was lower or higher than the actual bills, a change in the bills being paid from escrow, or an account balance that needs correction under applicable rules. If the account paid more than expected, the statement may show a surplus. If it paid less, it may show a shortage and explain how the servicer will address it. Read the statement rather than assuming every payment change is an increase in the loan cost.

What should you review in the annual escrow statement?

Use the statement as a year-to-year comparison. Keep the current statement with your tax and insurance records, and check that its projections and payments make sense.

  • Starting and ending balances: Compare the opening balance, deposits, bill payments, and projected closing balance.
  • Tax payments: Confirm the tax bills and payment dates shown match the property and the bills you received.
  • Insurance payments: Check the insurer, policy period, and amount paid. If you changed coverage or providers, verify that the servicer has current information.
  • Next year's estimate: Look at each projected expense and how it translates into a monthly escrow amount.
  • Shortage or surplus: Review the explanation and any repayment or refund options described by the servicer.
  • Contact details and deadlines: Note how to ask questions and when any stated action is due.

If a bill seems missing or an amount does not match your records, contact your mortgage servicer using the contact information on your statement. Keep copies of bills, insurance declarations, and correspondence. How can you prepare for a change?

Build room in your household budget for the possibility that taxes or insurance will change. When you receive a new tax bill or insurance renewal, compare it with the figures in your escrow analysis. Send updated insurance information to your servicer if requested, and ask how a shortage will be handled before assuming the monthly amount or timing.

When comparing a home loan, ask what the estimated payment includes and which costs may be paid outside escrow. Estimates are not guarantees: actual tax bills and insurance costs can differ from projections. A clear explanation of principal, interest, escrow, and any separate costs can make the figures easier to understand. Questions to ask when reviewing escrow

Does escrow cover every homeownership expense?

No. It usually covers specified bills, commonly property taxes and homeowners insurance. Repairs, utilities, and other expenses generally remain separate unless your loan arrangement specifically says otherwise.

Can I choose not to have an escrow account?

That depends on your loan terms and applicable requirements. Ask your servicer or mortgage professional to explain the rules for your specific loan rather than assuming escrow can be removed.

Should I pay a tax or insurance bill myself if I have escrow?

First check whether the bill is included in escrow and whether the servicer is scheduled to pay it. If you are unsure, contact the servicer promptly to avoid duplicate payment or a missed deadline.

Who can explain a change in my escrow payment?

Your servicer can explain its analysis, the bills it expects to pay, and how any shortage or surplus is handled. A mortgage professional can help explain how escrow fits into a loan estimate, but cannot replace the servicer's account records.
