What Is a Home Equity Loan and How Does It Work?

A home equity loan lets you borrow against the portion of your home you own. Your equity is the difference between your property’s current market value and the balance remaining on your mortgage. For example, if your home is worth $450,000 and you owe $300,000, you have approximately $150,000 in equity before accounting for other liens, closing costs, and the lender’s borrowing limits.

Unlike a personal loan, a home equity loan is secured by your property. You receive the approved amount as one lump sum, then repay it through scheduled monthly payments. Because the loan is separate from your existing mortgage, it is often called a second mortgage.

Before applying, consider why you want to borrow, how much you need, and what monthly payment fits your budget. Lenders will also review your credit history, income, debts, mortgage balance, property, and payment history. Get a lump-sum second mortgage with predictable payments

A home equity loan provides a set amount of money upfront. If you already have a mortgage, the new loan usually becomes a second lien on your property. You continue paying your first mortgage while making a separate payment on the home equity loan.

This structure can work well when you know exactly how much you need. Common uses include a renovation, roof replacement, debt consolidation, or another planned expense with a defined cost. The lender determines your approved amount after reviewing your equity, credit, income, debts, property, and payment history.

Many home equity loans have fixed interest rates and fixed monthly payments, though terms vary by lender and borrower. Bankrate explains the typical structure in its guide to how home equity loans work. Predictable payments can make budgeting easier, but you should still compare the full cost before accepting an offer.

Understand principal, interest, and amortization

Each payment generally includes principal and interest. The principal is the amount you borrowed, while interest is the lender’s charge for providing the funds. As you repay the loan, the principal balance gradually decreases.

This repayment process is called amortization. Your loan documents should explain the interest rate, payment amount, repayment term, total interest, and other charges. Home equity loan terms commonly range from five to 30 years, although available options depend on the lender and your qualifications.

A longer term may result in a lower monthly payment, but you could pay more interest over the life of the loan. A shorter term may require a higher monthly payment while reducing the time interest accrues. Review both the monthly payment and total repayment amount. Compare home equity loans and HELOCs

A home equity loan gives you a specific amount upfront. You then make regular payments based on the balance, interest rate, and repayment term. This may be a good fit for a one-time expense with a known price, such as a kitchen renovation or major home repair.

A home equity line of credit, or HELOC, works more like a revolving credit account. You can draw funds as needed during the draw period, up to an approved limit. Your payments may change as you borrow, repay, and draw again. Many HELOCs also have variable interest rates, which means the cost can rise or fall.

A home equity loan may be easier to budget because the rate and payment are often fixed. A HELOC may provide more flexibility when expenses will occur over time or the final cost is uncertain. Compare the draw period, repayment period, interest rate, fees, and payment rules before choosing.

Compare home equity loans, cash-out refinancing, and personal loans

A home equity loan is one of several ways to access funds. With cash-out refinancing, you replace your existing mortgage with a new, larger mortgage and receive part of the difference in cash. This may change your interest rate, loan term, closing costs, and monthly payment.

A home equity loan leaves your first mortgage in place and adds a separate payment. That may be worth considering if your current mortgage has a favorable rate that you do not want to replace. However, you still need enough equity and income to qualify for the additional debt.

A personal loan is usually unsecured, so your home does not directly secure the balance. Its interest rate may be higher because the lender takes on more risk. A home equity loan may offer a lower rate because the property secures the debt, but missed payments could put the home at risk. Compare the total cost, payment, flexibility, and consequences of default for each option.

Match the loan to your borrowing purpose

Start with the reason you want to borrow. A home equity loan may suit a planned project with a defined budget, such as replacing a roof, remodeling a bathroom, or adding accessibility features. It may also help cover a large, known expense or consolidate higher-interest debt, as long as the new payment fits your budget.

Avoid borrowing more than you need simply because you qualify for a larger amount. Additional debt can affect your monthly cash flow, future refinancing plans, and the equity available when you sell. If expenses will happen gradually or the final cost is uncertain, a HELOC may offer greater flexibility. For a smaller expense, compare an unsecured personal loan or available savings.

Use your home as collateral

When you take out a home equity loan, your property secures the debt. If you make payments as agreed, the loan follows its scheduled repayment plan. If you fall seriously behind, the lender may have legal remedies, including the possibility of foreclosure, depending on the circumstances and applicable law.

This does not mean a home equity loan automatically puts your home in immediate danger. It does mean you should treat the payment as a long-term housing obligation. Add it to your budget alongside your first mortgage, property taxes, homeowners insurance, utilities, and other debts.

Ask the lender what happens if you miss a payment, whether late fees apply, and how financial hardship may be handled. Also consider how the loan could affect a future sale or refinance, since the balance generally must be paid off or addressed when ownership changes. How Much Home Equity Can You Borrow?

Your home equity may provide access to meaningful funds, but you usually cannot borrow every dollar you have invested in the property. Lenders consider your home’s current value, mortgage balance, other liens, credit history, income, and the payment your budget can reasonably handle.

The maximum loan amount also varies by lender and loan program. Before applying, estimate your available equity, review your combined loan-to-value ratio, and account for appraisal and closing costs. A mortgage professional can compare loan options and explain how much you may qualify to borrow.

Calculate your equity from your home’s value and mortgage balance

Start by estimating your home’s current market value. Then subtract the unpaid principal balance on your existing mortgage. The result is your estimated home equity.

For example, if your home is worth $450,000 and you owe $300,000 on your mortgage, your estimated equity is $150,000. This figure represents the portion of the property you own, not necessarily the amount a lender will allow you to borrow.

Your mortgage statement may show your current principal balance, but it may not include interest, fees, or other amounts needed for a complete payoff. Request a payoff statement for a more precise figure. You can also review the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s explanation of home equity before estimating your borrowing capacity.

Include second liens and other property-secured debts

Your first mortgage is not the only debt that may affect your borrowing capacity. Include any existing home equity loan, HELOC, tax lien, judgment lien, or other obligation secured by the property. These debts reduce the equity available for a new loan.

A lender generally reviews the total amount owed against the home, not just the balance on your first mortgage. If you already have a HELOC, the lender may consider the outstanding balance or, depending on its guidelines, the full credit line. Ask how the lender handles an existing line of credit.

Review your mortgage records and credit reports for obligations you may have overlooked. The Federal Trade Commission’s guidance on home equity loans explains why all property-secured debts should be included in your calculations.

Estimate your combined loan-to-value ratio

Combined loan-to-value ratio, or CLTV, compares all property-secured debt with your home’s value. To estimate it, add your first mortgage balance, second mortgage balance, HELOC balance or applicable credit limit, and other liens. Divide that total by the estimated property value.

For example, if your home is worth $450,000 and your total property-secured debt is $360,000, your estimated CLTV is 80%. Lenders use this ratio to assess how much of the property’s value would support all of your mortgages.

A lower CLTV may give you more loan options, while a higher CLTV can lead to stricter approval standards, a smaller loan amount, or a higher interest rate. Lenders may calculate CLTV differently, especially when a HELOC is involved, so confirm the calculation before comparing offers.

Confirm your home’s value with an appraisal

Your estimate is useful for planning, but the lender may require an appraisal or another valuation method before approving the loan. This valuation confirms the property’s market value and gives the lender a consistent basis for calculating your CLTV.

An appraisal may consider recent comparable sales, the home’s size, condition, location, upgrades, and other property details. If the valuation comes in lower than expected, you may have less available equity than your initial estimate suggested.

Ask who orders the appraisal, who pays for it, and whether the fee is refundable if you do not close. Some lenders may use an automated valuation model or broker price opinion, depending on the loan amount and property type. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains how home appraisals work.

Check each lender’s equity and CLTV limits

Lenders set their own maximum CLTV limits based on the property, loan type, borrower profile, and market conditions. One lender may allow a higher total loan-to-value ratio, while another may require you to keep more equity in the home.

Do not assume that one percentage applies to every borrower. Your credit score, debt-to-income ratio, occupancy, property type, and lien position can affect the maximum amount available. A primary residence may receive different treatment from an investment property or second home.

When comparing lenders, ask about the maximum CLTV, minimum and maximum loan amounts, and restrictions on condos, multi-unit properties, or manufactured homes. Review EZ Way Mortgage Corporation’s loan programs to learn more about financing options that may fit your property and borrowing needs.

Subtract closing costs from your loan proceeds

The amount you qualify to borrow is not always the amount you receive in cash. A home equity loan may involve origination charges, appraisal fees, title services, recording fees, credit report fees, and other closing costs.

Some borrowers pay these expenses upfront, while others finance them as part of the new loan. Financing the costs may reduce the cash available for your project and increase your total balance and monthly payment. Ask for a written estimate that separates the loan amount, financed fees, and expected funds delivered at closing.

For example, if you borrow $50,000 and $2,000 in eligible costs comes from the proceeds, you may receive approximately $48,000 before other adjustments. Review the Loan Estimate carefully when one is provided, and ask about any fee you do not recognize.

Understand why lenders do not lend your full equity

Having $150,000 in equity does not usually mean you can borrow $150,000. Lenders generally require you to keep a portion of the property’s value as a cushion. That cushion helps protect the lender and your finances if home values decline or you experience payment problems.

The lender also reviews your income, credit history, existing debts, and ability to make the new payment. Even a borrower with substantial equity may not qualify for the maximum amount if the payment would place too much strain on the household budget.

Keeping some equity in the property can also help if you sell later. The sale proceeds may need to repay your first mortgage, home equity loan or HELOC, selling costs, and other liens. Leaving room between the home’s value and total debt may make the sale or future refinance easier to manage.

Choose an affordable loan amount

Treat the lender’s maximum approval as an upper limit, not a recommended target. Start with the purpose of the loan, estimate the amount required, and leave room for unexpected expenses. Then compare the proposed payment with your housing costs, utilities, insurance, property taxes, debt payments, savings goals, and emergency fund contributions.

A fixed-rate home equity loan can provide predictable payments, but the new payment still adds to your monthly obligations. If you borrow more than necessary, you may pay interest on money that does not serve your goal. Consider whether you can complete the expense in stages or cover part of it with savings.

Before choosing an amount, ask how the payment would fit if your income changed or another major expense arose. A mortgage broker can help compare scenarios, but the final amount should support your budget rather than simply match the largest offer available.

Are You Eligible for a Home Equity Loan?

A home equity loan can provide a lump sum for renovations, major expenses, debt consolidation, or another planned financial goal. Approval, however, depends on more than the amount of equity you have. Lenders also review your credit history, income, existing debts, property details, ownership, and ability to manage a new monthly payment alongside your current mortgage.

Requirements vary by lender and loan program. There is no single credit score, debt-to-income ratio, or equity percentage that guarantees approval. Reviewing your finances before applying can help you identify potential issues, estimate a realistic loan amount, and prepare the documents an underwriter may request. Review your credit score and payment history

Your credit score helps lenders evaluate how you have managed borrowed money. A higher score may improve your approval chances and help you qualify for more favorable interest rate and fee options. Lenders may also review your payment history, credit card balances, installment loans, collections, and recent credit inquiries.

There is no universal minimum score for every home equity loan. Some lenders may consider borrowers with lower scores when they have substantial equity, reliable income, and manageable debts. Review your credit reports before applying through AnnualCreditReport.com, the federally authorized source for free credit reports. Check for late payments, incorrect balances, or accounts you do not recognize. If you find an error, dispute it before submitting a loan application.

Verify your income, employment, and self-employed borrower documents

Lenders need to confirm that your income is stable and sufficient to cover your existing mortgage and proposed home equity loan payment. Employees may need recent pay stubs, W-2 forms, and employment details. If you receive commission, bonus, overtime, rental, alimony, or other income, the lender may request additional records before counting it as qualifying income.

Self-employed borrowers often provide personal and business tax returns, year-to-date profit and loss statements, business licenses, and recent bank statements. The lender may also verify how long the business has operated and whether earnings have remained consistent. Keep your records organized, especially when income changes from month to month. Clear documentation can make underwriting more efficient and explain changes in earnings.

Calculate your debt-to-income ratio and monthly obligations

Your debt-to-income ratio, or DTI, compares your recurring monthly debt payments with your gross monthly income. Lenders may include your first mortgage, proposed home equity loan payment, car loans, student loans, credit card minimums, personal loans, and other reported obligations. Certain support payments or housing-related expenses may also affect the calculation.

To estimate your DTI, add your monthly debt payments and divide that total by your gross monthly income. For example, if qualifying debts total $2,500 per month and your gross monthly income is $7,500, your estimated DTI is about 33%. Each lender sets its own limits, but a lower ratio generally leaves more room for a new payment. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains DTI and how lenders use it when reviewing applications.

Confirm your mortgage balance, lien position, and available equity

Home equity is the difference between your property’s current market value and the balances secured by the property. Start with a recent mortgage statement to confirm your unpaid principal balance. If you have a first mortgage, HELOC, or another lien, each balance may affect the amount you can borrow.

The lender will also review lien position. A home equity loan is often a second mortgage behind your existing first mortgage, although refinancing or another loan structure may change the order. Your available equity is not the same as the amount a lender will approve. Lenders generally apply a maximum combined loan-to-value limit, which compares all property-secured debts with the home’s value. Bankrate’s home equity loan guide provides more detail on how this type of borrowing works.

Review your property type, occupancy, title, and ownership

The property itself can affect eligibility. Lenders may have different rules for single-family homes, condominiums, townhomes, multi-unit properties, second homes, and investment properties. A primary residence may qualify under different terms than a rental property because occupancy affects the lender’s risk assessment.

The lender will also confirm who owns the property and whether the title is clear enough to support the new loan. Ownership held in a trust, business entity, or with another person may require additional documents or signatures. Unresolved liens, unpaid property taxes, probate matters, or title disputes can delay or prevent closing. If you own property in New Jersey, ask your mortgage professional which documents may be needed for the title and lien review.

Show reserves and overall financial stability

Savings and other liquid assets can demonstrate that you have funds available for emergencies, repairs, and unexpected income changes. A lender may review checking and savings accounts, retirement assets, investment accounts, and other documented resources. It may also ask about large deposits, particularly when those funds will be used for closing or reserves.

Financial stability includes more than your account balances. Lenders may consider your employment history, income consistency, payment patterns, and recent changes in debt. Avoid moving money between accounts without keeping a clear record, and do not take on significant new debt before closing unless your mortgage professional advises you to do so. Organized records help the lender understand your finances and can reduce requests for clarification.

Home equity loan requirements can differ from one lender to another. One lender may place greater emphasis on credit score, while another may offer more flexibility for self-employed income, a higher DTI, or a particular property type. Rates, maximum loan amounts, appraisal rules, and fee structures can vary as well.

Strengthen your application before applying

If your finances are not quite ready, a few practical steps may improve your application. Pay every bill on time, reduce high credit card balances, avoid opening unnecessary accounts, and check your credit reports for errors. Paying down a smaller debt may also lower your monthly obligations and improve your DTI.

Gather your income, asset, mortgage, property, and insurance documents before requesting a full application review. Gather your government-issued ID and personal information

Begin with a valid government-issued photo ID, such as a driver’s license, passport, or other accepted identification. You will typically also need your Social Security number, date of birth, current address, previous addresses, marital status, and employment details.

Make sure the information on your application matches your documents. Differences in names, addresses, or account information may lead to follow-up questions. If you recently moved, changed your name, or used multiple addresses, gather documents that explain the change.

Lenders generally need to confirm that your income is consistent and likely to continue. Prepare business records as a self-employed borrower

How Do You Get a Home Equity Loan?

A home equity loan lets you borrow against a portion of the equity you have built in your home. Unlike a HELOC, which usually provides a revolving line of credit, a home equity loan typically gives you a lump sum with a fixed interest rate and predictable monthly payments.

Because your property secures the loan, the application involves more than a credit check. The lender will review your income, debts, mortgage balance, property value, title, and ability to repay the new payment. You may also need an appraisal and title search before the loan can close.

The exact process varies by lender, loan amount, property type, and financial profile. However, most New Jersey homeowners can expect the following steps. Gathering your documents early and comparing multiple options can make the process easier to manage. 1. Define your borrowing goal and affordable payment

Start by deciding how much you need and why you need it. Homeowners commonly use home equity funds for renovations, debt consolidation, education expenses, emergency costs, or other major purchases. A specific goal can help you avoid borrowing more than necessary.

Next, determine a monthly payment that fits comfortably within your budget. Review your existing mortgage, property taxes, homeowners insurance, utilities, credit cards, auto loans, and other recurring expenses. Leave room for savings, maintenance, and unexpected costs instead of committing every available dollar to debt payments.

A home equity loan creates an additional payment, so consider the effect on your household budget. If you are funding a renovation, obtain realistic estimates from contractors before choosing a loan amount. 2. Calculate your equity and review your eligibility

Your home equity is the difference between your property’s current market value and the balance remaining on your mortgage. For example, a home worth $450,000 with a $300,000 mortgage balance has approximately $150,000 in equity.

Your available equity is not necessarily the amount you can borrow. Lenders usually limit the combined balance of your existing mortgage and new loan based on the property’s value. They may also review your credit history, income, debt-to-income ratio, property type, and occupancy.

Check your latest mortgage statement for an approximate balance and use a reasonable estimate of your home’s value. Online valuation tools can offer a starting point, but the lender may require an appraisal or another approved valuation method. Once you have a target loan amount and payment range, request quotes or prequalification from multiple lenders. Banks, credit unions, and mortgage brokers can have different programs and approval requirements.

Give each lender the same basic information so you can compare offers fairly. Ask about the interest rate, annual percentage rate, estimated payment, repayment term, closing costs, maximum loan amount, and minimum credit or equity requirements. 4. Choose an offer and submit a complete application

Compare each offer based on its total cost, not just its advertised interest rate. Consider the repayment term, monthly payment, upfront fees, amount of cash you will receive, and whether the loan fits your plans. A slightly lower rate may not save money if it comes with significantly higher fees.

The formal application will request information about your identity, employment, income, debts, property, existing mortgage, and desired loan amount. Answer every question accurately and disclose relevant financial details. Incomplete or inconsistent information can delay the review.

You will typically authorize the lender to verify your credit, income, employment, mortgage balance, property value, and title. Before submitting the application, ask which documents are required and how to provide them securely. A complete application gives the underwriting team a clearer view of your finances.

5. Complete the appraisal and property valuation

The lender must confirm your home’s current value before determining the final loan amount. Depending on the property and lender, this may involve a traditional appraisal, automated valuation, broker price opinion, or another approved method.

An appraiser may review the home’s size, condition, location, improvements, and comparable recent sales. Keep records of significant work, such as a finished basement, renovated kitchen, replacement roof, or added living space. Improvements do not guarantee a higher value, but documentation can help show what has changed.

You may pay the appraisal fee upfront or as part of your closing costs. If the valuation is lower than expected, the lender may reduce the loan amount or change the terms. The Federal Trade Commission’s home equity guidance explains why your property value affects how much you may be able to borrow.

6. Complete the title search and lien review

A title search confirms ownership of the property and identifies liens, judgments, unpaid taxes, or other claims. The lender will also verify your existing mortgage balance and determine the new loan’s position relative to other debts secured by the property.

For many homeowners, a home equity loan is a second lien behind the first mortgage. An additional HELOC, tax lien, judgment, or other claim may affect your eligibility and available equity. Make sure the names on your application match the names shown on the property title and mortgage records.

If the title search finds an issue, you may need to provide documents or resolve the matter before closing. Ask the title company or lender to explain the issue and required next steps. Addressing title concerns early can help prevent closing delays.

7. Submit financial documents for underwriting

Underwriting is the lender’s detailed review of your ability to repay the loan. You may need to provide recent pay stubs, W-2 forms, tax returns, bank statements, proof of assets, and details about your debts. The lender may also request your current mortgage statement, homeowners insurance information, and property tax records.

Self-employed borrowers often need additional documentation, such as personal and business tax returns, year-to-date profit and loss statements, business bank statements, and proof that the business remains active. The requirements depend on the lender and how your income is structured.

Upload clear, complete documents through the lender’s secure portal and keep copies for your records. Avoid sending sensitive financial information through unsecured email unless the lender directs you to use a protected system. Promptly submitting accurate documents can reduce unnecessary delays.

8. Respond to conditions and verification requests

After reviewing your application, the underwriter may issue conditions. These are requests for additional information or clarification before the lender can approve and close the loan. Examples include a missing bank statement page, an explanation of a large deposit, updated pay stubs, or proof that a debt was paid.

Read each request carefully and provide exactly what the lender asks for. If you are unsure about a condition, contact your loan professional rather than guessing or sending unrelated paperwork. Keep written explanations concise, factual, and consistent with the information in your application.

Underwriting questions are a normal part of the process and do not automatically mean your application is in trouble. Delays are more likely when documents are incomplete, outdated, or submitted after a deadline. Keep a record of what you send and follow up when you need confirmation.

9. Review disclosures, fees, rate, term, and payment

Before closing, review the final disclosures and compare them with the offer you selected. Confirm the loan amount, interest rate, annual percentage rate, repayment term, estimated monthly payment, and total closing costs. Check whether the payment estimate includes property taxes, insurance, or other expenses.

Review charges for origination, appraisal, title services, recording, and other lender or third-party services. Ask whether any fees are being added to the loan balance. Financing closing costs can reduce the cash you receive and increase the total amount you repay.

Pay close attention to the repayment schedule and any special terms. The CFPB’s explanation of loan estimates and closing disclosures can help you understand common mortgage documents. Ask questions before signing if any number or term differs from what you expected.

10. Sign the closing documents

At closing, you will sign the documents that create the home equity loan and secure it with your property. Depending on the lender, closing may take place at a title company, attorney’s office, lender location, or through an approved remote process.

Bring a government-issued photo ID and any documents requested by the closing agent. Review the loan agreement, promissory note, security instrument, payment terms, and closing disclosure. Confirm that your name, property address, loan amount, interest rate, and payment information are correct.

Do not sign documents you do not understand. Ask the closing agent or mortgage professional to explain unfamiliar language and request copies of everything you sign. If the final documents contain a significant unexpected change, pause and ask how it affects the loan before proceeding.

11. Confirm whether a rescission period applies

Certain home equity loans secured by a borrower’s primary residence include a three-business-day right of rescission. This right may allow eligible borrowers to cancel the transaction without a penalty after signing. It does not apply to every loan, including many purchase-money mortgages and some transactions involving second homes or investment properties.

The lender should provide a notice explaining whether rescission applies and how to exercise it. Read the notice carefully, including the deadline and required method for submitting a cancellation request. The CFPB’s guidance on the right of rescission provides general information about this consumer protection.

If a rescission period applies, funds may not be available immediately after signing. Ask the lender when the period ends, when the funds will be disbursed, and what happens if you cancel. Consult a qualified attorney if you need advice about your legal rights.

12. Receive your funds and set up payments

After the loan closes and any applicable rescission period ends, the lender will disburse the funds according to the agreement. You may receive the money by check, electronic transfer, or another approved method. Confirm that the amount matches the final documents and contact the lender promptly if it does not.

Set up automatic payments or another reliable payment method before the first due date. Keep your account number, payment address, servicing contact information, and loan documents in a secure location. Review your first statement to confirm the payment amount, due date, principal balance, and interest charges.

Because the loan is secured by your home, make payments on time and contact the servicer early if your circumstances change. If you later sell or refinance, ask how the second mortgage will be paid off and whether any fees apply.

Understand foreclosure risk when your home secures the debt

A home equity loan is secured by your property. If you stop making payments and do not resolve the delinquency with the lender, foreclosure may eventually become a possibility. The lender could seek to recover the unpaid balance through the legal foreclosure process.

This does not mean taking out a home equity loan automatically puts your home in danger. It does mean you should treat the payment as seriously as your primary mortgage payment. Before borrowing, consider whether your budget could handle the new obligation after a job change, medical expense, major repair, or other financial setback.

Account for higher debt, less equity, and payment strain

Borrowing against your home increases your total debt and reduces the portion of the property you own free and clear. That may leave you with less financial flexibility if home values decline, you need to move, or you face an unexpected expense.

Prepare for missed payments, credit damage, and default consequences

A missed payment can result in late fees, collection activity, and negative information on your credit reports. If the delinquency continues, the lender may begin default or foreclosure proceedings. If you expect trouble making a payment, contact the lender as soon as possible. Reaching out early may give you more time to discuss available assistance or payment arrangements. Plan for selling or refinancing with a second mortgage

A second mortgage can also affect a future refinance. The new lender may need the home equity lender to be paid off, approve the refinance, or agree to remain in a specific lien position. These requirements can add time and paperwork to the transaction.

Before borrowing, consider whether you may move or refinance within the loan term. Review your current mortgage balance, estimated property value, second mortgage balance, and expected selling costs. Understand why a loan does not automatically mean losing your home

Taking out a home equity loan does not mean you will automatically lose your property. Most borrowers make their scheduled payments and repay the loan according to the agreed terms. Foreclosure is a potential consequence of serious default, not an automatic result of borrowing.

Consider a HELOC for ongoing or unpredictable expenses

Consider cash-out refinancing when appropriate

Compare the complete cost of the new mortgage with the cost of keeping your current loan and adding a home equity loan. Consider a personal loan to avoid home-secured debt

Unsecured personal loans may carry higher interest rates or shorter repayment terms than home equity products. Compare the annual percentage rate, origination fee, monthly payment, repayment period, and total cost.

This option may fit a smaller expense that you can repay within a shorter period. It may also make sense when protecting your home from additional secured debt is more important than obtaining the lowest possible interest rate. Review both options based on the total cost and your ability to repay.

Use savings, delay the expense, or fund projects in stages
