How to Use a Rate and Term Refinance Calculator

Learn how a rate and term refinance calculator helps you compare loan options, estimate savings, and plan your next mortgage move with confidence.

Many homeowners begin researching refinancing with one simple question: Will a new mortgage actually save me money? A rate and term refinance calculator can help you find a more complete answer. Instead of focusing only on the monthly payment, you can compare total interest, upfront costs, cash required at closing, and the break-even period. You can also see how changing the loan term affects your results. A lower payment may provide more room in your budget, while a shorter term may reduce interest and help you pay off the home sooner. Use the calculator to organize your options, then review the estimates with a trusted mortgage professional.

Key Takeaways

  • Enter current, complete information: Use your latest mortgage statement, property records, income details, debt obligations, and estimated refinance costs for more useful projections.
  • Evaluate the entire loan change: Compare payment amounts, total interest, closing costs, cash needed at closing, repayment term, and break-even timing.
  • Confirm the numbers with a professional: Treat calculator results as estimates, then ask a licensed mortgage broker to review your eligibility, available programs, fees, and final loan terms.

What Is a Rate-and-Term Refinance Calculator?

A rate-and-term refinance calculator is an online tool that estimates how replacing your current mortgage could affect your monthly payment, interest costs, and payoff timeline. It is designed for homeowners who want to change their interest rate, loan term, or both without taking cash out of their home equity.

You can use a calculator before speaking with a lender to organize your goals and compare possible loan scenarios. For example, you may want to lower your principal-and-interest payment, pay off your mortgage sooner, or replace an adjustable-rate mortgage with a fixed-rate loan. Entering different terms can show how each choice may affect your budget and long-term costs.

The calculator usually compares your current mortgage with a proposed refinance. You may need your remaining principal balance, current interest rate, monthly payment, remaining term, estimated property value, and expected closing costs. For the new loan, you may enter the interest rate, loan term, loan amount, and loan type.

The results can help you prepare for a more productive conversation with a mortgage professional. However, they are estimates, not loan approvals or guaranteed offers. Your final terms can depend on your credit, income, debt-to-income ratio, property value, occupancy, loan program, and lender pricing. The Federal Reserve’s mortgage refinancing guide offers additional information about the costs and decisions involved.

Compare rate-and-term and cash-out refinancing

A rate-and-term refinance replaces your existing mortgage with a new loan that has a different interest rate, repayment term, or both. It does not provide extra cash from your home equity. Homeowners may choose this option to reduce their principal-and-interest payment, lower the total interest paid, or pay off the mortgage faster.

A cash-out refinance works differently. The new loan balance is larger than the amount you currently owe, and you receive the difference in cash. Some homeowners use those funds for renovations, debt consolidation, education expenses, or other major costs. Because you borrow more, however, a cash-out refinance can increase your payment and total interest.

When using a calculator, choose a rate-and-term scenario if you only want to change the mortgage’s rate or repayment structure. Select a cash-out scenario when you plan to take equity from the property. Keeping these options separate helps you compare the right loan amounts and costs.

See how the calculator estimates payments and interest

Most refinance calculators begin with details about your current mortgage. These may include the remaining principal balance, interest rate, monthly principal-and-interest payment, and years left on the loan. You then enter information about the proposed mortgage, such as the new loan amount, interest rate, term, and estimated closing costs.

The tool uses those figures to estimate the new principal-and-interest payment and the interest you may pay over the loan’s lifetime. Some calculators also provide an amortization schedule, which shows how each payment is divided between principal and interest as the balance declines. The U.S. Bank mortgage refinance calculator shows the types of information these tools commonly request.

Your results depend on the quality of your inputs. Use your latest mortgage statement for the balance, rate, and payment whenever possible. If you enter an estimated balance or a rate that does not match your financial profile, the comparison may not reflect your actual options.

Review payment, interest, closing costs, and break-even results

A useful calculator should show more than a lower monthly payment. Review the estimated payment under each loan, monthly and annual savings, total interest, loan term, and refinance costs. Closing expenses may include lender fees, title services, appraisal charges, recording fees, discount points, and other transaction costs.

The break-even period estimates how long it may take for your monthly savings to recover the net cost of refinancing. For example, if your closing costs are $6,000 and your estimated monthly savings are $200, the basic break-even point is 30 months. If you expect to move or refinance again before then, the savings may not outweigh the upfront expense.

Refinancing commonly costs about 2% to 5% of the new loan amount, though your actual costs will vary. Fannie Mae’s mortgage refinance calculator can help you review estimated savings and expenses together. Ask whether costs are paid upfront, financed into the new loan, offset by lender credits, or reduced by paying points.

Know what the calculator can and cannot show

A rate-and-term refinance calculator can compare loan scenarios, but it cannot approve your application or guarantee an interest rate. It may also show only principal and interest, rather than your complete housing payment. Property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, and HOA dues may be separate from the calculator’s main estimate.

The tool may rely on general assumptions for closing costs, prepaid expenses, escrow accounts, points, and lender credits. As a result, the amount shown may differ from your actual cash to close. A calculator may also overlook fees that appear later in the official loan estimate.

Use the results as a planning reference, then verify the details with a mortgage professional. Understand why results vary

Two refinance calculators can produce different results because they use different assumptions, fee estimates, tax settings, and payment methods. Your results can also change when you enter a different loan balance, property value, interest rate, loan term, or closing-cost estimate.

A lower monthly payment does not always mean the new mortgage costs less overall. For example, replacing a mortgage with 20 years remaining with a new 30-year loan may lower the payment, but the longer repayment period can increase total interest. A shorter term may have a higher payment while reducing lifetime interest and helping you pay off the home sooner.

Your credit score, debt-to-income ratio, occupancy, property type, loan-to-value ratio, and loan program can affect both eligibility and pricing. Run several realistic scenarios, record the assumptions you use, and compare the results over the number of years you expect to keep the loan. Then discuss those figures with a qualified mortgage professional before making a decision.

Compare upfront and financed closing costs

What Information Do You Need?

A rate-and-term refinance calculator is only as accurate as the information you enter. Before you begin, gather your latest mortgage statement, property tax bill, homeowners insurance details, and a reasonable estimate of your home’s current value. You may also need information about your income, debts, credit profile, and potential refinance costs.

Use the calculator to create a planning estimate, not a final loan quote. A lender will verify your documents, review your complete financial profile, and confirm the rate, loan amount, and terms you qualify for. Gathering these details first makes it easier to compare scenarios and ask focused questions during a refinance review.

Current balance, rate, payment, and remaining term

Start with your existing mortgage details. Find your remaining principal balance, current interest rate, monthly principal and interest payment, and the number of payments left on the loan. Your latest mortgage statement should include most of this information.

Separate principal and interest from property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, and other escrow items. A payment that includes escrow may be much higher than the loan payment used to calculate interest savings. New rate, loan amount, term, and loan type

Next, gather the details of the refinance you are considering. Most calculators ask for the proposed interest rate, new loan amount, repayment term, and loan type. Possible options include conventional, FHA, VA, USDA, fixed-rate, and adjustable-rate mortgages.

With a rate-and-term refinance, the new mortgage generally pays off your existing loan without providing substantial cash back. The new balance may include your remaining principal, eligible closing costs, prepaid expenses, or discount points if you decide to finance them.

Use a realistic rate range instead of relying on the lowest advertised rate. Your actual rate may depend on your credit score, LTV, occupancy, property type, loan size, and other factors. Property value, equity, LTV, and mortgage insurance

Enter your best estimate of the property’s current market value. You can review recent comparable sales, consult a real estate professional, or request an appraisal if one is required. Avoid using only the original purchase price, particularly if the market or your home’s condition has changed.

Your loan-to-value ratio, or LTV, compares your mortgage balance with your property value. For example, a $240,000 mortgage balance on a $300,000 home equals an 80% LTV. Your equity is the difference between the property’s estimated value and the amount you owe.

LTV may affect your interest rate, eligibility, and mortgage insurance requirements. You may not need 20% equity to refinance, since requirements vary by loan program. Review current mortgage insurance guidance from Fannie Mae, then confirm the details with a qualified mortgage professional.

Credit score, income, DTI, occupancy, and property type

Your credit score may affect both your eligibility and the interest rate used in the calculator. Enter your most recent score if you know it, but remember that a lender may use information from a formal credit review rather than the score shown by a free monitoring service.

Gather your gross monthly income and recurring monthly debts as well. Lenders use these figures to calculate your debt-to-income ratio, or DTI. Include auto loans, student loans, credit card minimum payments, personal loans, and other mortgages. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains how DTI works when lenders review mortgage applications.

Also identify how you use the property. A primary residence, second home, and investment property may have different requirements. Property type matters too, whether you own a single-family home, condominium, or multi-unit property. Self-employed borrowers may need additional income documentation.

Taxes, insurance, HOA dues, and escrow

Gather your annual property taxes, homeowners insurance, HOA dues, and current escrow information. You can usually find these figures on your mortgage statement, property tax bill, insurance declarations page, and HOA documents.

These expenses may not change simply because you refinance. If your property taxes and insurance stay the same, a lower principal-and-interest payment does not necessarily reduce your total monthly payment by the same amount. The calculator may show meaningful loan savings while your complete payment changes only slightly.

Keep escrow expenses separate when reviewing your results. Ask whether the new lender requires an escrow account, whether an initial deposit will be due at closing, and how your existing escrow balance will be handled. Prepaid taxes and insurance can affect your cash to close, even though they are not part of your interest savings.

Closing costs, prepaid expenses, points, credits, and financed fees

Gather a realistic estimate of your refinance costs before deciding whether the savings justify the transaction. Common expenses include lender fees, appraisal charges, title services, recording fees, credit report fees, prepaid interest, escrow deposits, and other settlement charges. The Federal Trade Commission’s mortgage guidance outlines several common closing expenses.

Compare discount points, lender credits, and no-cost refinance options separately. Paying points may lower your interest rate, while a lender credit may reduce your upfront costs in exchange for a higher rate. A “no-cost” refinance usually means certain expenses are covered through a credit or added to the loan, not that the transaction has no cost.

Enter each option as its own scenario. Compare the cash required at closing, new monthly payment, total interest, and break-even period. If you finance the closing costs, add them to the new loan amount because they increase both your balance and the interest paid over time.

Start with accurate information about your current loan, then test one change at a time. For example, compare your current 30-year mortgage with a new 30-year loan at a lower rate. Then test 20-year and 15-year options to see how a higher payment could reduce your total interest.

Break-even analysis is another useful feature. To estimate your break-even point, divide your net refinancing costs by your expected monthly savings. For example, $6,000 in costs divided by $250 in monthly savings equals 24 months. This estimate does not account for future refinancing, changes in property value, or the time value of money, but it offers a helpful starting point.

Enter a realistic rate for your financial profile

Start with a same-term comparison

First, compare your current mortgage with a new loan that has the same remaining term or a similar repayment period. This creates a cleaner comparison because it shows what a lower rate could do without changing the repayment schedule significantly.

For example, if you have 24 years remaining, compare your current loan with a new term close to 24 years. You can also compare a new 30-year loan, but recognize that extending the term may lower the monthly payment while increasing the total interest paid.

A same-term comparison helps answer a basic question: Are the savings coming from a better interest rate, or mainly from spreading repayment over more years? Once you understand that result, you can test shorter and longer terms more confidently.

Compare shorter and longer repayment terms

After running a same-term comparison, test different repayment periods. A shorter term, such as 15 or 20 years, may require a higher monthly principal-and-interest payment, but it can reduce total interest and help you pay off the home sooner.

A longer term may provide payment relief if your budget has changed or you want more monthly flexibility. However, restarting a 30-year schedule after making mortgage payments for several years can extend the time it takes to repay the balance.

Compare each option using monthly principal and interest, total interest, remaining balance, and estimated cash to close. Also consider how long you expect to keep the home and whether the payment on a shorter term fits comfortably within your budget.

Test fixed-rate and adjustable-rate scenarios

If you are considering an adjustable-rate mortgage, do not compare only its initial payment with a fixed-rate loan. Review the adjustment period, index, margin, rate caps, and highest possible payment under the loan terms. An initial rate may be lower, but future payments can change.

A fixed-rate refinance provides a more predictable principal-and-interest payment for the life of the loan. That stability may be useful if you plan to stay in the home for many years or prefer consistent housing costs.

Use the calculator to test different possibilities, then review the loan disclosures carefully. A calculator may not model every adjustment feature or future rate path. Ask a mortgage professional to explain how the payment could change and which assumptions were used.

Paying costs upfront requires more cash now, while financing them lowers your cash to close but increases the balance and interest expense.

Do not assume that a “no-cost” refinance has no cost. The lender may cover certain fees by charging a higher interest rate or adding expenses to the loan. Compare the rate, payment, total interest, and break-even point for each structure.

When Does Rate-and-Term Refinancing Fit Your Goals?

A rate-and-term refinance replaces your current mortgage with a new loan that changes the interest rate, repayment term, or both. The new loan typically pays off your existing mortgage without giving you additional cash beyond the amount needed for the payoff and eligible closing costs. That makes it different from a cash-out refinance, which increases the loan balance to provide funds for another purpose.

The right time to refinance depends on more than the rate advertised online. Your credit profile, income, debt-to-income ratio, home value, equity, loan type, occupancy, and expected time in the property all affect the available options. Closing costs also matter because they can reduce the benefit of a lower payment.

Start by using a calculator to compare realistic scenarios. Then discuss the results with a mortgage professional who can review your documents and account for lender requirements. Fannie Mae’s mortgage refinance calculator can help you estimate payment changes, interest savings, and the effect of different loan terms.

Lower your interest rate and monthly payment

Refinancing may fit if you can qualify for a lower rate than the one on your current mortgage. A lower rate can reduce the principal-and-interest portion of your monthly payment. Your total payment may still include property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, and HOA dues, so compare each part separately.

Begin with your current principal-and-interest payment, then compare it with the projected payment on the new loan. Next, account for closing costs such as lender fees, appraisal charges, title services, points, and prepaid expenses. A lower payment may improve your monthly cash flow, but extending the repayment term could increase the total interest paid over time.

Use the calculator to test both monthly savings and lifetime costs. This gives you a clearer view of whether the refinance supports your budget without focusing on the payment alone.

Shorten your term and reduce lifetime interest

A refinance can also help you move from a longer repayment period to a shorter one, such as changing from a 30-year mortgage to a 15-year mortgage. You may build equity faster and pay less interest over the life of the loan. The tradeoff is a higher required monthly payment.

Before choosing a shorter term, compare the payment with your full household budget. Include emergency savings, childcare, insurance, utilities, debt payments, and other financial goals. A lower interest rate does not necessarily make a shorter-term loan comfortable.

Review an amortization schedule to see how each payment is divided between principal and interest. Fannie Mae notes that a 15-year refinance can save substantial interest, but the monthly payment may rise significantly. Ask a mortgage professional to compare the shorter loan with keeping your current mortgage and making additional principal payments.

Replace an adjustable-rate mortgage with a fixed-rate loan

If you have an adjustable-rate mortgage, refinancing into a fixed-rate loan may provide more predictable principal-and-interest payments. An ARM can adjust after its initial fixed period according to the loan terms, index, and applicable caps. A fixed-rate mortgage generally keeps the same principal-and-interest payment throughout the loan term.

That predictability can make household budgeting easier, particularly if you expect to remain in the home for several years. Review your ARM’s next adjustment date, projected payment changes, and remaining term before comparing it with a fixed-rate option.

A fixed-rate loan may start at a higher rate than an ARM’s temporary rate, so compare the expected cost over the period you plan to keep the mortgage. EZ Way Mortgage’s loan programs can help you review fixed-rate and adjustable-rate choices based on your timeline and financial priorities.

Revisit refinancing after credit, income, or equity changes

Your refinance options may change after your financial situation improves. Paying down debt, correcting credit report errors, increasing income, or building more equity can affect your eligibility, pricing, and available loan programs. A change in your home’s value may also improve your loan-to-value ratio.

You do not have to apply immediately after a financial change. Instead, update your information and run a new comparison. The Federal Reserve’s consumer guide to mortgage refinancing recommends weighing expected savings against refinancing costs and considering how long you plan to keep the new loan.

Gather documents that support your current position, including pay stubs, tax returns, bank statements, and details about other debts. Self-employed borrowers may need additional income records. A complete review can show whether refinancing makes sense now or whether waiting may produce a more suitable result.

Check credit, equity, DTI, loan type, and occupancy requirements

Lenders evaluate several factors during a refinance application. These may include your credit score, payment history, debt-to-income ratio, equity, property type, occupancy, and current loan program. Requirements can differ for conventional, FHA, VA, USDA, jumbo, investment property, and second-home loans.

Your debt-to-income ratio compares monthly debt obligations with gross monthly income. A lower ratio may support your application, but lenders review the complete financial picture. Check your credit reports for inaccurate balances, outdated information, or incorrect missed-payment records before applying.

Occupancy also matters. A mortgage for your primary residence may have different terms from one for a rental property. If you are unsure which requirements apply, contact EZ Way Mortgage for a review before relying on an online estimate.

Understand why you may not need 20% equity

The belief that you must have 20% equity to refinance does not apply to every loan or borrower. Eligibility can depend on the loan program, lender guidelines, credit profile, occupancy, mortgage insurance, and other factors. Some homeowners may qualify with less equity, although the loan may include mortgage insurance or different pricing.

Estimate your equity by comparing your mortgage balance with your home’s current value. Online property estimates can help with early planning, but lenders may use an appraisal or another valuation method. The final value can affect your loan-to-value ratio and available options.

Less than 20% equity does not automatically rule out refinancing. It may affect the cost or structure of the new loan, so compare the complete offer instead of relying on one equity threshold. A mortgage broker can review programs that may fit your property and financial profile.

Match the break-even point to your moving plans

The break-even point estimates how long it may take for monthly savings to recover the net cost of refinancing. For example, $6,000 in eligible costs divided by $200 in monthly principal-and-interest savings produces a simple break-even period of 30 months.

Include lender fees, appraisal and title charges, points, credits, and any costs added to the loan. Keep prepaid taxes and insurance separate when reviewing the calculation because they may not represent a true refinancing expense.

Then compare the break-even point with your plans for the property. If you expect to sell or move before reaching that point, the refinance may not provide enough value. If you plan to stay longer, review the projected savings over your expected ownership period. Heritage Family Credit Union’s refinance guidance also emphasizes this timing comparison.

Consider a cash-out refinance, HELOC, or equity loan when you need cash

A rate-and-term refinance may not be the best fit if your primary goal is accessing home equity. A cash-out refinance replaces your current mortgage with a larger loan and provides the difference in cash. Homeowners may use those funds for renovations, education, debt repayment, or other major expenses.

You can also compare a home equity line of credit, known as a HELOC, with a home equity loan. A HELOC usually offers a reusable credit line, while a home equity loan generally provides a lump sum with scheduled payments. Rates, fees, payment structures, and qualification rules vary by product.

Consider how each option affects your budget and whether using your home as collateral is appropriate for the expense. A cash-out refinance changes the rate and terms on your entire first mortgage. A HELOC or equity loan may leave that mortgage in place, but adds another payment. Review these equity financing options with a mortgage professional.

Confirm results with a personalized refinance review

A calculator can provide useful projections, but it cannot confirm your approval, final interest rate, closing costs, or eligibility. The results depend on your inputs and may not include every lender fee, prepaid expense, mortgage insurance charge, or program requirement.

Bring your current mortgage statement, recent income documents, estimated property value, insurance information, and debt details to a refinance review. Ask for principal and interest to be separated from taxes, insurance, escrow, and HOA costs so you can compare loans consistently.

No calculator can show your exact approval terms or final costs, though. Results depend on the information you enter and the assumptions built into each tool. Compare each calculator’s assumptions, inputs, and fee estimates

Different calculators can produce different results because they use different inputs and assumptions. One tool may include property taxes and homeowners insurance in its payment estimate, while another may show only principal and interest. Some calculators use general rate information instead of a rate based on your financial profile.

The Federal Reserve’s guide to mortgage refinancing recommends reviewing the details of both your current mortgage and the proposed replacement loan. When comparing tools, check whether they include lender fees, appraisal costs, title services, recording fees, prepaid interest, points, lender credits, and escrow expenses.

Also confirm whether closing costs are paid upfront or added to the new loan balance. Financing those costs may reduce your cash needed at closing, but it can increase your balance and the interest charged over time.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a rate-and-term refinance calculator estimate?
It estimates how a new interest rate, loan term, or loan structure could affect your payment, total interest, closing costs, and break-even period. The results are for planning purposes and do not represent a guaranteed rate or loan approval.

What information should I enter into the calculator?
Use your latest mortgage statement for the remaining balance, interest rate, monthly principal-and-interest payment, and remaining term. You may also need your estimated home value, proposed loan amount, new rate, loan term, property taxes, insurance, and expected refinance costs.

Does a lower monthly payment always mean refinancing will save money?
Not necessarily. A lower payment may result from extending the repayment period or adding closing costs to the new loan. Compare total interest, cash required at closing, remaining loan balance, and the time needed to recover the refinance costs.

How do I calculate the refinance break-even point?
Divide your net refinance costs by the estimated monthly savings. For example, $6,000 in costs divided by $250 in monthly savings equals a 24-month break-even period. Compare that timeline with how long you expect to keep the home or new mortgage.
