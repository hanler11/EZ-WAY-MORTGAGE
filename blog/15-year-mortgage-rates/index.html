Get the latest on 15 year mortgage rates, how they compare to 30-year loans, and tips for finding the best rate for your home purchase or refinance.

What Are Current 15-Year Mortgage Rates?

A 15-year fixed-rate mortgage gives you a consistent principal-and-interest payment and a defined payoff timeline. Since you repay the loan in half the time of a 30-year mortgage, the interest rate is often lower. The trade-off is a higher monthly payment because you are paying down the balance more quickly.

Published rate averages can help you understand market conditions, but they are not personalized offers. Your actual rate may depend on your credit history, loan program, down payment, property type, loan amount, and lender. New Jersey borrowers should compare complete loan offers, including fees and cash-to-close requirements, rather than relying on a single advertised rate.

Average 15-Year Purchase Rates

Recent national averages for 15-year fixed purchase mortgages are approximately 5.87% to 6.12%. This range offers a useful starting point as you research financing, but it is not a guaranteed rate. Mortgage pricing can change between the time you begin shopping, submit an application, and lock your loan.

A published average may also assume excellent credit, a specific down payment, an owner-occupied property, and a conforming loan amount. Your quote could be higher or lower depending on your financial profile and the home you plan to purchase. Freddie Mac’s Primary Mortgage Market Survey provides national mortgage-rate data and helpful context for borrowers.

For a fair comparison, ask each lender to use the same loan amount, down payment, term, and points structure. This makes it easier to compare the total cost of each offer instead of choosing based only on the lowest advertised percentage.

Average 15-Year Refinance Rates

Average 15-year refinance rates may differ from purchase rates because refinancing involves a different transaction, property valuation, and borrower profile. A recent national average for a 15-year refinance was 6.19%, down from 6.28% the previous week, according to Bankrate’s 15-year mortgage rate data.

A weekly rate change does not automatically make refinancing worthwhile. You also need to consider your current interest rate, remaining loan balance, closing costs, and how long you expect to keep the property. Refinancing may help you lower your rate, shorten your payoff timeline, or change the structure of your payment.

Request a side-by-side comparison of your current mortgage and the proposed refinance. Include the new payment, total interest, closing costs, and estimated break-even point. This gives you a clearer view of whether the refinance supports your financial goals.

How We Source and Update Rate Averages

Mortgage-rate averages come from different sources, and each source may use its own methodology. Freddie Mac’s Primary Mortgage Market Survey uses mortgage-rate information from thousands of loan applications submitted by lenders nationwide through its Loan Product Advisor system. The weekly survey is widely used as a general indicator of mortgage-market conditions.

Rates can change as lenders respond to economic data, bond-market movement, loan demand, and other pricing factors. Averages may also differ from rates shown by banks, online marketplaces, or mortgage brokers because those providers may use different borrower assumptions or sample sizes.

When reviewing a published rate, check the loan term, loan purpose, points, credit assumptions, and update date. Treat the number as a research reference, then request a personalized quote based on your income, assets, credit profile, property, and loan amount.

Mortgage Rates vs. APR

The interest rate is the cost of borrowing the principal balance. It helps determine how much interest appears in each scheduled payment. The annual percentage rate, or APR, includes the interest rate plus certain finance charges, giving you a broader view of the loan’s cost. Bankrate explains the difference between mortgage rates and APR in its rate guidance.

For example, one lender may advertise a lower interest rate that requires several discount points, while another offers a slightly higher rate with fewer upfront fees. The first option may have the lower rate, but the second may require less cash at closing or have a lower overall borrowing cost for your situation.

Use APR as one comparison tool, not the only deciding factor. APR calculations may not include every expense, and the result can vary based on how long you keep the mortgage. Review the rate, APR, points, lender fees, credits, monthly payment, and cash to close together.

Points, Credits, and Mortgage Fees

Discount points are upfront charges that may reduce your mortgage interest rate. One point generally equals 1% of the loan amount, although the rate reduction depends on the lender and market conditions. Paying points may be worthwhile if you have sufficient cash and expect to keep the mortgage long enough to recover the upfront cost.

Lender credits work in the opposite direction. You may accept a slightly higher interest rate in exchange for help with certain closing costs. This can reduce the money you need upfront, which may be helpful if preserving cash is a priority.

Compare the trade-off with a break-even calculation. Divide the cost of the points by the estimated monthly savings to estimate how long it may take to recover the expense. Also review origination, underwriting, appraisal, title, recording, prepaid tax, and insurance charges. Freddie Mac’s rate resources explain how points can affect mortgage pricing.

Why Advertised Rates Differ From Your Quote

An advertised mortgage rate is usually based on an ideal borrower and specific loan assumptions. Your rate may vary according to your credit score, down payment, loan size, property type, occupancy, debt-to-income ratio, and loan program. Pricing for a primary residence may not match pricing for an investment property or second home.

An advertisement may also require discount points, automatic payments, a particular loan amount, or a short lock period. Some promotions highlight the rate without giving equal attention to the APR or lender fees. Read the terms carefully and ask which assumptions apply before using the rate for comparison.

Your personalized quote should show the proposed rate, APR, points, lender charges, estimated payment, and cash to close. Freddie Mac’s mortgage-rate information can help you compare the broader market with the offer you receive from a lender or broker.

Rate Locks, Lock Periods, and Float-Downs

A mortgage rate lock temporarily protects you from an increase while your loan moves toward closing. Lock periods commonly last a set number of days, and the appropriate length may depend on your purchase contract, appraisal, underwriting process, and closing schedule. A longer lock may have different pricing from a shorter one.

Ask whether the lender charges a lock fee, what happens if closing is delayed, and whether a lock extension is available. You should also ask about a float-down option. A float-down may allow you to receive a lower rate if market pricing improves during the lock period, but eligibility rules, timing requirements, and fees vary by lender. Wells Fargo’s mortgage-rate guidance provides an example of questions borrowers may have about rate locks.

A lock does not eliminate every potential change to your loan terms. Your pricing may need to be updated if your financial information, property details, loan amount, or closing timeline changes. Review the lock agreement carefully and tell your lender promptly about anything that could affect the transaction.

How Do 15-Year Rates Compare With 30-Year Loans?

A 15-year mortgage generally offers a lower interest rate than a 30-year mortgage, but the shorter repayment period usually creates a higher monthly payment. The better option depends on your budget, financial priorities, available savings, and how long you expect to keep the loan.

When comparing terms, look beyond the advertised rate. Review the monthly payment, total interest, upfront costs, remaining loan balance, and flexibility each option provides. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau recommends comparing loan offers using the same assumptions, including the loan amount, down payment, and fees.

Typical Rate Differences

Lenders often price 15-year mortgages below 30-year mortgages because the loan balance declines faster and the lender receives repayment sooner. The difference changes with market conditions, loan programs, credit history, property type, and the number of discount points you pay. In many cases, the 15-year rate may be about three-quarters of a percentage point lower.

For example, a Bankrate rate comparison used a 5.47% rate for a 15-year mortgage and a 6.16% rate for a 30-year mortgage. These figures illustrate how rates may compare, but they are not personalized offers. Your quote could differ based on your credit score, debt-to-income ratio, down payment, loan program, occupancy, and whether you are buying or refinancing.

Monthly Principal-and-Interest Payments

A lower interest rate does not usually make a 15-year mortgage payment lower than a 30-year payment. Since you repay the balance in half the time, each payment must cover more principal along with the interest.

Consider a $400,000 loan. At 5.47%, the estimated principal-and-interest payment for a 15-year mortgage is about $3,262 per month. At 6.16%, the estimated payment for a 30-year mortgage is about $2,440, according to Bankrate’s example. Neither figure includes property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, or HOA dues.

Before choosing a term, calculate your complete housing payment. A 15-year payment may fit comfortably for one household but leave another with too little room for savings, repairs, or changing expenses.

Total Interest and Payoff Timeline

The shorter term can substantially reduce the interest paid over the life of the mortgage. The balance falls faster, and the loan ends 15 years earlier, which gives interest less time to accumulate.

Using the same $400,000 example, the estimated total interest is about $187,155 for the 15-year loan, compared with approximately $478,221 for the 30-year loan. The difference will vary according to your rate, loan amount, closing costs, and payment history. You may also sell or refinance before reaching the end of the original term.

That timing matters. If you expect to move within a few years, compare the projected balance and total payments during the period you expect to own the home. A lower lifetime interest cost may not be the only factor that matters if you will not keep the mortgage long term.

Build Equity Faster With a 15-Year Loan

Home equity is the difference between your home’s value and the amount you still owe on the mortgage. With a 15-year loan, you make larger principal payments from the start, so the balance usually declines more quickly than it would with a 30-year loan. Rate.com explains that shorter-term loans typically help borrowers build equity faster.

Faster principal reduction can help you reach an equity target sooner. It may also support future financing options, such as a home equity loan or HELOC, if you meet the lender’s requirements. However, home prices can rise or fall. Paying down your mortgage increases your ownership stake, but it does not guarantee that your total equity will increase if the property’s market value declines.

Make Extra Principal Payments on a 30-Year Loan

A 30-year mortgage gives you a lower required payment while allowing you to pay extra toward principal when your budget permits. You could add a set amount each month, make an additional payment each year, or apply a bonus or tax refund to the balance.

This approach can reduce interest and shorten the payoff timeline, but it depends on consistent follow-through. The lower required payment also gives you the option to scale back during a period of reduced income or higher expenses. American Financing notes that extra payments can help repay a mortgage sooner, but borrowers should check the terms of their specific loan.

Before making extra payments, confirm that your lender applies them to principal and ask whether any prepayment restrictions apply. You may also want to compare paying down the mortgage with building emergency savings or addressing higher-interest debt.

Compare Cash Flow, Flexibility, and Opportunity Cost

A 30-year mortgage keeps more money available each month. You could direct the difference toward an emergency fund, retirement savings, home improvements, education costs, or other financial goals. That flexibility can be valuable for households with variable income, growing expenses, or limited cash reserves.

A 15-year mortgage puts more of your monthly income toward the home and reduces the balance faster. For borrowers who want to repay their mortgage quickly, that may be worth the higher payment. The tradeoff is that less cash remains available for other priorities.

Think about what you would do with the payment difference, and whether you would follow through consistently. The Money Advantage discusses how a longer mortgage can preserve cash flow for other uses. Before choosing the shorter term, make sure the payment still leaves room for savings, maintenance, and unexpected costs.

What Affects 15-Year Mortgage Rates?

A 15-year mortgage rate depends on two broad factors: market conditions and your financial profile. Economic trends influence the rates lenders offer across the market, while your credit, income, down payment, loan program, and property help determine the rate you may receive.

That is why two borrowers applying for the same loan amount can receive different offers. A borrower with strong credit, stable income, substantial reserves, and a lower loan-to-value ratio may qualify for more favorable pricing than someone with higher credit risk or a more complicated application.

Rates can also vary from one lender to another. Each lender sets pricing based on funding costs, risk, operating expenses, and lending goals. When comparing offers, look beyond the interest rate. Review the annual percentage rate (APR), discount points, lender fees, estimated cash to close, and monthly payment.

Inflation, Federal Reserve Policy, and Treasury Yields

Inflation affects mortgage rates because it influences borrowing costs throughout the economy. When inflation remains high, investors may expect interest rates to stay elevated. Lenders can then adjust mortgage pricing to account for those conditions. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains how changing mortgage rates affect borrowers, including the role of broader monetary policy.

The Federal Reserve does not directly set 15-year mortgage rates. However, its decisions can influence market expectations, short-term borrowing costs, and investor demand. Mortgage rates also respond to mortgage-backed securities and longer-term Treasury yields. When Treasury yields rise, mortgage rates often move higher, although the two rates do not always change by the same amount.

Employment, Economic Growth, and Mortgage Conditions

Employment and economic growth can shape expectations about future interest rates. Strong job growth and expanding economic activity may increase concerns about inflation, which can place upward pressure on mortgage rates. A weaker economy may lead investors to expect lower rates, but other market risks can still affect mortgage pricing.

Mortgage market conditions matter as well. Lenders consider loan demand, available capital, housing activity, and the level of risk in the market. Economic forecasts may also account for demographics, housing demand, and long-term interest rate trends. The Texas Real Estate Research Center explains where mortgage rates come from, including the connection between economic models and long-term rates.

Credit Score, Payment History, and Credit Utilization

Your credit profile helps lenders estimate how reliably you have managed debt. A higher credit score may help you qualify for better mortgage pricing, while late payments, collections, and high revolving balances can make borrowing more expensive or more difficult.

Credit utilization measures how much of your available revolving credit you are using. High utilization can suggest financial strain, even when all payments are made on time. Paying down credit card balances before applying may strengthen your profile, but avoid closing accounts or making major changes without first speaking with your loan professional.

The effect of credit can be significant over a long repayment period. The CFPB’s mortgage rate guidance shows how credit scores can influence borrowing costs and total interest. Review your credit reports early, then allow time to correct errors or reduce outstanding balances.

Debt-to-Income Ratio, Income Stability, and Cash Reserves

Your debt-to-income ratio, or DTI, compares your recurring monthly debt payments with your gross monthly income. Lenders use this figure to assess whether the proposed mortgage payment fits alongside your current obligations. A lower DTI may make your application stronger, although acceptable limits vary by loan program and borrower profile.

Income stability also affects underwriting. Salaried employment with a consistent history is often straightforward to document. Self-employed income, commissions, bonuses, seasonal work, and multiple income sources may require tax returns, bank statements, profit-and-loss statements, or other records.

Cash reserves can support your application by showing that you have funds available after closing. Reserves may be especially important for borrowers with variable income, investment properties, or larger loan balances. Keeping money available for unexpected repairs and emergencies can also help you avoid depending on credit after buying a home.

Down Payment, Loan-to-Value, and Loan Amount

Your down payment affects your loan-to-value ratio, or LTV. This ratio compares your mortgage balance with the property’s value. A larger down payment generally creates a lower LTV, which may reduce lender risk. Depending on the loan program, it may also reduce mortgage insurance costs or support more favorable pricing.

A smaller down payment can still make homeownership possible through programs with flexible requirements. However, compare the complete monthly payment rather than focusing only on the interest rate. Mortgage insurance, taxes, homeowners insurance, and other costs can change the amount you pay each month.

The loan amount can affect pricing, too. A balance above the applicable conforming loan limit may require a jumbo mortgage with different qualification standards. A 15-year mortgage often has a lower rate than a 30-year loan, but the shorter term creates a higher required payment. Review both figures before choosing a loan structure.

Property Type, Occupancy, and Location

The property itself can affect your mortgage rate and approval requirements. A single-family primary residence may receive different pricing from a condominium, multifamily property, vacation home, or investment property. Properties with multiple units or non-owner occupancy may present additional risk to the lender.

Occupancy is another important factor. A home you plan to live in generally qualifies for different terms than a property purchased solely for rental income. Be accurate about how you will use the property, since providing incorrect occupancy information can create serious underwriting and closing problems.

Location can influence eligibility and pricing as well. Lenders may review local property values, marketability, insurance conditions, and comparable sales. Requirements can differ between communities in Middlesex County and nearby New Jersey markets. Loan Program and Jumbo Pricing

The loan program you select can change both your interest rate and the requirements attached to the loan. Conventional, FHA, VA, and USDA mortgages each have different guidelines for credit, down payment, property type, mortgage insurance, and eligibility. A program with a lower advertised rate may not have the lowest total cost after fees and insurance are included.

Jumbo loans cover balances above applicable conforming loan limits. Since these loans are larger, lenders may require stronger credit, more cash reserves, a lower DTI, or a larger down payment. Pricing can also vary based on the property, occupancy, loan size, and overall borrower profile.

Some borrowers may benefit from a specialized jumbo or portfolio loan. The right option depends on your income, assets, goals, and property. Purchase and Refinance Pricing

Purchase and refinance loans may receive different pricing, even when the borrower and loan amount are similar. With a purchase, the lender reviews the sales contract, down payment, property value, and intended occupancy. A refinance may involve an appraisal, payoff of the existing mortgage, and closing costs that you need to recover over time.

A rate-and-term refinance changes the interest rate, repayment term, or both. A cash-out refinance replaces your current mortgage with a larger loan and provides access to part of your home equity. Since the balance and risk may increase, cash-out pricing can differ from standard refinance pricing.

The value of a 15-year refinance depends on more than the new rate. Compare your current payment, remaining term, closing costs, and how long you expect to keep the home. Lender Pricing, Points, and Rate-Lock Timing

Lenders may price the same 15-year mortgage differently. Their offers can reflect funding costs, profit margins, underwriting standards, loan volume, and lender fees. Comparing APR can provide a broader view of borrowing costs because it includes certain loan charges, although it does not capture every expense or account for how long you will keep the mortgage.

Discount points allow you to pay more upfront in exchange for a lower interest rate. Lender credits work in the opposite direction. You accept a higher rate in exchange for assistance with eligible closing costs. The better choice depends on your available cash, monthly budget, and expected time in the home.

A rate lock holds your quoted rate for a specific period while your loan moves toward closing. Lock periods may last 15, 30, 45, or 60 days, with pricing that can vary by length. How Much Does a 15-Year Mortgage Cost?

A 15-year mortgage usually comes with a lower interest rate than a 30-year loan, but the shorter repayment period creates a higher monthly principal-and-interest payment. In return, you repay the balance in half the time and typically pay much less interest over the life of the loan. Your total cost depends on more than the rate advertised by a lender.

When estimating the cost of a 15-year mortgage, include the loan payment, property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, HOA dues, maintenance, utilities, and closing costs. Your down payment, credit profile, loan program, property location, and loan amount will also affect the numbers. A clear estimate gives you a better basis for comparing homes and loan offers.

Calculate Principal and Interest

Principal is the amount you borrow, while interest is the lender’s charge for providing the funds. These two amounts form the core of your mortgage payment. With a 15-year loan, each payment generally applies more money toward principal because you are repaying the balance over a shorter period.

For example, a $300,000 mortgage at 6% would require a higher monthly principal-and-interest payment over 15 years than over 30 years. However, the 15-year borrower would pay off the loan sooner and generally spend far less on interest overall. A 15-year mortgage calculator can help you compare loan amounts, interest rates, and repayment terms.

Your actual rate may vary based on your credit score, debt-to-income ratio, down payment, loan program, property type, and lender pricing. Before choosing an offer, ask for the monthly principal-and-interest payment and estimated total interest.

Add Taxes, Insurance, and Escrow

Your total mortgage payment may include property taxes and homeowners insurance in addition to principal and interest. When these costs are placed into escrow, you make monthly payments to the lender, which then pays the tax and insurance bills when they come due.

Property taxes vary by municipality, and insurance premiums depend on the home, coverage, location, and claims history. New Jersey buyers should account for local tax differences when comparing homes in Middlesex County and nearby communities. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s home loan guidance explains why these expenses belong in your housing estimate.

Escrow payments can change even when your mortgage rate stays fixed. If taxes or insurance premiums increase, your monthly payment may rise. Request a detailed payment breakdown and ask which costs are fixed, estimated, or subject to change.

Include Mortgage Insurance and Program Costs

A conventional loan with less than 20% down may require private mortgage insurance, or PMI. PMI protects the lender, not the borrower, and adds to your monthly housing cost. Its price may depend on your down payment, loan-to-value ratio, credit profile, and the insurer’s guidelines.

Other loan programs have their own costs. FHA loans include mortgage insurance premiums, while USDA and VA loans may include program-specific fees or funding charges, subject to eligibility and applicable exemptions. These expenses can affect both your monthly payment and the amount you need at closing.

Ask whether mortgage insurance can be removed later and what requirements apply. You can compare conventional, FHA, VA, USDA, and jumbo options through EZ Way Mortgage’s loan programs. Account for HOA Dues, Maintenance, and Utilities

Some housing expenses do not appear in your mortgage payment. If you buy a condo, townhouse, or property in a planned community, you may owe monthly, quarterly, or annual HOA dues. These fees may cover shared landscaping, amenities, exterior maintenance, or other services, and they can vary significantly between communities.

You should also budget for repairs, routine maintenance, utilities, and seasonal costs. A larger home may require more heating, cooling, and upkeep. Older properties may need more frequent repairs, while a condo may have fewer exterior responsibilities but higher association fees.

Before making an offer, request the HOA fee schedule and ask about planned special assessments. You can also ask the seller for recent utility information. Estimate Closing Costs and Cash to Close

Closing costs are separate from your down payment. They may include lender fees, appraisal charges, title services, recording fees, prepaid interest, homeowners insurance, and initial escrow deposits. These costs often range from about 2% to 5% of the loan amount, although the actual total depends on the property, lender, transaction, and loan program.

Your cash to close may include the down payment, closing costs, prepaid expenses, escrow deposits, and adjustments for deposits or credits already paid. On a $300,000 loan, closing costs in that range could equal approximately $6,000 to $15,000, before the down payment. Treat this as a planning estimate, not a quote.

Test the Payment Against Your Budget

A 15-year mortgage can reduce long-term interest, but the monthly payment must fit comfortably within your budget. Start with take-home income, then account for recurring debts, living expenses, childcare, transportation, savings, and other financial commitments. Include a reserve for repairs and unexpected bills.

Households with variable income, limited reserves, or significant financial goals may need more room in their monthly budget. What Are the Pros and Cons of a 15-Year Mortgage?

A 15-year mortgage can help you pay off your home sooner and reduce the total interest you pay over the life of the loan. Because the repayment period is shorter, lenders often offer a lower interest rate than they would for a comparable 30-year mortgage. You also build equity faster, which may give you more financial flexibility if you plan to stay in the home for many years.

The main tradeoff is a higher monthly payment. Although a 15-year loan can cost less overall, it may place more pressure on your monthly budget. Before choosing this term, compare the payment with your income, debts, emergency savings, retirement contributions, and other financial priorities. Reviewing different mortgage programs with a licensed professional can help you decide whether the potential savings justify the higher payment.

Pay Less Interest With a Lower Rate

One of the main advantages of a 15-year mortgage is the potential for a lower interest rate. Lenders may price shorter-term loans more favorably because the balance is repaid sooner. Build Equity and Pay Off Your Mortgage Faster

This helps you build equity faster than you would with a 30-year mortgage for the same loan amount and interest rate.

You also pay off the loan in half the time. Finishing the mortgage sooner may support goals such as entering retirement without a housing payment, selling the home with more equity, or reducing long-term expenses. Balance Flexibility With Other Financial Goals

You may have less room to save for college, replace a vehicle, start a business, or contribute to retirement accounts. Compare the interest savings with the value of keeping more cash available. The best choice depends on your goals, risk tolerance, expected time in the home, and ability to handle the required payment.
