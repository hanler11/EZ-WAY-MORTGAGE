Getting a Mortgage With Child Support

A mortgage with child support can count support you receive as income, or support you pay as a debt. See what lenders ask to document.

A mortgage with child support is not a special loan product. It is a regular home loan where the file also includes support you receive, support you pay, or both. Lenders can treat documented support you receive as income. They can treat a court-ordered payment you must make as a monthly debt. The outcome depends on the court order, the payment history, and whether you ask the lender to count the income at all.

This guide explains how underwriters look at those two sides of the same topic. It is educational, not a promise that any file will be approved. Partner lenders set their own rules, and those rules can differ from the common Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac standards described here.

What a mortgage with child support actually means

People search this phrase for two different reasons. Some receive support and want it counted so they can qualify for a home. Others pay support and want to know whether that payment will shrink the loan they can consider. A complete file answers both questions, even if only one applies to you.

You do not have to reveal child support, alimony, or separate maintenance that you receive if you do not want that money considered. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says a lender or broker may ask whether income on the application comes from those payments. The lender must also tell you that you do not have to disclose it if you do not want it counted. That rule is about income you receive, not about a payment you are ordered to make.

If you want the income counted, say so on the application. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide, section B3-3.4-02, allows alimony, child support, equalization payments, or separate maintenance as income only when the borrower discloses it and asks that it be considered. Leaving it off, then hoping the underwriter will find deposits and add them later, is not the same thing.

Paying support is different. A written agreement can require the payment. That agreement may be a divorce decree, a separation agreement, or another legal document. If the payments must continue for more than ten months, Fannie Mae section B3-6-05 treats the payment as recurring monthly debt. Voluntary payments do not have to be counted. A copy of the order still belongs in the file when the obligation exists.

Income you receive versus a payment you must make

Think of support as two columns. The left column is money that comes in. The right column is money that must go out. A borrower can have one, the other, or both. Mixing them up is the most common reason a file stalls.

Question. Support you receive. Support you must pay.
How it can affect the file. It may be added as qualifying income if you ask and the documents support it. It may be counted as a monthly debt when the order requires it for more than ten months.
Do you have to disclose it? No, if you do not want it considered as income. Yes, when a written order or agreement requires the payment.
Core document. Use a divorce decree, separation agreement, court order, or other written legal agreement. Use the same kind of order. It should show the amount and how long it lasts.
Recent history. Show full, regular, on-time payments for at least six months. Provide the order itself. Add any history the lender asks for if someone else pays the debt.
How long it must last. It is expected to continue at least three years from the application date. It is counted when more than ten months of payments remain.
What usually fails. Cash handoffs, partial payments, or an order that ends soon. A voluntary gift that is not in an order does not have to be counted.

The six-month receipt rule and the three-year continuance rule come from Fannie Mae section B3-3.4-02 and from Freddie Mac Seller/Servicer Guide section 5305.1, effective May 7, 2025. The ten-month debt rule is in Fannie Mae section B3-6-05. A government, portfolio, or other partner program can use a different test. Ask before you assume one guide covers every option.

Alimony follows the same documentation idea, but one detail differs when you pay it. Fannie Mae lets the lender subtract alimony, equalization, or separate maintenance from qualifying income instead of listing it as a debt. Child support you pay is listed as a debt. It does not get that income-reduction option. This article is not a lesson in how a debt-to-income ratio is calculated. That is a separate topic. The point here is only which column the support belongs in.

Documents that show the order and the amount

Underwriters want the paper that created the payment, not a text thread about what someone promised. Fannie Mae accepts one of these for income:

  • A divorce decree, or a separation agreement if the divorce is not final.
  • Any other written legal agreement or court decree that describes the payment terms.
  • Documentation of a state law that requires the payment, when that is what creates the obligation.

If you are separated and there is no agreement that names the support, Fannie Mae says proposed or voluntary payments should not be treated as income. A plan to start payments after closing is not income today.

Freddie Mac asks for documentation of the payor's obligation for the prior six months, including the amount and the duration. Acceptable records include a final divorce decree, a signed court order, a legally binding separation agreement, a legally binding child support agreement, or other legally binding documentation.

Read the order for three facts before you send it:

  1. The amount of each payment, and whether it is weekly, biweekly, or monthly.
  2. Who must pay, and who must receive it.
  3. When the duty ends, including a child's age or a stated end date.

Bring the full order, not a single page. Riders, amended orders, and wage-garnishment notices can change the amount. If a New Jersey court later modified the order, include the newest order and say which one controls. Probation or the New Jersey Child Support Program may also have a payment ledger. That ledger can help show receipt, but it does not replace the order that sets the terms.

Keep names consistent. The recipient on the order should match the name on the loan application. If you changed your name, add the legal document that connects the two names. Do not ask a friend to "explain" the order in a letter unless the lender asks for one. A letter cannot create a payment the order does not require.

How lenders check that payments actually arrive

An order shows what should be paid. Bank records show what was paid. Fannie Mae requires proof of receipt for the most recent six months. Acceptable proof includes bank statements, canceled checks, or other electronic evidence of the deposits. The history must show full, regular, and timely payments. A lump-sum equalization payment is not treated as steady income.

Freddie Mac is just as direct. Payments in the full amount must have been received consistently for the most recent six months. The income is not used if payments are inconsistent, if they are less than the full amount, or if the payor has been obligated for fewer than six months. Proof can be deposits or cashed payments into your account, transfers into a money-transfer account you own, or a statement from a government agency that names you as the recipient and lists the amounts paid.

Build a simple trail:

  • Highlight each deposit that matches the order.
  • Note the date and whether the amount is full.
  • Explain a missed or short month with a document, not a story. A wage-garnishment change or a corrected order is useful. "He paid me cash sometimes" is not.
  • If payments arrive through a state disbursement unit, include that statement beside the bank deposits so the amounts match.

Partial payments are a problem even when some money arrives every month. Both guides look for the full amount. If the order says one figure and the deposits show a smaller figure, the lender may refuse the income or may use only an amount the history supports. Do not add the missing cash back in yourself.

Cash payments are the hardest to prove. If the order allows them, ask the payer to switch to a traceable method before you apply, then wait until you have a clean history. Starting that history after you are already in contract is often too late for this income to help.

How long the income has to continue

Past deposits are only half the test. The lender also asks whether the income should keep coming. Fannie Mae requires documentation that the income is expected to continue for at least three years from the application date. The guide tells lenders to check limits such as the age of the children or the period alimony must be paid. Freddie Mac requires proof that the payor is obligated to pay for at least the next three years.

The CFPB makes the same point in plain language. If you want child support or alimony considered, you likely need a court order and proof the payments were received. If the payments are inconsistent, or if they are expected to end within three years, you might not be able to use that income to qualify.

Read the end date against your application date, not against the day the order was signed. A duty that ends in two years may be real money in your budget and still fail the continuance test. A duty that runs until a child turns eighteen may qualify today and fail next year. Bring the birth dates the order relies on so the underwriter can do that math from the document, not from memory.

When the income does qualify, Fannie Mae says the documented monthly amount may be used. Documented child support is nontaxable, and the guide allows it to be grossed up under the general income rules in section B3-3.1-01. Gross-up means the lender may adjust nontaxable income so it can be compared with taxable wages. It is not a larger check, and it is not a payment quote. Freddie Mac says the monthly income amount is the documented fixed payment. Some files validated through Fannie Mae's Desktop Underwriter validation service can follow a different documentation message. If that message appears, follow the message.

None of this is a promise that support income will be counted on your file. Program rules, the age of the children, and the partner lender's overlays all matter.

When the support you pay counts as a debt

If you are the payer, the question flips. Fannie Mae section B3-6-05 says a required alimony, child support, equalization, or separate maintenance payment is a recurring monthly debt when it must continue for more than ten months. The loan file needs the divorce decree, separation agreement, court order, or equivalent document that confirms the amount.

A few limits keep the rule from sweeping in every family payment:

  • Voluntary payments do not have to be counted. A transfer you make without an order is not the same as a court-ordered duty.
  • If ten months or fewer remain, the Fannie Mae rule above does not force the payment into the debt tally. Count the remaining payments from the application, using the order's schedule.
  • For alimony, equalization, and separate maintenance that you pay, the lender may reduce qualifying income by that amount instead of listing a debt. Child support you pay stays a debt. Desktop Underwriter has a specific way to enter a negative income amount when the income-reduction option is used. That entry is the lender's job.

Sometimes another person actually makes a payment that is still in your name. Fannie Mae allows some non-mortgage debts, including child support, to be excluded in that case. You must be obligated, but you must not be the person repaying it. The other party also cannot be an interested party in the home sale. The lender needs the most recent twelve months of canceled checks or bank statements from the person who pays. Those records must show no delinquent payments. A promise that your former partner usually handles it is not that record.

Do not hide a known order because the payment feels small. Incomplete disclosure is worse than a debt the underwriter can measure. If the order was modified, send the modification. If payments are wage-garnished, the pay stub may already show the deduction, and the underwriter will ask where it comes from.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get a mortgage with child support as my only income?

Maybe, but only if the support meets the documentation, history, and continuance tests and the rest of the file works. Many borrowers use support along with wages or other income. One income type does not guarantee a loan.

Do I have to tell the lender about support I receive?

No. You do not have to reveal child support, alimony, or separate maintenance if you do not want it considered as income. If you do want it counted, you must disclose it and ask that it be considered.

How many months of payments do lenders want to see?

Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac both look for full, regular payments over the most recent six months when support is used as income. A shorter history, or a history of partial payments, can disqualify that income.

What if my child support order ends in two years?

Income that is not expected to continue for at least three years from the application date generally cannot be used under those guides. The payment can still help your household budget. It just may not help you qualify.

Does support I pay always reduce what I can borrow?

Not always. A required payment with more than ten months left is commonly counted as a monthly debt. Voluntary payments do not have to be counted. Alimony you pay may be subtracted from income instead of listed as a debt. The order controls the facts.
