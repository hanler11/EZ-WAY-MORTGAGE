Mortgage Rate Lock Timing

Mortgage rate lock timing for New Jersey buyers: how long a lock lasts, what can still change it, and what to do if closing slips.

Mortgage rate lock timing is the decision about when to freeze the rate on a home loan, and for how many days that freeze should last. Rates can move daily, and sometimes within the same day. A lock holds the rate you were offered until closing, but only if you close inside the lock window and your file stays the same. What mortgage rate lock timing actually decides

A rate lock, sometimes called a lock-in, is a lender's promise that the interest rate on your offer will not change between that offer and closing. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says the promise holds only if you close inside the stated time and nothing material changes on your application. If you are still floating, the rate can change at any time until you lock.

Timing is not the same question as "what is a lock." Timing asks three practical things. When should you ask to lock? How many days should the lock cover? What do you do if the closing date moves? Those answers live in the lender's lock rules, not in a single national calendar.

You want a written window that matches the contract date, the appraisal, and the title work, not a window that runs out while those pieces are still moving.

It does not tell you what an extension would cost, or whether a shorter or longer window was available. Ask those questions before you accept the lock. The Loan Estimate is the snapshot. The lock agreement is the clock.

When borrowers usually lock

Most purchase borrowers lock after they have a signed contract and a lender that has issued a Loan Estimate, and before the rate is still floating into the week of closing. There is no federal rule that says you must lock on a certain day. Some lenders will lock when they issue the Loan Estimate. Others will not lock until you ask. Confirm which one you have.

A useful lock starts when three things are true. You know the property and the loan amount. You know a realistic closing date. And the documents the lender still needs are ones you can deliver inside that date. Locking earlier can protect you if rates rise while you shop inspections and insurance. Locking later can leave you exposed if the file is not ready and the window is short.

Refinance timing is different from a purchase. There is no seller and no contract deadline, so the clock is mostly underwriting and title. Do not lock a refinance on a date you cannot document income, assets, or the payoff. A lock that expires because a pay stub or a bank statement is still missing is a timing problem, not a market problem.

New Jersey purchase contracts often name a mortgage contingency date and a closing date. Those two dates should drive the lock, not the other way around. If the contract gives the lender a short window to clear conditions, a short lock can match. If the condo, the well, or the title search usually runs long in that town, say so before you pick the lock length.

How long a lock window usually lasts

The CFPB notes that rate locks are typically available for 30, 45, or 60 days, and sometimes longer. Those are common choices, not a promise from any one lender, and not a quote from EZ Way. Partner lenders set their own windows, and a longer window is not automatically the better one. Ask what is available for your file and what changes if you pick a different length.

Count the window in calendar days from the lock date through the day you expect to sign, and then add a small cushion for the last conditions. Do not count only business days unless the lock agreement says it does. Weekends and holidays still use lock days on many programs.

Match the window to the slowest open item, not to the fastest hope.

  • A standard resale with a clear title and a scheduled appraisal often fits a shorter window.
  • A condo that still needs project review, a rural property, or a file with self-employed income usually needs more days.
  • A new build or a sale that depends on the buyer of your current home can outlast a standard window. Ask about a longer lock before you are inside the last week.

If you already worry the period is too short, ask about switching to a longer lock now. Extending later is a different request, and the CFPB warns that an extension can be expensive. Get the length right while you still have a choice.

Build the lock around the closing calendar

Write the dates down before you lock. You do not need a perfect forecast. You need the dates already on paper.

  1. Contract or application date, and the mortgage contingency deadline if you have one.
  2. The day the appraisal was ordered, and the day it is due back.
  3. The day you will deliver income, asset, and insurance documents.
  4. The target clear-to-close date, then the scheduled signing date.
  5. A cushion of several days past signing, in case the wire, the final title update, or a last condition slips.

Put the lock expiration on that same list. If expiration lands before clear-to-close, the window is too short. If it lands many weeks after a simple file, ask whether a shorter window was offered and what the difference is. What can still change a locked rate

A lock is not a promise that nothing on the loan can move. The CFPB lists common reasons a locked rate can still change. You switch the loan type. You change the down payment or the loan amount. The appraisal comes in higher or lower than expected. Your credit score changes because you opened a new account or missed a payment. The lender cannot document overtime, bonus, or other income you used to qualify.

Those are application changes, not market changes. The lock protects you from the market inside the window. It does not protect you from a new version of your own file. If you are about to buy a car, open a card, or move a large deposit, tell your broker before you do it. A surprise in the last week is one of the fastest ways to lose the rate you thought you had frozen.

Read the lock confirmation, not only the Loan Estimate checkbox. You want the rate, the lock date, the expiration date, and the property address in writing. Ask what happens if you lock and rates later fall. Some lenders offer a float-down. Many do not. The CFPB notes that a lock can keep you from a lower rate if rates drop after the offer. That tradeoff is part of the timing choice. You are buying certainty for a set number of days, not a right to the best rate on the day you sign.

If the closing slips past the lock

Call before the expiration date, not after. Ask what a delay does, whether an extension is available, and what must be true for the original lock to survive. Policies vary by lender. There is no single extension rule we can print here, and we will not quote a fee. The person who holds the lock is the one who can say whether extra days are even offered.

A late appraisal, a condo questionnaire, or a title issue can push a New Jersey closing past the lock. So can a repair the seller still owes, or one more document from a self-employed borrower. None of those is fixed by watching rates. They are fixed by clearing the item or by resetting the lock with the lender while you still have time.

If you are thinking about leaving the lender because the lock is about to die, slow down. Switching lenders before closing starts a new application, a new Loan Estimate, and often a new appraisal clock. That can cost the days you were trying to save. Talk through the open conditions first. Our team tracks those conditions so you can see whether the file can still close inside the window you already have.

A lock that expires does not mean you cannot close. It means the old promise is over. The next rate is whatever the lender will offer on the new day, and it can be higher or lower. That is why the cushion belongs in the original window.

Questions to ask before you lock

The CFPB suggests a short list. Use it. The answers belong in your notes, next to the expiration date.

  • What does it mean if I lock today?
  • What lock time frame does this Loan Estimate provide?
  • Is a shorter or longer lock available, and what is different about it?
  • What if closing is delayed and the lock expires?
  • If I lock, can the rate still change, and for which reasons?
  • If I lock and rates go down, what happens?

Add two local questions. Does this window cover the appraisal and the title search for this property? Who tells me when we are ten days from expiration? If nobody owns that warning, put it on your own calendar the day you lock.

Bring the contract, the target signing date, and any item you already know will be slow. A brokerage conversation is more useful with those facts than with a guess about the market. We compare options across partner lenders and explain the step in plain English before you choose a window.

Frequently asked questions

When should I lock my mortgage rate?

Lock when you have a property, a realistic closing date, and a lender offer you understand. Many borrowers lock after the contract and the Loan Estimate, with a window that reaches signing plus a few extra days. There is no required day. Ask whether the lender locks only when you request it.

How long does a mortgage rate lock last?

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says locks are typically available for 30, 45, or 60 days, and sometimes longer. Your lender's menu controls the choices. Pick a length that covers appraisal, title, and the scheduled signing, not a length that merely sounds standard.

Can a locked rate still change?

Yes, if the application changes. A different loan type, a different down payment, an appraisal that misses the price, a credit change, or income the lender cannot document can all move the rate. The lock is aimed at market movement inside the window, not at a new version of your file.

What if my New Jersey closing is delayed?

Contact the lender before the lock expires and ask whether an extension exists. Do not assume the original rate survives the expiration date. Clear the open condition if you can. Starting over with a new lender often adds a new clock.

Does the Loan Estimate show my lock?

Page 1 of the Loan Estimate shows whether the rate is locked and for how long. It does not show the cost of an extension or the price of a different window. Ask for those details, and keep the written lock confirmation with the expiration date.
