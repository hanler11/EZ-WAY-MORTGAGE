Refinance to Get Rid of PMI: Costs, Savings & Timing

Find out how to refinance to get rid of PMI, compare costs and savings, and learn when refinancing makes sense for your mortgage and financial goals.

Building equity may give you a chance to lower your housing costs, but refinancing isn’t the only way to remove mortgage insurance. If you have a conventional loan, your servicer may let you request PMI cancellation once you meet the requirements. FHA MIP follows different rules, so the right choice depends on your current mortgage and your home’s value. If you’re considering whether to refinance to get rid of pmi, start by checking your loan balance, asking your servicer about cancellation, and getting a written refinance estimate. Comparing these details can help you see whether a new loan offers real savings.

Key Takeaways

  • Identify your mortgage insurance type: Conventional PMI and FHA MIP have different costs and cancellation rules. Check your loan documents or ask your servicer which terms apply.
  • Check cancellation options before refinancing: Conventional borrowers may be able to request PMI removal after meeting their loan’s equity and eligibility requirements. FHA MIP follows separate rules.
  • Compare refinance costs with potential savings: Review closing costs, the new rate and term, monthly payments, and total interest. Estimate how long it will take for savings to cover upfront costs.

What Is PMI, and How Does FHA Mortgage Insurance Differ?

Why conventional lenders require PMI

Private mortgage insurance, or PMI, is usually required on a conventional loan when your down payment is less than 20% of the home’s purchase price. It protects the lender against some financial loss if you default, but you pay the premiums. PMI doesn’t cover your mortgage payments if you lose income, and it doesn’t protect your home or belongings.

PMI can be paid monthly, upfront at closing, or through a combination of both. Its cost depends on factors such as your down payment, credit profile, loan amount, and mortgage terms. A smaller down payment can mean a higher premium, so include PMI when estimating your monthly housing costs. Framework Homeownership explains how PMI works.

A PMI requirement doesn’t necessarily mean you need to wait until you have a 20% down payment to buy. It can make homeownership possible sooner, but it’s worth understanding the cost and the conditions for ending the coverage. Your loan documents and servicer can help clarify the rules for your specific mortgage.

How FHA upfront and annual premiums work

FHA loans charge mortgage insurance premiums, or MIP, rather than conventional PMI. MIP generally includes an upfront premium and an annual premium. The standard upfront premium is generally 1.75% of the base loan amount. You can pay it at closing or, in many cases, add it to the loan balance.

The annual premium is usually divided into monthly payments and included in your mortgage bill. Its amount depends on factors such as your loan balance, term, and loan-to-value ratio. If you finance the upfront premium, you borrow more, which can also increase the interest you pay over time.

Premium rates and requirements can vary by loan details, so check your own estimate and closing documents. For current program information, review HUD’s FHA mortgage insurance guidance. A mortgage professional can help you see how both premiums affect your payment and overall borrowing costs.

When FHA mortgage insurance ends based on loan terms and down payment

FHA MIP doesn’t follow the same cancellation rules as PMI on a conventional loan. For many FHA loans with case numbers assigned on or after June 3, 2013, annual MIP lasts 11 years if the original loan-to-value ratio was 90% or less. If the original LTV was above 90%, annual MIP generally lasts for the life of the loan.

That often means borrowers who put at least 10% down pay annual MIP for 11 years, while borrowers who put down less may pay it for the full loan term. The rule depends on your loan’s details, so check your closing documents or ask your servicer to confirm when your premium is scheduled to end. Framework Homeownership summarizes these FHA insurance rules.

If your MIP is set to last for the life of the loan, refinancing into a conventional mortgage may be an option. You’ll need to qualify for the new loan and meet its requirements, including any equity threshold needed to avoid PMI.

How mortgage insurance differs from homeowners insurance

Mortgage insurance and homeowners insurance protect different interests. PMI or FHA MIP protects the lender against some losses if you default. It doesn’t pay your mortgage for you, cover damage to your home, or prevent foreclosure. As Bankrate explains, PMI protects the lender, not the borrower.

Homeowners insurance helps protect you and your property against covered events, such as certain kinds of fire damage or theft. It may also include liability coverage. Lenders commonly require homeowners insurance as a condition of the mortgage, but it doesn’t replace mortgage insurance. Likewise, mortgage insurance doesn’t replace a homeowners policy.

If your monthly mortgage payment includes insurance charges, check your statement to see how each one is listed. Your servicer can explain charges collected with your payment, while your insurance agent can review what your homeowners policy covers. Knowing which coverage you’re paying for makes it easier to understand your housing costs and determine which charges may change over time.

Cancel PMI Without Refinancing

Refinancing isn’t the only way to stop paying mortgage insurance. If you have a conventional loan with private mortgage insurance (PMI), you may be able to ask your servicer to cancel it after you build enough equity. For many loans, you can make that request when your balance reaches 80% of the home’s original value. PMI may also end automatically at a later milestone.

The rules depend on your loan type, payment history, and servicer requirements. In particular, conventional PMI and FHA mortgage insurance premiums (MIP) follow different rules. Check your loan documents and ask your servicer which cancellation options apply. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s PMI guidance explains the standard milestones and conditions.

Request cancellation when your balance reaches 80% of the home’s original value

For many conventional loans, you can request PMI cancellation when your principal balance reaches 80% of the home’s original value. For a purchase loan, original value generally means the lower of the purchase price or the home’s appraised value when you bought it. For a refinanced loan, it generally refers to the appraisal value used for that loan.

You might reach the 80% threshold through regular payments, extra payments toward principal, or both. Contact your servicer to confirm the balance you need to reach and how to submit a cancellation request. The servicer may also check your payment history and whether you have other liens on the home. Framework’s PMI guide outlines the 80% request milestone, but your servicer can explain the rules for your mortgage.

Understand automatic termination at 78% or the loan-term midpoint

If you don’t request PMI cancellation at 80%, federal rules generally require automatic termination when your balance is scheduled to reach 78% of the home’s original value, as long as your payments are current. This date is typically based on your original amortization schedule. Extra principal payments may help you request cancellation sooner, but they may not change the scheduled date for automatic termination.

A separate rule may apply if PMI remains in place when you reach the midpoint of your loan’s amortization period. Automatic termination at that point generally requires that you’re current on payments. These milestones don’t apply to every type of mortgage insurance, so confirm your loan’s coverage and timeline with your servicer. The CFPB explains the 78% and midpoint rules.

Ask if appreciation or home improvements qualify for earlier cancellation

Your home may be worth more now than when you bought it, particularly if local values have risen or you’ve completed substantial renovations. Some conventional loans, including certain loans owned or backed by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, may allow PMI cancellation based on the home’s current value. The requirements can depend on the loan, how long you’ve had it, and your payment history.

Ask your servicer whether a current-value review is available and whether improvements count toward its decision. You may need to pay for an appraisal or another valuation, and an online estimate may not meet the servicer’s requirements. Don’t assume appreciation alone is enough. The CFPB notes that some Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac loans have separate cancellation guidelines.

Check your payment history, servicer rules, and appraisal requirements

Before you request cancellation, confirm that your mortgage payments are current and ask whether your payment history meets the servicer’s standards. A late payment or other loan issue may affect eligibility, even if your balance has reached the required level.

Ask the servicer to describe the process and requirements in writing. Find out whether you need to submit a formal request, provide documents, or pay for an appraisal. If the servicer reviews your home’s current value, it may require an approved appraiser or valuation method. Keep copies of your request and supporting records, and ask when you can expect a decision. The CFPB’s cancellation guidance covers common conditions, while your servicer can confirm how they apply to your loan.

Confirm your loan’s eligibility; FHA MIP follows different rules

FHA loans use mortgage insurance premiums (MIP), not conventional PMI, and different cancellation rules apply. The duration of FHA MIP can depend on when you took out the loan, your original down payment, and the loan-to-value ratio. For many FHA borrowers who put less than 10% down, annual MIP lasts for the life of the loan. Some borrowers may have a limited MIP period, so check your closing documents or ask your servicer for your loan’s specific terms.

If your FHA MIP cannot be removed under those rules, refinancing into a conventional loan may be an option if you qualify. You’ll need to meet the new lender’s requirements, including any equity threshold to avoid PMI. Review the FHA MIP rules and refinance considerations, then compare the potential savings with the costs of a new loan.

When Can Refinancing Remove PMI?

Refinancing may eliminate mortgage insurance when you replace your current mortgage with a new loan that does not require it. A common example is refinancing an FHA loan into a conventional mortgage after building enough equity. To avoid PMI on the new conventional loan, you’ll generally need a loan-to-value ratio (LTV) of 80% or less.

Equity is only one part of the decision. A lender will also review your credit, income, debts, property, and the terms of the new loan. Before you apply, compare the potential insurance savings with closing costs and any change to your rate or loan term.

Refinance an FHA loan into a conventional mortgage to replace FHA MIP

FHA loans have mortgage insurance premiums, known as MIP, rather than conventional private mortgage insurance (PMI). Depending on your loan’s terms and down payment, FHA MIP may last for 11 years or for the life of the loan. If your MIP is scheduled to continue for the life of your mortgage, refinancing into a conventional loan may be a way to replace it.

To avoid PMI on the new loan, you’ll generally need at least 20% equity, as well as lender approval. The new mortgage may come with a different interest rate, payment, and term, so weigh those changes against the MIP you would no longer pay. Review the FHA mortgage insurance rules and ask your servicer how long MIP is expected to remain on your current loan.

Reach the equity required to avoid conventional PMI, typically 20%

Conventional lenders generally require PMI when a borrower puts less than 20% down or has more than 80% LTV. For a refinance, the lender compares the new loan amount with the home’s appraised value. If the new loan is no more than 80% of that value, you may qualify to refinance without PMI, subject to the lender’s requirements.

You can build equity by paying down your mortgage, through an increase in your home’s value, or both. However, having 20% equity does not guarantee approval or ensure that refinancing will save money. Closing costs, the new rate, and the loan term also affect the overall value of the refinance. The CFPB’s guidance on PMI cancellation explains the 80% LTV benchmark for conventional PMI.

Estimate your loan-to-value ratio using your home’s current value

To get a rough LTV estimate, divide the amount you plan to borrow by your home’s estimated value, then multiply by 100. For example, a three hundred twenty thousand dollars loan on a home valued at four hundred thousand dollars has an estimated LTV of 80%. This calculation can help you decide whether to request a refinance estimate, but it is not a lender’s final determination.

The lender will use an appraisal or another approved valuation method to establish the property’s value. Your final loan amount may also include costs you choose to finance, which can raise the LTV. Ask how the lender will determine your home’s value and calculate the new loan amount. The CFPB explains when an appraisal may be required, which can help you understand why an estimate based on online home values may differ from the lender’s figure.

See how appreciation, principal paydown, and credit affect eligibility

Your home’s value may rise over time, and each mortgage payment typically reduces the principal you owe. Either change can improve your equity position, and together they may bring your LTV below 80%. A new appraisal can help establish whether the home’s current value supports a refinance without PMI. Ask the lender which valuation methods it accepts before relying on an estimated increase in value.

Credit also matters. Lenders review your credit history when deciding whether to approve a new mortgage and what rate to offer. If your credit has improved since you took out your current loan, you may qualify for more favorable terms. If it has declined, approval or pricing may be less attractive. Refinancing to remove PMI can make sense only when the full costs and potential savings work for your situation.

Meet the new loan’s credit, income, debt, occupancy, and underwriting requirements

A refinance is a new mortgage application, so the lender will review your finances and the property. You may need to provide details about your income, employment, debts, assets, and credit. The lender will also consider the home’s value, whether it is your primary residence or another type of property, and the loan amount you’re requesting. Requirements vary by lender and loan program, so meeting the equity threshold alone may not be enough.

Gather recent pay statements, tax documents, bank statements, and information about your existing mortgage before requesting estimates. Self-employed borrowers may need additional records to document income. Ask lenders what they require and how occupancy affects the loan terms. Know that another FHA loan usually won’t remove FHA mortgage insurance

Refinancing into another FHA loan generally does not eliminate FHA mortgage insurance. The new FHA mortgage has its own MIP requirements, so changing FHA loans may not remove the cost you want to avoid. If eliminating mortgage insurance is your goal, ask a lender to compare an FHA refinance with a conventional refinance, including the rate, closing costs, and insurance for each option.

Your current FHA loan’s terms determine how long MIP applies. Factors such as the loan’s origination date and original down payment may affect its duration. Confirm those details with your servicer before deciding whether to refinance. The CFPB’s mortgage insurance guidance also notes that different rules can apply to loans with lender-paid mortgage insurance. Compare the full cost of each option rather than focusing on the insurance payment alone.

Weigh Refinancing Costs Against Savings

Refinancing may remove mortgage insurance, but the new loan comes with costs and could change your rate, payment, or payoff timeline. Compare the full financial picture, not just the insurance charge, before deciding whether the savings justify refinancing.

Compare lender fees, appraisal costs, and other closing costs

Ask lenders for a written estimate of all refinance expenses. Common charges include lender origination and underwriting fees, appraisal and title services, recording fees, and other closing costs. Check your current loan documents for any fee that may apply when you pay off the loan.

Compare these costs with the mortgage insurance you expect to save. If you plan to roll closing costs into the new loan, account for the higher balance and the interest you’ll pay on it. A refinance cost breakdown can help you identify expenses to ask about. Request estimates from multiple lenders, using similar loan amounts and terms so you can make a fair comparison.

Evaluate the new rate, payment, term, and total interest

Look beyond the monthly payment. Compare your current interest rate with the proposed rate, and check whether the refinance changes your fixed or adjustable rate, repayment term, or other loan features. A lower payment may come from extending the loan term, even if that means paying more interest over time.

Compare the full monthly housing payment, including principal, interest, taxes, homeowners insurance, and any mortgage insurance on the new loan. Then consider how much interest you may pay during the time you expect to keep the mortgage. Refinancing may be more attractive if it removes mortgage insurance and also offers a rate or term that fits your needs. Calculate your break-even point using insurance and payment savings

A basic break-even estimate divides your refinance costs by your expected monthly savings. For example, if closing costs total four thousand dollars and your full monthly payment falls by two hundred dollars, it would take about 20 months to recover those costs. This estimate is a useful starting point, but it does not capture every change in loan balance or interest.

Use the difference between your current and proposed payments, including principal, interest, and mortgage insurance, rather than counting only the PMI charge. Include any costs added to the loan balance, too. If the new loan has a different term or rate, ask a mortgage professional to help you compare the total costs. This break-even guide explains the basic calculation.

Consider how long you’ll keep the home and loan

Your plans for the home can affect whether refinancing makes sense. If you expect to move or refinance again before reaching the break-even point, you may not have enough time to recover the upfront costs. If you plan to stay longer, estimate how much you could save after reaching that point, while also considering the new loan’s total cost.

Check when your current mortgage insurance could end without refinancing. If cancellation or automatic termination is near, waiting may cost less, particularly if your current rate is lower than the refinance offers you receive. Ask your servicer to explain the rules and timeline for your loan. This discussion of refinance timing also highlights why your expected time in the home matters.

Account for rolled-in costs, a possible term reset, and slower principal paydown

Rolling closing costs into the new loan can reduce the cash you need at closing, but it increases the amount you borrow. You’ll pay interest on those costs, and extending the repayment term can slow how quickly you pay down principal. As a result, a lower monthly payment does not always mean the loan will cost less overall.

Compare your current balance and remaining term with the proposed balance and term. Ask how much principal you would pay off over the next several years under each option. If refinancing restarts a 30-year term, request a comparison with a shorter term as well. This mortgage comparison guide covers costs, term changes, and insurance savings. Refinance or Keep Your Current Loan?

A refinance replaces your current mortgage with a new one, so removing mortgage insurance is only one part of the decision. Compare refinancing with asking your servicer to cancel PMI or waiting for it to end. Then weigh the full cost of the new loan, including its rate, term, and closing costs.

Compare refinancing with requesting PMI cancellation or waiting for automatic termination

If you have a conventional loan, you may be able to cancel PMI without refinancing. Depending on your loan terms, payment history, and equity, you can ask your servicer to remove it once your balance meets the required threshold. PMI may also end automatically at a point set by your original repayment schedule. Ask your servicer when you qualify and whether you need an appraisal or other documentation.

Keeping your current loan may let you retain its rate and term while avoiding refinance costs. Lower explains that refinancing is not always necessary to remove PMI. FHA mortgage insurance follows different rules, so confirm which requirements apply to your loan.

Check whether a higher rate could outweigh insurance savings

Removing mortgage insurance may reduce your monthly housing costs, but a higher refinance rate could offset some or all of that savings. Compare the new principal-and-interest payment, any mortgage insurance, and your current payment. Include closing costs, such as lender fees and appraisal charges, in your estimate.

To get a rough break-even point, divide the refinance costs by your expected monthly savings. The result estimates how many months it could take for savings to cover the upfront expense. If you might move before that point, or the new payment is higher, refinancing may not be the right fit. Yahoo Finance’s overview also recommends weighing insurance savings against the full cost of refinancing.

Compare your current rate with refinance offers, not just monthly payments

A lower monthly payment does not always mean a lower-cost loan. The payment may decrease because the refinance extends repayment over a longer period, which can mean paying more interest over time. Compare your current rate, remaining balance, and time left on the loan with the proposed rate, loan amount, and repayment term.

Ask lenders for written estimates so you can compare the same details. Review lender fees, appraisal costs, and any closing costs added to the new loan balance. Review changes to your rate type, term, and other loan features

A refinance can change more than your monthly payment. Check whether the new loan has a fixed or adjustable rate, whether the repayment term changes, and whether you would give up any features that matter to you. A lower rate and no PMI may be appealing, but a longer term can slow your progress in paying down the principal. A shorter term may help you build equity faster, though it can raise your monthly payment.

Ask your servicer for your exact cancellation rules and current balance

Contact your mortgage servicer for your current principal balance and the specific steps to request PMI cancellation. For many conventional loans, you can request cancellation when your balance reaches 80% of the home’s original value. Extra payments may help you reach that threshold sooner.

Ask whether you need to submit a written request or meet other conditions, such as having a strong payment history or obtaining an appraisal. Get a refinance estimate and confirm whether the new loan requires PMI

Ask a lender for a refinance estimate that includes the interest rate, monthly payment, closing costs, and any mortgage insurance on the new loan. Conventional loans generally require PMI when a borrower has less than 20% equity, while FHA loans follow different mortgage insurance rules.

If you have an FHA loan, refinancing into a conventional mortgage may replace FHA mortgage insurance, but you’ll need to qualify and meet the new loan’s equity requirements. If you already have a conventional loan, you may be able to request PMI cancellation without refinancing. Compare both options before making a decision. Compare written loan estimates, fees, and total borrowing costs

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I remove PMI without refinancing?
Often, yes. If you have a conventional mortgage, contact your servicer to ask whether you qualify to cancel PMI under your loan’s rules. They can confirm the required balance, payment history, and whether you need an appraisal or written request.

Does reaching 20% equity automatically remove PMI?
Not always. For many conventional loans, reaching 20% equity may let you request cancellation, but your servicer may need to approve it. Automatic termination generally follows a separate schedule, so ask your servicer for the dates that apply to your mortgage.

Can I cancel FHA mortgage insurance the same way as conventional PMI?
No. FHA mortgage insurance premiums, or MIP, follow different rules from conventional PMI. How long you pay MIP depends on your loan details, including its terms and original down payment. Check your closing documents or contact your servicer for your specific timeline.

Will refinancing always save money if it removes mortgage insurance?
No. Compare the expected insurance savings with closing costs, the new interest rate, loan term, and total interest. A lower monthly payment may not mean a lower overall cost, especially if the refinance extends your repayment period.

What should I ask a lender before refinancing to remove mortgage insurance?
Request a written estimate showing the rate, loan term, monthly payment, closing costs, and any insurance required on the new loan. Ask how the lender will determine your home’s value and whether your estimated equity meets the program’s requirements. Then compare the figures with your current loan and ask when you might recover the refinance costs.
