Reverse Mortgage vs Home Equity Loan

Reverse mortgage vs home equity loan: compare age, payments, and repayment, then ask a New Jersey broker which option fits.

A reverse mortgage vs home equity loan comparison comes down to how the loan behaves after you sign, not which product sounds more generous. A Home Equity Conversion Mortgage, the most common reverse mortgage, is only for homeowners age 62 or older, and you do not make a monthly principal-and-interest payment. A home equity loan can fit a wider range of adult homeowners, pays a lump sum, and usually asks for a fixed payment on a set schedule. Both use your home as collateral, and both can lead to foreclosure if you do not meet the loan's rules.

What each loan actually does

Equity is what your home is worth minus what you already owe on it. Both loans borrow against that equity. They do not hand you a gift, and they do not replace the duty to keep the house insured and the taxes paid.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes a HECM as a loan for homeowners 62 and older. You keep title. You do not make a monthly mortgage payment. Interest and fees are added to the balance each month, so the amount you owe goes up and the equity left in the home goes down. The loan is repaid when you no longer live in the home, usually by selling it. The CFPB's plain statement is that a reverse mortgage is not free money. You, or your heirs, still repay it.

A home equity loan is different in timing. The CFPB says you receive a specific amount as a lump sum, often at a fixed rate, though some loans use an adjustable rate. If you already have a first mortgage, this new loan is a second mortgage. You pay it in addition to the first mortgage. If you cannot repay, the lender can foreclose.

A home equity line of credit is a third structure: a revolving line you draw from over time, often at a variable rate. If that is what you want, the comparison in this article is the wrong one. Start with the programs page and ask about a HELOC instead of a closed-end equity loan.

The age line is the first filter. If you are under 62, a HECM is not available to you under the rule the CFPB states. A home equity loan might still be, if a lender approves the equity, the payment, and the rest of the file. If you are 62 or older, age alone does not pick the winner. The payment and the growing balance do.

What you still owe after a reverse mortgage

Skipping a principal-and-interest payment is the feature people remember. The obligations they skip in the brochure are the ones that cause trouble later. The CFPB lists three HECM duties. Pay property charges, including property taxes and homeowners insurance, on time. Keep the home in good repair. Live in it as your principal residence. Fail those and you can lose the home to foreclosure even though you were not sending a mortgage payment.

Before closing, the lender runs a financial assessment. It looks at how you will keep paying taxes and insurance, and flood insurance when that applies. If your credit, your history of paying property charges, and your residual income look sufficient, you may pay those charges yourself. You may also have the servicer pay them from the loan funds.

If the lender decides a reserve is needed, part of the proceeds is set aside for those bills. That set-aside is why two neighbors with similar homes can see very different amounts available to spend. The loan did not get smaller. More of it was reserved for bills you already have.

The balance itself moves the other way from a normal loan. Each month, interest and fees join the amount you owe. Equity that felt comfortable on the day you signed can be much thinner years later. That is the trade for not making a principal-and-interest payment. Anyone promising that a reverse mortgage costs nothing, or that the balance stays flat, is not describing the HECM the CFPB explains.

What a home equity loan asks of you instead

A home equity loan is easier to picture because it behaves like the mortgage you already know. You borrow a set amount, you receive it once, and you repay it on a schedule. The CFPB notes that the rate is usually fixed, which makes the payment more predictable than a line of credit whose rate can move. Predictable is not the same as cheap. Upfront costs can apply, and the only honest way to judge them is to compare the full loan estimate from more than one lender, not a single monthly figure.

Because the loan is often a second mortgage, the payment stacks on top of the first mortgage, taxes, and insurance. That stack is the qualification question. A lender has to believe the household can carry it. There is no universal score or income rule on the CFPB page we are using, so do not treat a number from an ad as a promise.

If the payment would be tight, the CFPB suggests a nonprofit credit counselor before you pledge the house to consolidate other debts. Borrowing against the home to fund an investment is a separate warning. No investment is risk-free, and a loss can leave you unable to repay a loan secured by where you live.

The useful contrast is direction. On a performing home equity loan, principal goes down and equity can rebuild. On a HECM, the loan balance goes up and equity shrinks unless the home's value rises enough to offset it. One is a scheduled paydown. The other is a planned increase in what you owe, repaid later.

Who each loan tends to fit

Think of the fit as a household question, not a product ranking. A homeowner who is 62 or older, plans to stay, and cannot comfortably add a new principal-and-interest payment is the person a HECM was built for. The CFPB still requires that person to cover taxes, insurance, and upkeep, and to keep the house as a principal residence. If those bills are already late, the financial assessment can shrink what is available or stop the loan.

A home equity loan tends to fit a homeowner with equity, income a lender will count, and a one-time use for the money. The use might be a repair with a known scope. It might be a project you will pay back on purpose. It might be a debt you have already decided to move onto the house after talking with a counselor. The payment is the price of keeping the balance on a downward path. If that payment would strain the same budget that pays the first mortgage, the loan is the wrong tool even when the equity looks large.

Some files fit neither. A homeowner under 62 cannot use the HECM age rule as written. A homeowner with little equity may not have enough to borrow under either structure. Someone who wants to draw, repay, and draw again is describing a line of credit, which is a different product from both loans in this comparison. And someone who needs the house to be an investment property is outside the HECM principal-residence rule. Those cases belong in a broader look at the programs EZ Way can shop, not in a forced choice between these two.

What heirs and a future sale should know

Repayment day is easy to ignore because it may be years away. On a HECM, that day arrives when the borrower no longer lives in the home. A sale is the usual way the loan gets paid. Heirs do not inherit a free-and-clear house by default. They inherit a house with a balance that has been growing. The CFPB's framing is direct: borrowed money, plus interest, plus fees, becomes a rising balance, and someone repays it.

A home equity loan changes the heir conversation in the other direction, but only if the payments were made. Each principal payment leaves a little more equity. Missed payments do the opposite and can put the house in foreclosure before any inheritance discussion happens. If you already have a first mortgage, heirs or a future buyer are looking at two liens, not one, until both are paid off or refinanced.

Neither outcome is something to guess from a blog. Ask the lender, in writing, what must be repaid if you sell, if you move to assisted living, or if you die while the loan is outstanding. Ask whether anyone else on the title has to be a borrower. Those answers decide whether the loan still fits the way your family uses the house. They matter more than a slogan about staying in your home.

How to choose without guessing a payment

Use the loan that matches the problem, then confirm it with a person who can see your file.

  1. Write down why you need the money. A one-time project with a clear payoff date points toward a lump-sum home equity loan. A need to stay in the home with no new principal-and-interest payment points toward asking whether a reverse mortgage is even available.
  2. Check the hard gates. Under 62, stop on the HECM path and look at equity loans or other options on the programs page. At 62 or older, still ask whether you can keep taxes, insurance, and maintenance current, because those duties do not go away.
  3. Ask what the balance does in year five, not just what you receive in month one. A growing HECM balance changes what heirs inherit. A shrinking equity-loan balance does the opposite, but only if the payment is one you can keep making.
  4. Ask who holds the other lien. A home equity loan behind a first mortgage means two payments. A reverse mortgage usually has to fit with, or pay off, what is already on the title. That structure is a lender question, not something to assume from a blog.
  5. Bring the comparison to a brokerage conversation instead of picking from an advertisement. EZ Way shops partner lenders for the programs it actually offers. Ask which of those programs a lender will consider, and ask for the loan estimate before you decide.

Frequently asked questions

Is a reverse mortgage the same as a home equity loan?

No. A HECM reverse mortgage is for homeowners 62 or older, does not require a monthly principal-and-interest payment, and grows the balance over time. A home equity loan is a lump sum that you repay on a schedule, often at a fixed rate, and it is a second mortgage if you already have a first mortgage.

Do you ever repay a reverse mortgage?

Yes. The CFPB says the loan is repaid when you no longer live in the home, usually by selling it. Heirs generally face the same repayment. "No monthly mortgage payment" does not mean "no repayment."

Can you lose the house with either loan?

Yes. A home equity lender can foreclose if you do not repay. A HECM servicer can move toward foreclosure if you do not pay property charges, do not keep the home in good repair, or do not live there as your principal residence.

Does EZ Way offer reverse mortgages?

The programs page lists home equity and HELOC choices among other loans. It does not advertise a reverse mortgage. Ask during a pre-approval conversation which partner-lender programs are available for your situation. Availability is not a promise that you will be approved.

Should you use either loan to pay off credit cards?

Only after you understand that you are moving unsecured debt onto the house. The CFPB suggests talking with a nonprofit credit counselor before using a home equity loan to consolidate debts. The same caution applies before you use home equity, in any form, for an investment.
