Mortgage Income Requirements: Large Deposit Explanation

Learn why lenders ask about large mortgage deposits, which records may help explain gifts, transfers, and sales, and how to prepare for review.

A mortgage large deposit explanation can help you prepare when money appears in your bank account shortly before a home purchase. A lender may ask where a sizable deposit came from so the funds can be documented and reviewed as part of your mortgage application. Knowing what records to gather can make that conversation clearer.

Why might a lender ask about a large deposit?

During a mortgage review, a lender looks at your finances and supporting documents. An unexplained deposit can raise questions about the source of funds, whether the money is available for the transaction, and whether it is a gift, a loan, or your own savings. A request for more information does not, by itself, mean a loan will be declined. The lender needs enough documentation to assess the application under its underwriting guidelines.

Requirements can vary by loan program and lender. A bank statement may show a transfer without identifying the account or person it came from. A short, accurate explanation and supporting records can help fill in that context. Requirements for documenting assets are part of federal mortgage underwriting rules; see the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's Ability-to-Repay rule for the regulation's framework. Your lender will explain which records apply to your application.

What kinds of deposits may need documentation?

A lender may ask about money that is unusually large compared with regular account activity or cannot be traced from the statement. Common situations include:

  • A gift from a relative or another permitted source.
  • Money moved between your own bank or investment accounts.
  • Funds from selling a vehicle, personal property, or another asset.
  • A work bonus, commission, or other one-time payment.
  • An insurance payment, inheritance, or other documented source.
  • Cash deposited into an account, where the source may be difficult to verify.

These examples are not a promise that a particular deposit will be accepted. The lender decides what documentation is needed and whether funds meet the requirements for the loan program.

How do you explain a deposit clearly?

Start with the facts: when the deposit occurred, its source, who provided or transferred it, and whether you must repay it. Then gather records that connect the explanation to account activity. Do not guess, leave out a related loan, or describe borrowed money as a gift. If you are unsure how to classify a deposit, ask the mortgage professional handling your application before submitting an explanation.

  1. Identify the transaction. Find the date and amount shown on the statement. Note whether it was cash, a check, a transfer, or an electronic payment.
  2. Write a short explanation. State the source in plain language. Keep it factual and consistent with your records.
  3. Collect supporting documents. The needed records depend on the source. Provide complete pages or records when requested, rather than cropped images that omit relevant details.
  4. Send documents through the requested channel. Financial records contain sensitive information. Use the lender or mortgage professional's instructions for secure submission.
  5. Respond to follow-up questions. The lender may need another record to connect the money from its original source to your account.

What documents can support the explanation?

Match your records to the type of deposit. A transfer between your own accounts may be supported by statements from both accounts showing the withdrawal and matching deposit. For a gift, the lender may require a gift letter and evidence of the transfer, with details depending on program rules. A sale may call for a bill of sale and proof that the funds reached your account. A work payment may be supported by a pay record and bank statement. The lender will tell you which documents are appropriate for your situation.

Keep the explanation and records consistent. If the deposit was a loan, say so and ask what documentation is required. A loan may affect how the lender evaluates your finances. If the money came from several sources, describe each one separately instead of combining them into a vague explanation.

How can you trace common deposit scenarios?

Think of documentation as a chain connecting the source to your account. For a transfer, keep the outgoing record from the first account and the incoming record from the second. For a sale, retain the agreement or receipt and a record of the payment, then match it to the bank activity. For a gift, ask the lender what letter and transfer records it needs before the money moves. These steps help make the source easier to follow; they do not establish that funds qualify under a particular program.

For example, if a statement shows a single electronic credit, it may not identify the sender. A related statement or transaction confirmation might show the originating account. If the money came from more than one source, note the amount and date for each deposit rather than describing the total as one lump sum. Keep copies of what you submit so you can answer follow-up questions consistently.

What if the money was deposited in cash?

Cash can be harder to document because a bank statement may show the deposit without showing where the funds came from. Tell the lender the true source and ask what evidence it can consider. Do not create paperwork after the fact or move money around to make a deposit appear different. If you have not deposited the funds yet, ask about documentation before doing so. The lender can explain its process, but only underwriting can determine whether the funds can be used for the transaction.

How can you prepare before applying?

Keep financial records organized and avoid unnecessary transfers while your application is under review. If you expect to receive a gift, sell an asset, or move money between accounts, ask what records to retain. Do not assume that a deposit is too small or too familiar to need an explanation; follow the lender's specific request. If a record is unavailable, explain why and ask what alternative documentation may be considered.

  • What secure method should I use to send financial documents?
