How to Deduct Points on Refinance: A Tax Guide

Learn how to deduct points on refinance, what records to keep, and which tax rules apply when claiming mortgage points on your next tax return.

A lower mortgage rate may sound appealing, especially when you plan to stay in your home for years. But paying discount points at closing adds an upfront cost, so it’s worth understanding both the loan savings and possible tax treatment before you decide. Homeowners often search “deduct points on refinance” and find answers that depend on details such as whether they itemize deductions, how they use cash-out proceeds, and whether the charge actually qualifies as a point. This guide breaks down the basics in plain language. You’ll learn how points differ from other closing costs, when deductions may apply, and which documents to save for your tax preparer.

Key Takeaways

  • Not every refinance fee qualifies as a mortgage point: Check your Closing Disclosure and confirm which charges are prepaid interest rather than fees for services.
  • Most qualifying points are deducted over the new loan’s term: Cash-out spending, qualifying home improvements, and an early payoff may affect when or whether you can claim them.
  • Keep documents that support your calculation: Save closing paperwork, payment records, loan statements, tax returns, and receipts for cash-out expenses, then ask a tax professional to review your situation.

What Are Mortgage Points on a Refinance?

Discount points vs. origination fees and closing costs

Discount points are optional upfront payments that may lower your mortgage interest rate. One point typically costs 1% of the loan amount. For example, one point on a $300,000 refinance would cost $3,000.

Origination fees are different. They cover lender services involved in processing and setting up your loan, and they do not necessarily reduce your interest rate. Closing costs is a broader term for the expenses due at closing. These may include discount points, origination fees, appraisal charges, title services, and other costs.

Loan paperwork can use the word “points” for charges that are not discount points. Review the Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure to see what each fee covers, and ask your lender to explain any unclear item. The IRS guidance on mortgage points describes qualifying points as prepaid interest, but a fee’s label alone does not determine its tax treatment.

How discount points may lower your rate

Paying discount points may lower your interest rate, which can reduce your monthly payment and the interest you pay over time. The reduction varies. One point may lower a rate by about 0.25 percentage points, but the actual change depends on factors such as the lender, loan type, and market conditions.

To assess whether points make sense, compare their upfront cost with the estimated savings over the time you expect to keep the refinanced loan. If you sell or refinance again before those savings make up the cost, paying points may not be worthwhile. Ask your lender for rate options with and without points, then compare the costs and payments side by side. NerdWallet’s explanation of discount points provides more detail on how they can affect a mortgage.

Why qualifying points count as prepaid interest

Discount points are paid at closing in exchange for a lower interest rate over the loan term. Because qualifying points represent interest paid in advance, they may receive the tax treatment of mortgage interest. However, that does not usually mean you can deduct the full amount in the year you refinance. Points paid on a refinanced mortgage are generally deducted over the loan term, subject to applicable tax rules.

Keep your Closing Disclosure and proof of payment, and make sure discount points are listed separately from other fees. These records can help you and your tax professional determine whether the charges qualify and when to claim any deduction. The IRS explains the general rules for deducting mortgage points, including how points on a refinance are typically spread across the loan term.

Are Refinance Points Tax-Deductible?

Refinance points may be deductible, but usually not all at once. Your eligibility depends on whether the charge qualifies as a point, whether you itemize deductions, and how you use the loan proceeds. Keep your closing documents and spending records, and ask a qualified tax professional how the rules apply to your return.

The general rule: spread points over the loan term

Points paid to refinance a mortgage are generally deducted in equal portions over the term of the new loan. For example, if you pay $3,000 in qualifying points on a 30-year refinance, the basic annual deduction would be $100, subject to applicable limits and other eligibility rules.

The IRS guidance on home mortgage points explains how points are treated over a loan’s term. Check your Closing Disclosure to identify the charges, but don’t assume every lender fee is a point. A charge for an appraisal, title search, or another service generally does not count as prepaid interest. If the documents group charges together, ask your lender for a clear breakdown so you can share it with your tax professional.

Itemized deductions and qualified-residence rules

You generally must itemize deductions on your federal return to claim a deduction for mortgage points. If you take the standard deduction, you usually cannot claim points separately. Itemizing alone does not make a charge deductible: the points and the loan must also meet the applicable tax rules.

The mortgage must be secured by a qualified residence, typically your main home or a second home that meets IRS requirements. The deduction may also be limited by the rules for qualified home mortgage interest, including limits based on the amount and use of the debt. The IRS explanation of mortgage points is a useful starting point, but your eligibility depends on your specific loan and tax situation. Consider reviewing the details with a qualified tax professional before filing.

How mortgage-interest limits and loan purpose affect eligibility

Mortgage points are generally treated as prepaid interest, so rules that apply to home mortgage interest can affect whether you can deduct them. The loan must be secured by a qualified home, and the charge must be for borrowing money, not for a separate closing service. Limits on eligible mortgage debt may also restrict the amount you can claim.

The purpose of the refinance matters, particularly if you take cash out. Points tied to proceeds used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home may qualify, subject to the relevant requirements. Points connected to proceeds used for other purposes generally do not qualify as home mortgage interest. Keep records of the loan amount and how you used the funds. The IRS rules for home mortgage points can help clarify the distinction.

How to treat points on cash-out proceeds

Cash received through a cash-out refinance is generally borrowed money, not taxable income, because you must repay it. However, that does not mean points connected to the cash-out portion are automatically deductible. Their treatment depends on how you use those proceeds and whether the other requirements for deducting mortgage interest are met.

If you use cash-out funds to buy, build, or substantially improve the home securing the loan, the points allocated to that portion may qualify for deduction over the loan term. Points related to money spent on unrelated expenses generally do not qualify as home mortgage interest. Keep invoices, receipts, and other records that show where the funds went. Because a refinance may serve several purposes, ask a tax professional to help determine how to allocate the points.

When Can You Deduct Refinance Points in the Year You Pay Them?

Refinance points are generally deducted over the term of the new loan. An exception may apply to the portion of points tied to proceeds used for qualifying improvements to the main home securing the loan. Even then, you must meet IRS requirements, and the deduction may depend on whether you itemize.

Review your Closing Disclosure and keep records showing how you used the refinance funds. The IRS guidance on home mortgage points explains the requirements and which charges do not qualify.

Points for qualifying improvements to your main home

If you use part of your refinance proceeds to improve the main home securing the loan, you may be able to deduct the points associated with that portion in the year you pay them. The remaining points generally must be deducted over the loan term. The immediate deduction is not automatic: the points and the loan must meet the applicable IRS conditions.

Keep invoices, contracts, and proof of payment so you can document the improvement costs and how much of the loan went toward the work. If the refinance also paid off an existing mortgage or provided cash for other purposes, you may need to allocate the points among those uses. TaxAct’s guidance on mortgage points outlines conditions that may apply to deducting qualifying points in the year paid. A tax professional can help determine how the rules apply to your return.

IRS tests for an immediate deduction

For points tied to qualifying improvements to your main home to be deductible in the year paid, several conditions generally must be met. You must use the cash method of accounting, the loan must be secured by your main home, and paying points must be a customary practice in your area. The points also cannot replace separately stated costs, such as appraisal or title fees. In addition, the funds you provide at or before closing, together with any seller-paid points, must be at least as much as the points charged.

If these tests are not met, you will generally deduct qualifying points over the loan term instead. Rules for spreading points over time are different from the requirements for taking a deduction in the year paid. Review the IRS rules for home mortgage points and ask a tax professional to assess your specific loan and payment details.

Points must be customary, percentage-based, and separately stated

To qualify as points, a charge generally must be a usual lending practice in your area and fall within the amount commonly charged there. It must be calculated as a percentage of the mortgage principal and clearly identified as points on your settlement statement or Closing Disclosure. A lump sum labeled “closing costs” is not enough to show that a charge qualifies.

Check the itemized disclosure before closing and ask your lender to clarify any charge you do not understand. A fee does not become deductible mortgage interest simply because it is described as a point. If it pays for a separate service, it is generally treated as that service’s fee instead. The IRS’s overview of home mortgage points explains the criteria for determining whether a charge may qualify.

Fees that don’t qualify as points

Many refinance costs are not deductible as mortgage interest. These can include appraisal fees, mortgage-note preparation, notary fees, and mortgage insurance premiums. Charges for services such as title work, inspections, legal assistance, or property taxes also generally do not qualify as points. The same applies when a lender labels a fee as points but uses it to cover one of these separate costs.

Read the itemized charges on your Closing Disclosure rather than relying on a general estimate or verbal description. If a fee’s purpose is unclear, ask the lender to explain it in writing, then share the explanation with your tax preparer. Keeping the disclosure and related records can help you distinguish potentially deductible points from other refinance costs. The IRS list of mortgage-point rules describes expenses that do not count as deductible points.

How Do You Calculate and Claim the Deduction?

To calculate a deduction for refinance points, first confirm which closing costs qualify as prepaid interest. Then determine whether you can deduct them in the year you paid them or need to spread them over the new loan’s term. Your use of the refinance proceeds and whether you itemize deductions can affect the result.

Find qualifying points on your Closing Disclosure

Review your Closing Disclosure for discount points and other charges described as points. These are prepaid interest, often paid in exchange for a lower interest rate. They are not the same as fees for services such as an appraisal, title search, or credit report. A fee’s label alone does not determine whether it qualifies, so ask your lender to explain any unclear charge.

Keep the Closing Disclosure and proof of payment with your tax records. The IRS guidance on home mortgage points explains that points must represent interest, rather than payment for separate services, to qualify. If the amount shown on your closing documents differs from your lender’s records, ask for clarification before calculating the deduction.

Allocate points to the refinance, improvements, and cash-out proceeds

If your refinance included cash back, document how you used those funds. Points associated with loan proceeds used for personal expenses generally do not qualify as deductible mortgage interest. Points tied to proceeds used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home may qualify, subject to IRS requirements.

Keep receipts, contractor invoices, and bank statements that show how you spent the money. If you used the proceeds for multiple purposes, you may need to allocate the points among those uses. The IRS explains the rules for points paid on home mortgage debt, but the allocation can depend on your specific loan and spending. Consider asking a tax professional to review the calculation if the funds had several uses.

Divide amortized points across the loan term

Points on a refinance are generally deducted over the term of the new loan, rather than all at once. To estimate the annual deduction, divide the qualifying points by the total number of scheduled monthly payments, then multiply that figure by the number of payments made during the tax year.

For example, $6,000 in points on a 30-year loan is spread across 360 payments. That equals about $16.67 per payment, or roughly $200 for 12 payments. Your allowable amount may differ if only some points qualify or you made fewer than 12 payments that year. The IRS outlines the general rule for deducting points over a loan’s term.

Claim the eligible amount when you itemize

You generally claim deductible mortgage points as mortgage interest on Schedule A if you itemize deductions. If you take the standard deduction, you cannot claim a separate deduction for points. Compare your eligible itemized deductions with the standard deduction for your filing status to see which approach applies.

Keep your calculation, Closing Disclosure, and proof of payment together. If you’re claiming points related to home improvements or cash-out proceeds, save records showing how you used the funds. Mortgage-interest limits and qualified-residence rules may also affect the amount you can deduct. Jackson Hewitt’s overview of refinance tax deductions also explains the itemizing requirement.

What Happens If You Refinance Again or Pay Off the Loan?

If you pay off a mortgage or refinance before its term ends, the tax treatment of points you have not yet deducted may change. The rules depend on whether you refinance with the same lender or a different one, and how you use any cash-out proceeds.

Keep your Closing Disclosures, payoff statement, and prior tax returns so you can track the points paid and the amounts already deducted. Whether you can claim a deduction also depends on the loan and your tax situation, including whether you itemize. A tax professional can help you apply the rules to your circumstances.

Deduct remaining points when you pay off the loan

If you pay off a mortgage before the end of its term, you may generally deduct qualifying points that you have not yet deducted in the year the loan is paid off. This can also apply when you refinance with a different lender, because the original loan ends.

To calculate the remaining amount, start with the qualifying points paid on the original loan, then subtract any points deducted in earlier tax years. Keep the payoff statement and prior tax returns to support your calculation. The deduction remains subject to applicable rules, including whether you itemize.

The IRS guidance on deducting points over a mortgage’s life explains how an early payoff or refinance with a different lender can affect undeducted points.

How refinancing with the same lender may change the schedule

When you refinance with the same lender, you generally cannot deduct all the remaining points from the old loan in the year of the refinance. Instead, you continue deducting that balance over the term of the new loan, following the applicable schedule.

Keep records for both loans, including the original points paid, amounts deducted in prior years, and the new loan’s Closing Disclosure. These details help you track the remaining balance and distinguish it from any points charged for the new refinance.

The IRS mortgage-points FAQ describes how the schedule differs when you refinance with the same lender. If you are unsure whether your new loan counts as a same-lender refinance, ask a qualified tax professional to review your documents.

What happens to unamortized points with a new lender

When you refinance with a different lender, the original loan ends. Qualifying points from that loan that remain undeducted may generally be deductible in the year it ends, subject to the rules that apply to your situation.

Points charged on the new loan are separate. They generally follow the deduction schedule for that refinance, rather than being combined with the remaining points from the old loan. Keep a record of each loan’s points, the amounts deducted, and the date the original mortgage was paid off.

The IRS explains the early-payoff rule. Use your Closing Disclosures and prior returns to support your calculations, and ask a tax professional how the rule applies if your refinance involves unusual terms or more than one loan.

Recalculate the deduction if you take cash out

With a cash-out refinance, the use of the proceeds can affect whether points qualify. Points associated with money used to buy, build, or substantially improve your home may qualify under the applicable rules. Points tied to cash used for other purposes, such as paying personal bills, generally do not qualify as home mortgage interest.

You may need to allocate points among the portion used to refinance the existing mortgage, qualifying home improvements, and other cash-out uses. Keep bank statements, invoices, receipts, and other records showing where the proceeds went. These documents can help support your allocation and any deduction you claim.

The IRS guidance on home mortgage points covers points paid when refinancing and the treatment of funds used for home improvements. Because the rules can depend on how the loan proceeds are applied, check your allocation with a qualified tax professional before filing.

What Records Support a Refinance-Points Deduction?

Good records help you identify the points you paid, understand how the loan proceeds were used, and support the amount you report on your tax return. Keep documents from closing, lender statements, and receipts or invoices related to any cash-out funds. Together, these records can help show whether points are spread over the loan term or may qualify for a deduction sooner.

Keep your files organized by tax year, and save copies of the returns where you reported mortgage points. Tax treatment depends on details such as whether you itemize deductions, how you used the loan proceeds, and whether you later paid off or refinanced the loan. The IRS overview of mortgage points explains the general rules, but your records should make it possible to trace each amount back to the transaction and its purpose. If you are unsure how a particular fee or payment should be treated, ask a qualified tax professional before filing.

Keep your Closing Disclosure, settlement statement, and proof of payment

Save the Closing Disclosure and any settlement statement you received for the refinance. These documents show the loan terms and list charges paid at closing, helping you identify points and distinguish them from appraisal, title, or other fees. Keep proof of payment too, such as a bank record, closing account statement, or lender receipt. If points were added to the loan balance, retain documents showing how they were financed.

Review the paperwork as a set. A fee labeled “points” does not automatically qualify for a tax deduction, so keep enough detail to show what the charge covered. Jackson Hewitt also recommends saving closing documents and records showing how you used cash-out funds. Its refinance tax guide offers additional recordkeeping suggestions.

Save Form 1098 and lender statements, even if they don’t report points

Keep Form 1098, which reports mortgage interest paid during the year, alongside your monthly or annual lender statements. The form may include points, but it may not report every amount you need to support a refinance deduction. Compare it with your Closing Disclosure and other loan records, and keep the documents even if no points appear on the form.

If you use tax-preparation software, follow its instructions for entering Form 1098, then check whether it asks separately about points paid at closing. TaxAct’s instructions for mortgage interest and points explain how reported points may be entered. Save a copy of your filed return with the documents you used to prepare it. That makes it easier to review your calculation if you amend a return or need to answer a question later.

Keep improvement invoices and records of cash-out spending

For a cash-out refinance, keep records showing how you used the proceeds. If you used some of the money to buy, build, or substantially improve your home, save contractor invoices, receipts for materials, permits, contracts, and proof of payment. A clear record of the work and its cost can help support how you treated the related points.

A bank statement may confirm that you paid a contractor, but it usually will not explain what work was done. Pair payment records with detailed invoices or receipts, and keep them with your refinance documents. The IRS guidance on mortgage points discusses how points related to home-improvement funds may be treated. Since the rules can depend on how proceeds were allocated, consult a tax professional if the refinance funded both improvements and other expenses.

Retain prior tax returns and an updated amortization schedule

Save the tax returns where you reported points, along with an amortization schedule or your own year-by-year tracking record. For points deducted over the loan term, track the original amount, the loan term, and the amount allocated to each year. Update the schedule if you refinance again or pay off the loan early, since the remaining undeducted points may need different treatment.

If you refinance with a new lender, keep the old loan’s payoff statement and the new loan’s closing documents. These records help distinguish points remaining from the original loan from points paid on the replacement loan. The IRS FAQ on points when a loan ends early explains how unamortized points may be handled after a payoff or refinance.

How Can You Plan a Refinance Around Tax Rules?

Tax treatment can affect the cost of refinancing, but it shouldn’t be the only factor in your decision. Compare the interest rate, closing costs, monthly payment, and how long you expect to keep the loan. If you’re refinancing in New Jersey, ask your mortgage professional to explain the loan terms and fees in plain language. A qualified tax professional can help you understand how current tax rules may apply to your circumstances.

Compare point costs with potential interest savings

Discount points are an upfront cost you pay to obtain a lower mortgage rate. Before paying for them, compare the cost with the interest savings you expect over the time you plan to keep the loan. Ask for payment estimates both with and without points, then use the difference to estimate how long it may take to recover the upfront cost. For example, if points cost $2,400 and lower your payment by $40 per month, the simple break-even estimate is 60 months. This estimate doesn’t account for every loan or tax detail. NerdWallet explains how to assess the break-even point for mortgage points.

Ask your lender to list points separately

An advertised mortgage rate may already include discount points, making it harder to compare offers. Ask each lender for a quote with no points, or request quotes using the same number of points. Then compare the rates and costs on an equal basis.

Ask for an itemized estimate that lists discount points separately from origination charges and other closing costs. Review your Closing Disclosure before closing, and ask about charges you don’t recognize. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s guide to the Closing Disclosure explains where to find loan costs and other details. Keep this document with your refinance records so you can review the charges with your tax professional.

Track how you use loan proceeds

If your refinance includes cash out, keep records showing how you use the money. Tax treatment may depend on the purpose of the borrowed funds, so don’t assume all points on a cash-out refinance qualify for the same deduction. Save invoices, receipts, contractor agreements, and proof of payment for home improvements. Note which expenses you paid with refinance proceeds, and keep those records with your Closing Disclosure.

TaxAct describes circumstances in which points tied to qualifying improvements to a main home may receive different treatment in its guidance on mortgage points and home improvements. Frequently Asked Questions

Are all fees called “points” on a refinance tax-deductible?
No. A charge generally needs to represent prepaid interest and meet IRS requirements to qualify. Origination fees and charges for services such as appraisals or title work usually aren’t deductible as mortgage points. Check your itemized closing documents, and ask your lender to clarify any charge you don’t recognize.

Do I have to itemize deductions to claim refinance points?
Generally, yes. If you take the standard deduction, you typically can’t claim mortgage points separately. Other rules, including limits on mortgage interest and whether the property qualifies as a residence, may also affect eligibility.

Can I deduct points on a cash-out refinance?
It depends on how you use the cash-out funds. Points tied to money used for qualifying home improvements may receive different treatment from points associated with funds used for personal expenses. Keep receipts and payment records, and ask a tax professional how to allocate the points.

What if I refinance again or pay off the loan early?
The treatment of points you haven’t yet deducted can depend on whether you refinance with the same lender, use a different lender, or pay off the mortgage. Keep records of points already claimed and ask a tax professional how to handle the remaining amount.

What documents should I keep for tax purposes?
Save your Closing Disclosure, proof of payment, Form 1098, mortgage statements, and copies of returns where you reported points. If you used cash-out funds for improvements, keep invoices and receipts that show how the money was spent.
