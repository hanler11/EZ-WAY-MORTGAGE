What Is a Rate-and-Term Refinance, and How Does It Work?

A rate-and-term refinance replaces your current mortgage with a new loan. Rather than taking cash from your home equity, you change one or more features of your existing financing, such as the interest rate, repayment term, loan type, or payment structure.

Homeowners often choose this option to lower their interest rate, reduce their monthly payment, shorten the loan term, or replace an adjustable-rate mortgage with a fixed-rate mortgage. The right choice depends on your remaining balance, home value, credit profile, income, loan type, closing costs, and how long you plan to keep the property. A lower advertised rate does not automatically mean refinancing will save you money. Closing costs, the length of your new loan, and the time you expect to stay in the home all affect the result. Replace your mortgage with a new loan

When you refinance, the new mortgage pays off your existing loan. You then make payments on the new mortgage according to its interest rate, repayment term, and balance. For example, you could replace a 30-year mortgage with another 30-year loan at a lower rate, or select a 15-year term to repay the balance sooner.

Refinancing does not erase your mortgage debt. It replaces the original loan agreement with a new one, and your new balance may include eligible closing costs if you choose to finance them. Review the loan estimate for the amount borrowed, projected payment, interest rate, and estimated cash required at closing.

Since refinancing creates a new mortgage, the lender will review your finances and property again. EZ Way Mortgage can help you compare refinance programs based on your current loan, home equity, income, and financial goals.

Lower your rate or change your term

A lower interest rate may reduce the interest charged on your remaining balance. The actual savings depend on how much you owe, the new loan’s costs, and how long you keep the mortgage. If the closing costs are high, it may take several years for the monthly savings to outweigh the upfront expense.

You can also change the repayment term. A shorter term may increase your monthly payment, but it can help you pay off the mortgage sooner and reduce total interest. A longer term may lower the monthly payment by spreading the balance across more payments, though you could pay interest for additional years.

Ask for a comparison that shows the monthly payment, total interest, closing costs, and estimated break-even point. CNBC’s overview of mortgage refinancing explains why both the interest rate and repayment schedule deserve attention.

Switch from an ARM to a fixed-rate mortgage

A rate-and-term refinance can replace an adjustable-rate mortgage, or ARM, with a fixed-rate mortgage. An ARM may start with an attractive introductory rate, but the rate can change later according to the loan’s adjustment schedule and market conditions. As a result, future payments may be difficult to predict.

A fixed-rate mortgage keeps the same interest rate throughout the loan term, subject to the terms of the agreement. That consistency can make household budgeting easier, particularly if you plan to stay in your home for several years. The tradeoff is that the new fixed rate may be higher than your ARM’s initial rate.

Before refinancing, review when your ARM can adjust, how frequently changes can occur, and the maximum rate increase allowed. Compare the projected ARM payments with the payment on a new fixed-rate loan. PNC’s explanation of rate-and-term refinancing offers additional information about making this type of change.

Lower payments or repay the loan sooner

Your main goal may be lowering your monthly payment, paying off the mortgage faster, or finding a balance between the two. Extending the loan term can reduce the monthly amount because you spread the balance across more payments. However, a longer schedule may increase the total interest you pay.

A shorter term works in the opposite way. The monthly payment may be higher because you repay the balance over fewer years, but you can build equity faster and potentially pay less interest overall. The best choice depends on your income, budget, savings, and plans for the home.

Ask the lender to provide side-by-side estimates for different terms. If you expect to move within a few years, a lower payment may not recover the refinance costs before you sell. If you plan to stay long term, a shorter term may better support your payoff goals.

Remove mortgage insurance with enough equity

Refinancing may give you an opportunity to remove private mortgage insurance, or PMI, if you have enough equity and meet the new lender’s requirements. PMI commonly applies to certain conventional loans when the borrower has less than 20% equity, but cancellation and refinance rules vary by loan type and lender.

The lender may order an appraisal or use another approved valuation method to determine your home’s current value. Your loan-to-value ratio, credit history, occupancy, and selected loan program can affect whether you qualify without mortgage insurance. FHA mortgage insurance follows different rules, so refinancing does not automatically remove it.

Removing PMI could lower your monthly payment, but the potential savings should be compared with the cost of refinancing. Request an estimate that includes your current payment, new payment, mortgage insurance, closing costs, and expected break-even date before making a decision.

Account for closing costs and limited cash back

A rate-and-term refinance is intended to change your mortgage terms, not provide a large cash payout. Some loan programs may permit limited cash back at closing for certain adjustments, but lender and program rules apply. If you want to access a significant portion of your home equity, compare a cash-out refinance, HELOC, and home equity loan.

Refinance costs may include the loan origination fee, appraisal, credit report, title services, recording fees, prepaid interest, and escrow adjustments. PNC’s refinance guidance notes that closing costs often fall within a percentage range of the loan amount, though your actual costs depend on the property, lender, and loan program.

You may pay the costs upfront, add eligible expenses to the new loan balance, or accept a lender credit in exchange for a higher interest rate. Each option affects your payment and total cost. Review the loan estimate carefully and ask how each choice changes your break-even point.

Complete the application, appraisal, underwriting, and closing

The process begins with an application and a review of your income, assets, debts, credit history, payment history, and property details. You may need to provide recent pay stubs, bank statements, tax returns, homeowners insurance information, and mortgage statements. Self-employed borrowers may need additional income records, such as business tax returns or profit and loss statements.

The lender may order an appraisal or another property valuation to confirm the home’s value. During underwriting, the lender verifies your documents and checks whether you meet the requirements for the selected loan program. Responding promptly to document requests can help prevent unnecessary delays.

At closing, you sign the new mortgage documents and pay any required funds. The new lender uses the loan proceeds to pay off your existing mortgage, and you begin making payments under the new schedule. Compare the final closing disclosure with the earlier loan estimate and ask about any changes before signing. What Is a Cash-Out Refinance, and How Does It Differ?

A cash-out refinance lets you use some of your home equity without selling your property. You replace your current mortgage with a new, larger loan, use the new loan to pay off your existing mortgage, and receive the remaining amount in cash after closing costs and other deductions.

This differs from a rate-and-term refinance. With a rate-and-term refinance, the primary goal is usually to change your interest rate, loan term, or monthly payment. You typically do not receive a significant amount of cash. A cash-out refinance, on the other hand, increases your mortgage balance so you can access part of the equity you have built.

The right choice depends on your goal, available equity, income, credit profile, and comfort with the new payment. It also depends on how long you plan to keep the home and whether the cash will support a worthwhile financial purpose. Borrow against your home equity

Home equity is the portion of your property’s value that you own outright. To estimate it, subtract your current mortgage balance from your home’s market value. For example, if your home is worth $400,000 and you owe $250,000, you have approximately $150,000 in equity.

A cash-out refinance lets you borrow against some of that equity by taking out a new mortgage for more than you currently owe. However, you generally cannot borrow the full amount of your equity. The lender will apply a maximum loan-to-value, or LTV, limit, which determines how much of the home’s value can be financed.

Your credit score, income, debts, property type, occupancy, and loan program may also affect the amount you qualify for. Since your home secures the new mortgage, borrowing against it requires careful planning. Chase explains the difference between cash-out and rate-and-term refinancing.

Pay off your mortgage and receive the remaining cash

A cash-out refinance replaces your current mortgage instead of adding another payment to it. At closing, the new loan pays off your existing mortgage. The lender then subtracts closing costs, prepaid expenses, and other required charges before sending the remaining funds to you.

For example, suppose your home is worth $300,000 and your current mortgage balance is $200,000. If you qualify for a new $250,000 mortgage, the loan would pay off the original balance and leave approximately $50,000 before closing costs and other deductions.

The amount you receive may be lower than your initial estimate. Your final cash proceeds depend on the property appraisal, mortgage payoff amount, maximum loan limit, closing costs, and lender requirements. Calculate cash after closing costs and loan limits

Your total home equity is not the same as the amount you can take out. To estimate your available cash, start with the lender’s maximum loan amount based on the appraised value and applicable LTV limit. Then subtract your current mortgage payoff, closing costs, prepaid interest, escrow deposits, and any other charges.

Refinancing costs may include an origination fee, appraisal, title services, recording fees, credit report charges, and lender fees. The exact costs vary by lender, loan program, property type, and borrower profile. Some programs also limit how much cash you can receive or require you to leave a certain amount of equity in the home.

Cash-out refinancing may have a higher interest rate and stricter qualification standards than a rate-and-term refinance. Waltz explains these differences, including the possible need for stronger credit and more remaining equity. Ask for a detailed loan estimate before deciding.

Fund renovations, high-interest debt, education, or major expenses

Homeowners often use cash-out refinance proceeds for planned renovations, roof repairs, education costs, medical expenses, or high-interest debt consolidation. Using equity for improvements may make sense when you have a detailed budget and the project supports the home’s condition or long-term value.

Debt consolidation can also reduce the interest rate compared with credit cards or other high-interest accounts. However, it changes the type of debt you carry. Credit card debt is generally unsecured, while a refinanced mortgage is secured by your home. If you cannot make the new mortgage payments, your property could be at risk.

Consider the purpose, cost, and repayment timeline before borrowing. Avoid taking out more than you need, and do not treat home equity as an open-ended spending account. Newrez discusses common uses for cash-out refinancing and why homeowners should review the long-term effects.

Understand how a larger balance affects payments and interest

Taking cash out increases your mortgage balance. Depending on the amount borrowed, interest rate, and loan term, your new monthly payment may increase. Even if the payment remains similar, restarting the loan with a longer term may result in more total interest over time.

For example, a lower interest rate may reduce the cost of borrowing, but adding cash to the principal can offset some of those savings. Choosing a new 30-year term may make the payment more manageable, but it can extend the time you pay interest. A shorter term may reduce total interest, though it usually comes with a higher monthly payment.

Create a repayment plan before using your equity

Decide how you will use the cash before applying. If you are planning a renovation, collect written estimates and include a reserve for unexpected expenses. If you are consolidating debt, create a payoff plan and address the spending habits that led to the balances.

You should also consider how long you expect to keep the home and the new mortgage. Refinancing costs can take time to recover, especially when the new loan includes additional cash. A break-even calculation can help you understand when the savings may outweigh the upfront costs, but a cash-out refinance requires you to evaluate the added debt as well.

Make sure your income can support the new payment if expenses increase or your circumstances change. PNC recommends considering how long you plan to keep a refinanced loan when reviewing refinancing costs and potential savings.

Compare a cash-out refinance with a HELOC or home equity loan

A cash-out refinance replaces your existing first mortgage with a new loan. A home equity loan usually provides a separate lump sum with its own payment, while a home equity line of credit, or HELOC, provides a revolving credit line you can draw from as needed.

A cash-out refinance may be suitable if you want to change the terms of your first mortgage and prefer one monthly mortgage payment. A home equity loan may be useful when you want to keep your current mortgage rate and receive a predictable lump sum. A HELOC can offer more flexibility for expenses that happen over time, although its interest rate may change.

Compare the interest rate, payment, closing costs, repayment period, and total interest for each option. Also consider how much equity will remain after borrowing and whether the payment fits your budget. Rate-and-Term vs. Cash-Out Refinance: Key Differences

Both refinance options replace your current mortgage with a new loan, but they serve different purposes. A rate-and-term refinance focuses on improving the structure of your mortgage, usually by changing the interest rate, loan term, or loan type. A cash-out refinance increases your mortgage balance so you can access part of your home equity as cash.

The best choice depends on your goals, finances, and plans for the property. A homeowner who wants a lower payment or a shorter payoff timeline may benefit from a rate-and-term refinance. Someone planning a major renovation or paying off high-interest debt may consider cash-out refinancing, provided the new payment remains manageable.

Do not compare these options based on the advertised interest rate alone. Your loan balance, property value, credit history, income, debt-to-income ratio, closing costs, expected time in the home, and use of the funds all affect the outcome. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s refinance guidance explains how a cash-out refinance changes the amount of debt secured by your property.

Before choosing, ask how each option would affect your monthly payment, total interest, equity, and financial flexibility. A side-by-side review can show whether refinancing supports your goals or simply moves costs into a new loan.

Compare each refinance’s primary purpose

A rate-and-term refinance changes your mortgage rate, loan term, or both. You generally borrow enough to pay off your existing mortgage and eligible closing costs, without receiving substantial cash from your equity. Homeowners often choose this option to lower their rate, reduce their payment, replace an adjustable-rate mortgage with a fixed-rate loan, or repay the mortgage sooner.

A cash-out refinance serves a different purpose. You replace your existing mortgage with a larger loan and receive the difference in cash after paying off the old loan and closing costs. You might use the funds for home improvements, debt consolidation, education, or another major expense.

The key difference is how the new loan uses your equity. Rate-and-term refinancing primarily changes the mortgage structure, while cash-out refinancing converts some equity into borrowed funds. That cash must be repaid with interest and is secured by your home.

Review the balance, cash received, and remaining equity

Suppose your home is worth $400,000 and your current mortgage balance is $200,000. With a rate-and-term refinance, the new loan may remain close to the existing balance, plus eligible closing costs. Your equity would generally stay similar, although the exact amount depends on the new loan amount and property value.

With a cash-out refinance, the mortgage balance increases so you can receive money from your equity. For example, a new $280,000 loan could pay off the $200,000 mortgage and provide approximately $80,000 before closing costs and other adjustments. The final amount depends on the appraisal, lender guidelines, loan program, and your financial profile.

Cash received through refinancing is not free money. It is debt that you repay over the loan term. Before applying, estimate the amount you need, the cash you would receive after costs, the new monthly payment, and the equity remaining after closing.

Understand LTV and CLTV limits

Loan-to-value, or LTV, compares your first mortgage balance with your home’s appraised value. Combined loan-to-value, or CLTV, includes other debts secured by the property, such as a home equity loan or HELOC. Lenders use these ratios to measure how much of the property is financed and how much equity remains.

For example, a $240,000 mortgage on a home appraised at $400,000 has a 60% LTV. If the property also has a $40,000 HELOC balance, the CLTV calculation includes both debts. A cash-out refinance must generally remain within the applicable LTV or CLTV limit for the loan program.

Limits can vary based on occupancy, property type, credit history, loan program, and lender requirements. The Federal Housing Finance Agency’s refinancing resources provide general information, but your lender will review your specific situation. An appraisal may also change the calculation if the property value differs from your estimate.

Compare rates, payments, terms, and total interest

A cash-out refinance may have different pricing from a rate-and-term refinance because the loan balance increases and the lender assumes additional risk. Still, the interest rate alone does not show which option costs less. Compare the annual percentage rate, monthly payment, closing costs, loan term, and total interest.

The new term matters, too. Refinancing a mortgage with 20 years remaining into a new 30-year loan could lower your payment while extending repayment and increasing total interest. Choosing a shorter term may reduce long-term interest, but it can create a higher monthly payment.

Ask lenders for comparable Loan Estimates. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s Loan Estimate guide explains the document you receive after applying. Review the same figures for each offer, including principal, interest, mortgage insurance, taxes, fees, and cash to close.

Review mortgage insurance and cash-out rules

Mortgage insurance can affect the cost of either refinance. With a conventional loan, private mortgage insurance may apply when the new loan represents a higher percentage of the home’s value. A new appraisal and loan amount can change your mortgage insurance status, even if your current loan does not require it.

Government-backed programs have different rules. FHA loans generally include mortgage insurance premiums, while VA loans follow separate requirements and may include a funding fee. USDA loans also have specific eligibility and guarantee guidelines. Cash-out availability varies by program, occupancy, property type, and borrower qualifications.

Having 20% equity does not guarantee approval or eliminate every fee. Credit, income, payment history, debt-to-income ratio, and lender requirements still matter. Ask how each proposed loan handles mortgage insurance, upfront charges, and ongoing premiums before comparing monthly payments.

Weigh cash access against reduced equity

Cash-out refinancing gives you access to money without selling your home, but it reduces the equity you own. If your new mortgage is larger, you will have less equity immediately after closing than you would with a rate-and-term refinance. That may limit your flexibility if property values fall or you plan to sell soon.

Borrow only what you need. A renovation budget, debt payoff plan, or detailed expense estimate can help you choose a reasonable loan amount. Taking extra cash may increase your payment and total interest without providing a clear financial benefit.

Remember that your home secures the new mortgage. If you cannot make the payments, the loan carries foreclosure risk. Review your income stability, emergency savings, and expected housing costs before using equity for a discretionary expense. Cash access can be useful, but it should fit within a realistic repayment plan.

Understand how debt consolidation secures unsecured debt with your home

A cash-out refinance can replace high-interest credit card or personal loan debt with mortgage debt that may carry a lower rate. It can simplify several payments into one, but it also changes the risk involved. Credit card debt is generally unsecured, while a refinanced mortgage is secured by your home.

A lower rate may not reduce your total interest if you repay the balance over a much longer term. For example, moving a five-year personal loan into a new 30-year mortgage could lower the monthly payment while extending repayment for decades.

Debt consolidation works best when you also address the spending or cash-flow issue that caused the debt. Otherwise, you could pay off your cards, accumulate new balances, and still carry a larger mortgage. The Federal Trade Commission’s debt consolidation guidance offers questions to consider before choosing a debt-relief strategy.

Consider tax treatment based on how you use the cash

Mortgage interest treatment can depend on how you use cash-out proceeds. Under IRS rules, interest on debt used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home securing the loan may qualify for the home mortgage interest deduction, subject to applicable limits and requirements. Funds used for credit cards, tuition, vehicles, or other personal expenses may not receive the same treatment.

Tax treatment may also depend on whether you itemize deductions, when the loan originated, the amount of qualifying mortgage debt, and how you spent the proceeds. Keep records showing where the money went, especially if you use the funds for a home improvement project.

Do not assume a lower mortgage rate automatically creates a tax benefit. Review the IRS guidance on home mortgage interest, then ask a qualified tax professional how the refinance may affect your return. A mortgage professional can explain loan costs and structure, but tax advice should come from a tax adviser.

Correct myths about the 1% rule, automatic savings, and 20% equity

There is no universal rule requiring your new mortgage rate to be 1% lower before refinancing makes sense. A smaller reduction may still work if you have a large balance, plan to keep the home for several years, or can recover the closing costs quickly. However, even a significant reduction may not help if fees are high or you plan to move soon.

Refinancing also does not automatically reduce your total cost. A longer loan term, prepaid interest, points, closing costs, or a larger balance can offset monthly payment savings. Compare your current loan with the proposed loan over the period you expect to own the property.

Finally, 20% equity is not a universal requirement for every refinance. Program and lender guidelines vary, and some borrowers may qualify with less equity. Approval still depends on the complete application, including credit, income, property value, occupancy, and debt obligations.

Compare the numbers, not just the advertised rate

Start with the complete cost of each option. Compare the interest rate, annual percentage rate, monthly principal and interest payment, mortgage insurance, closing costs, prepaid items, cash received, and total loan balance. For a cash-out refinance, include the amount of equity you use and the purpose of the proceeds.

You can estimate a basic break-even point by dividing refinance costs by your expected monthly savings. For example, $6,000 in closing costs divided by $250 in monthly savings equals 24 months. This estimate does not account for moving, taxes, insurance changes, or additional interest from extending the loan term.

Your plans also matter. If you expect to move within a few years, significant upfront costs may not be worthwhile. If you plan to stay, a carefully structured refinance may support your long-term goals. Refinancing Costs and Risks to Compare

Refinancing can change your interest rate, monthly payment, loan term, or access to home equity. It also creates a new mortgage transaction, so you may pay many of the same costs you paid when you purchased your home. A lower advertised rate does not necessarily mean refinancing will save money. Compare the full loan offer, including upfront fees, recurring payments, total interest, and the equity you will retain.

Ask each lender for a detailed breakdown of the proposed loan. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s refinance guidance explains that refinancing replaces your current mortgage with a new one. That new loan should support a clear goal, such as lowering your rate, changing your term, replacing an adjustable-rate mortgage, or accessing equity for a planned expense.

Your expected time in the home also matters. If you move before recovering the refinance costs, a lower payment may not create meaningful savings. A mortgage professional can compare rate-and-term and cash-out options using your balance, property value, income, debts, and long-term plans.

Estimate origination, appraisal, title, recording, and lender fees

A refinance typically includes lender and third-party fees. Common charges include origination, underwriting, processing, credit report, appraisal, title search, title insurance, settlement, and county recording fees. Some lenders may also charge administrative, verification, or document preparation fees. The total depends on the lender, loan type, property, and location.

Review the Loan Estimate carefully after applying. This document presents estimated costs in a standard format, making it easier to compare offers. Pay attention to both total closing costs and services you can shop for, such as title and settlement services. Ask the lender to explain any unfamiliar fee before you proceed.

A new appraisal may be required, especially for a cash-out refinance. If the property value is lower than expected, you may qualify for less favorable terms or receive less cash. Confirm how the lender handles appraisal costs if the loan does not close.

Include points, prepaid interest, escrow, and other closing costs

Points are upfront charges paid to reduce the mortgage interest rate. One point generally equals 1% of the loan amount, but the rate reduction varies by lender and market conditions. Points may make sense if you plan to keep the loan long enough to recover the upfront cost. They may be less useful if you expect to move or refinance again soon.

Prepaid interest covers the interest that accrues between closing and the date covered by your first mortgage payment. Your closing documents may also include initial escrow deposits for property taxes and homeowners insurance. These deposits are not lender fees, but they still increase the cash you need at closing.

Ask whether your current escrow balance will be refunded after your existing loan is paid off. The timing can vary by servicer. The CFPB’s closing cost overview explains how prepaid items, escrow deposits, and other settlement charges appear in the transaction.

Understand no-closing-cost refinance tradeoffs

A no-closing-cost refinance does not mean the transaction is free. The lender may cover some costs by charging a higher interest rate, adding the expenses to your loan balance, or offering lender credits. Each choice affects your monthly payment, total interest, or available equity.

A higher rate can reduce your upfront expense but increase the amount you pay over the life of the loan. Adding closing costs to the balance preserves cash at closing, but it increases your debt and may reduce your equity. Lender credits can offset certain charges, though they may also come with a higher rate.

Ask for two versions of the loan offer: one with the lowest available rate and another with credits or reduced upfront costs. Compare the cash required at closing, monthly payment, five-year cost, and total interest. Looking at these figures together gives you a clearer view than relying on the phrase “no closing costs.”

Since the new loan amount is higher, the rate and fees may differ from those for a rate-and-term refinance. If you plan to use the funds for renovations or debt repayment, create a written budget before applying. Include points, lender credits, upfront fees, and changes in your monthly escrow payment. If the new loan lowers principal and interest but your taxes or insurance increase, your actual savings may be smaller than expected. For a cash-out refinance, calculate the cost of accessing the funds separately from any change in payment.

Compare the break-even period with how long you expect to keep the home and the new loan. If you may sell before reaching that point, refinancing may not provide enough time to recover the expense. Weigh lower payments against longer terms and added interest

Assess higher payments, lower equity, and foreclosure risk

Even when the payment remains manageable, taking cash from your home reduces the equity available for future needs, selling costs, or financial emergencies.

Debt consolidation requires particular care. Paying off credit cards with mortgage proceeds may reduce the interest rate, but it changes unsecured debt into debt secured by your home. Review how a new loan changes your payoff timeline

Refinancing pays off your existing mortgage and begins a new amortization schedule. You may also pay more interest at the beginning of the new loan because early payments generally apply more toward interest.

What Are the Eligibility Requirements for Each Refinance?

Eligibility for a rate-and-term or cash-out refinance depends on more than your current interest rate. Lenders review your credit, payment history, income, debts, home equity, property, and financial reserves before deciding which programs you may qualify for. A cash-out refinance can involve additional requirements because you are replacing your current mortgage with a larger loan and withdrawing part of your home equity.

Requirements vary by loan type and lender. For example, one lender may approve a borrower with a higher debt-to-income ratio, while another may require more reserves or a stronger credit history. Your occupancy status, property type, loan amount, and reason for refinancing can also affect the available terms.

Before applying, gather your financial documents and review your credit profile. Meet credit, payment history, and debt-to-income requirements

Lenders typically review your credit score, recent payment history, and debt-to-income ratio, or DTI. A score around 620 may meet the starting point for some conventional refinance programs, but minimum requirements vary by lender, loan type, property, and other parts of your application. A stronger credit profile may give you access to more options or more favorable terms.

Your payment history also matters. Recent late mortgage payments, missed credit card payments, collection accounts, or other serious delinquencies can make approval more difficult. Lenders also compare your monthly debts with your gross monthly income to determine whether the new mortgage payment is manageable.

Before applying, check your credit reports for errors and avoid taking on new debt if possible. Do not assume that a qualifying score guarantees approval. Lenders evaluate your entire application, including your income stability, equity, assets, and proposed loan amount.

Document employment, self-employment, income, assets, and reserves

Meet equity, LTV, CLTV, and cash-out limits

Your home equity affects whether you qualify and how much you can borrow. Loan-to-value, or LTV, compares your mortgage balance with your home’s appraised value. Combined loan-to-value, or CLTV, includes other debts secured by the property, such as a home equity loan or HELOC.

A rate-and-term refinance generally replaces your current mortgage without providing significant cash back. A cash-out refinance pays off your existing mortgage and allows you to borrow additional funds, so lenders often require more remaining equity. Maximum LTV and cash-out limits depend on the lender, loan program, occupancy, property type, and borrower profile.

The value of your home and the size of your new loan also determine whether you remain within conforming loan limits. Review the current conforming loan limits published by the FHFA when comparing conventional refinance options. An appraisal, existing liens, and the amount of cash you request can all change the final calculation.

Complete an appraisal and meet property condition standards

Many refinances require an appraisal or another valuation method. The valuation helps the lender estimate your home’s current market value, which affects your LTV, CLTV, available cash, and loan eligibility. Depending on the program and your financial profile, the lender may use an automated valuation or approve an appraisal waiver instead of ordering a traditional appraisal.

Your property must also meet the lender’s condition standards. Safety concerns, structural problems, roof damage, faulty utilities, or unfinished repairs can delay underwriting or affect approval. Government-backed programs may have additional requirements for the property and appraisal. An appraisal does not guarantee approval. The lender still reviews your credit, income, debts, title, insurance, and other documentation. If repairs are required, ask whether they must be completed before closing or can be addressed through another approved solution.

Review occupancy, property type, and lender guidelines

Lenders consider how you use the property. A primary residence may have different requirements from a second home or investment property. Investment properties often involve higher rates, larger reserve requirements, and stricter LTV limits because rental income and occupancy can create additional risk.

When Should You Choose Rate-and-Term vs. Cash-Out Refinance?

The right refinance depends on what you want the new mortgage to accomplish. A rate-and-term refinance changes your interest rate, loan term, or both, without taking substantial cash from your home equity. A cash-out refinance replaces your current mortgage with a larger loan and gives you the difference in cash after closing costs and other requirements.

Start with a specific goal. You may want to lower your monthly payment, repay your mortgage sooner, replace an adjustable-rate mortgage with predictable payments, or access funds for a planned expense. Your goal can help narrow the choice, but you still need to compare the new payment, total interest, closing costs, loan balance, and remaining equity. As Newrez explains, refinancing should reflect your financial situation and goals instead of relying on market timing alone.

A mortgage professional can compare both options using your current loan details, income, debts, credit profile, and estimated property value. Choose rate-and-term to lower your rate or payment after reviewing costs

A rate-and-term refinance may make sense when you can qualify for a lower interest rate or change the loan term to create a payment that better fits your budget. The new mortgage pays off your existing loan, but you generally do not receive substantial cash from the transaction.

A lower rate may reduce your monthly principal and interest payment. However, refinancing involves costs such as lender charges, appraisal fees, title services, and recording expenses. Compare those costs with your expected savings before applying. If you plan to move soon, you may not remain in the home long enough to recover the upfront expense. CNBC’s overview of refinancing options explains how changing the rate or repayment length can support different homeowner goals.

Choose rate-and-term to shorten the loan and reduce interest

If your income has increased or your financial priorities have changed, you may consider a shorter mortgage term. Moving from a 30-year loan to a 15-year loan, for example, can help you repay the balance sooner and may reduce the total interest paid over the life of the mortgage.

The tradeoff is usually a higher monthly payment, even when the new interest rate is lower. Review your emergency savings, monthly obligations, retirement contributions, and other financial goals before committing. You should be comfortable with the payment during both strong and difficult months. Refinancing can also restart the amortization schedule, so reviewing the effect of a new term can help you understand how the payment is divided between principal and interest.

Choose rate-and-term to replace an ARM with predictable payments

A rate-and-term refinance can help you replace an adjustable-rate mortgage, or ARM, with a fixed-rate mortgage. An ARM may begin with a lower rate, but its rate and payment can change after the introductory period according to the loan’s terms and market conditions.

A fixed-rate mortgage provides a more consistent principal and interest payment, which can make household budgeting easier. This option may appeal to homeowners who plan to stay in the property, prefer payment stability, or are concerned about future increases. Compare the new rate, loan term, closing costs, and any prepayment terms before deciding. PNC’s explanation of rate-and-term refinancing offers additional context on switching from an ARM to a fixed-rate loan.

Choose rate-and-term when preserving equity matters most

Choose rate-and-term refinancing when your priority is improving your mortgage without taking money from your home. Since you are not borrowing a larger amount for cash, you may retain more of the ownership stake you have built over time.

Preserving equity can matter if you expect to sell, want to maintain financial flexibility, or may need access to equity later. It can also provide more protection if property values decline, because a lower loan balance relative to the home’s value leaves you with more ownership in the property. A rate-and-term refinance does not provide cash, but it may save money through a lower rate or shorter term, as Chase notes in its refinance comparison. Make sure the expected savings justify the closing costs.

Choose cash-out for planned renovations and a clear budget

A cash-out refinance may fit a renovation project when you have a detailed scope of work, reliable contractor estimates, and a realistic repayment plan. The new mortgage pays off your existing loan, and you receive part of your available equity as cash, subject to lender guidelines, property value, and loan limits.

Use the funds for improvements that support your financial plan, such as repairing a roof, updating a kitchen, or adding space for a growing family. Avoid borrowing more than the project requires. Create a budget that includes materials, labor, permits, and a reasonable reserve for unexpected costs. Newrez identifies home improvements as a common reason for using cash-out refinancing, but you should still review the new payment, interest rate, total debt, and remaining equity before moving forward.

Choose cash-out for high-interest debt with a disciplined payoff plan

Cash-out refinancing may help consolidate high-interest debt when the new mortgage payment remains manageable and you have a plan to avoid rebuilding the balances. Credit cards and some personal loans may carry higher rates than a mortgage, so replacing several payments with one mortgage payment could reduce your monthly interest expense.

This strategy changes the type of debt you carry. Credit card debt is usually unsecured, while a mortgage is secured by your home. If you use equity to pay off other balances, you put your property at risk if you later cannot make the mortgage payments. Navy Federal explains cash-out refinancing for debt consolidation, but homeowners should compare the long-term interest cost and create a spending plan before applying. Paying off cards without changing the habits that created the balances may leave you with both a larger mortgage and new unsecured debt.

Consider cash-out for major expenses when payments remain manageable

Cash-out refinancing can provide funds for a significant expense, such as education, a business investment, or a major family need. Depending on your equity, income, credit profile, and lender guidelines, it may provide access to more money than some other borrowing options.

The key question is whether the new mortgage payment fits comfortably within your budget. A larger loan balance can increase your monthly payment, total interest, and repayment timeline. It also reduces the equity available to you in the future. Review the amount you need, the expected benefit, and the full cost of borrowing. Fannie Mae’s cash-out refinance guidance reflects the importance of evaluating the increased mortgage balance and total debt before completing the transaction.

Avoid cash-out when income is uncertain or no repayment plan exists

Cash-out refinancing may be a poor fit when your income is unstable, your current budget is tight, or you are using home equity to cover recurring expenses. The cash may solve an immediate problem, but the larger mortgage payment can create additional pressure for many years.

Be cautious if you cannot explain how you will repay the new loan or if you are relying on a future raise, bonus, home sale, or other uncertain event. You should also reconsider the strategy if the cash would fund spending without a clear financial purpose. The Center for Responsible Lending’s research on cash-out refinancing highlights the potential long-term harm when homeowners take on additional mortgage debt without enough room in their budgets. A smaller loan or different financing option may be more appropriate.

Compare refinancing with a HELOC, home equity loan, or other financing

A cash-out refinance is not the only way to access your home equity. A home equity loan may provide a separate lump sum with its own fixed rate and payment. A home equity line of credit, or HELOC, may offer a revolving credit line that you can draw from as needed, often with a variable rate.

Each option affects your payment, interest cost, loan terms, and available equity differently. A cash-out refinance replaces your existing first mortgage, while a home equity loan or HELOC is generally an additional lien. Replacing a low-rate first mortgage with a higher-rate cash-out loan may not make sense, even if you need access to funds. Compare these choices with personal loans or other financing when appropriate. Review the rates, fees, repayment schedules, and risks of each option. Navy Federal recommends comparing loan options from multiple lenders before selecting a way to use your equity.

Match the refinance to a specific goal, not just a lower rate

A lower advertised rate does not automatically make refinancing worthwhile. The new loan may have higher closing costs, a longer repayment period, or a larger balance. In some cases, a lower payment results from restarting the loan over a longer term, which may increase the total interest you pay.

Write down your goal and compare the numbers against your current mortgage. If you want to reduce your payment, calculate the monthly savings and break-even point. If you want cash, calculate the amount you will receive after costs and the effect on your equity. If you want predictable payments, compare fixed-rate options with your current ARM terms.
