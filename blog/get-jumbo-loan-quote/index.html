Compare Jumbo Loans and Conventional Mortgages

When you request a mortgage quote in New Jersey, one of the first questions is whether your loan will be conforming or jumbo. The answer can affect your lender options, down payment, required reserves, documentation, interest rate, and closing costs.

A conventional mortgage is not automatically a conforming mortgage. “Conventional” generally means the loan is not insured or guaranteed by the federal government. “Conforming” means the loan meets the guidelines and loan limits used for mortgages that may be purchased by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac. A jumbo mortgage falls outside those requirements, often because the loan amount exceeds the limit set by the Federal Housing Finance Agency.

That distinction matters when you compare quotes. A jumbo loan may be appropriate for a higher-priced property, but it may require more detailed financial records and larger reserves than a conforming loan. A licensed mortgage broker can compare both options and explain how each one affects your monthly payment, cash to close, and long-term costs.

What Makes a Mortgage Jumbo or Nonconforming?

A jumbo mortgage exceeds the conforming loan limit for the property’s location and unit type. Because it falls outside the guidelines used by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, it is also called a nonconforming loan. The lender takes on more responsibility when evaluating and funding the mortgage, so requirements can vary more between lenders.

A loan can be jumbo even when the property is a standard single-family home. For example, a home in a higher-cost New Jersey market may require financing above the local conforming limit. It does not need to be a luxury property or include unusual features.

A conventional loan, meanwhile, can be either conforming or nonconforming. That is why asking whether a mortgage is “conventional” does not tell you enough. Ask about the loan amount, applicable county limit, down payment, reserve requirements, and underwriting guidelines. Why the Loan Amount, Not Home Price, Sets the Category

The purchase price does not determine whether a mortgage is jumbo. The loan amount does. If you buy a $1 million home and make a large enough down payment to keep the mortgage below the applicable conforming limit, your loan may remain conforming. If you buy a less expensive home but borrow more than the limit, the mortgage may be jumbo.

For example, assume the applicable conforming limit is $832,750. A $1 million purchase with a $200,000 down payment would create an $800,000 loan, which may fit within the limit. A $900,000 purchase with a $50,000 down payment would require an $850,000 mortgage, which may be jumbo.

The exact classification depends on the county, number of units, property type, and current loan limits. A larger down payment may give you more financing choices, so ask your broker to compare different down payment scenarios. Compare Jumbo, Super-Jumbo, and Other High-Value Loans

“Jumbo” is a broad category. Some lenders divide it into pricing or underwriting tiers based on the loan amount. A mortgage that is slightly above the conforming limit may receive different terms from a loan several million dollars above it.

Some lenders use super jumbo to describe very large loan balances. However, the term has no single nationwide definition. In some lower-cost markets, it may describe loans of around $1.5 million to $2 million or more. In expensive markets, it may refer to loans above $3 million. The label matters less than the lender’s actual requirements.

High-value financing may also include portfolio loans, private banking programs, asset-based loans, and other customized products. These options can be useful for self-employed borrowers, investors, or clients with substantial assets and complex income. Compare the interest rate, fees, reserves, property restrictions, and repayment terms instead of relying on the loan name alone. How Jumbo Underwriting Differs

Jumbo underwriting usually includes a detailed review of your income, credit, debts, assets, and property. The lender wants to confirm that you can afford the full payment while maintaining enough reserves after closing. Depending on the program, you may need two years of federal tax returns, W-2s or 1099s, recent pay stubs, and two to three months of bank or investment statements.

Self-employed borrowers may also provide business tax returns, profit-and-loss statements, balance sheets, and records showing stable income. Lenders may review bonus income, commissions, restricted stock, rental income, trusts, retirement accounts, and large deposits. Gift funds or proceeds from another property must usually be documented.

The property itself may receive additional attention. The lender can review the appraisal, condition, insurance, marketability, and intended occupancy. A primary residence, second home, and investment property may each have different requirements. Clarify Common Jumbo Loan Misconceptions

One common misconception is that jumbo loans are only for wealthy borrowers or luxury properties. A jumbo mortgage may help a family finance a primary residence in a higher-cost area or purchase a home that falls just above the local conforming limit. The loan amount and borrower profile matter more than the property’s image.

Another misconception is that jumbo loans are always much harder to obtain. The review may be more thorough, but borrowers with strong credit, steady income, manageable debts, sufficient reserves, and an eligible property may qualify. Some lenders also offer programs for self-employed borrowers and clients with nontraditional income.

Jumbo loans are not automatically more expensive, either. Rates, fees, down payment requirements, and reserve rules vary by lender and loan structure. How Do Conforming Limits Affect Jumbo Eligibility?

Conforming loan limits help determine whether your New Jersey mortgage follows standard guidelines or falls into the jumbo category. A conforming loan stays within the maximum amount that Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac can purchase or guarantee. A jumbo loan exceeds that limit and is evaluated under a lender’s own requirements.

The applicable limit depends on more than the state. Lenders also consider the property’s county, number of units, occupancy, and loan purpose. A one-unit primary residence, for example, may have a different limit from a two-unit investment property.

The loan amount, not the home’s purchase price, determines the category. A $1 million home with a $200,000 down payment requires an $800,000 mortgage, which may remain conforming in some areas. The same home with a $100,000 down payment requires a $900,000 loan, which could be jumbo depending on the county and property details.

Review Baseline and High-Cost-Area Limits: $832,750 and $1,249,125

For 2026, the baseline conforming loan limit for a one-unit home in most of the continental United States is $832,750. In designated high-cost areas, the one-unit limit can reach $1,249,125. These amounts are useful reference points, but they do not apply automatically to every property in New Jersey.

The baseline limits also vary by the number of units. For 2026, they are $832,750 for a one-unit property, $1,066,250 for a two-unit property, $1,288,800 for a three-unit property, and $1,601,750 for a four-unit property. You can review these figures in this overview of 2026 conforming loan limits.

If your requested mortgage exceeds the applicable limit, the loan may be classified as jumbo. The final determination requires a county-specific review.

Check County, Unit, Property, and Occupancy Limits

Conforming limits are assigned by county, not only by state. A loan amount that is conforming in one New Jersey county could be jumbo in another. Start by identifying the property’s county and confirming whether it has one, two, three, or four units.

Occupancy also affects the application. A primary residence, second home, and investment property can have different underwriting requirements, even when the loan amount is identical. Property features may also affect eligibility, including mixed-use space, unusual construction, acreage, or multiple buildings.

Remember that the limit applies to the mortgage amount rather than the purchase price. If you purchase a $1 million home with a $200,000 down payment, your loan amount is $800,000. Depending on the county and property type, that amount may remain within conforming guidelines. A jumbo mortgage calculator can help you estimate the amount you may need to borrow.

Verify the Limit Before Requesting a Quote

Provide the property address, purchase price, estimated down payment, unit count, occupancy type, and loan purpose before requesting a quote. These details help the lender determine whether the mortgage may be conforming, high-balance conforming, or jumbo.

Ask the lender or broker to identify the conforming limit used for the quote. This can explain why two companies provide different rates, fees, reserve requirements, or documentation requests. It also helps prevent an estimate from being based on the wrong loan category.

Loan limits can change from one year to the next. An older preapproval or online estimate may not reflect the current amount for your county. Checking the limit before making an offer or requesting final pricing can provide a more reliable starting point, as noted in this jumbo loan guidance.

Use a Larger Down Payment to Stay Conforming

If your requested loan amount is only slightly above the applicable conforming limit, increasing your down payment may keep the mortgage within conforming guidelines. For example, using additional savings could reduce a $850,000 loan to $832,750 or less, depending on the property’s limit.

This approach may affect your interest rate, reserve requirements, mortgage insurance, appraisal conditions, and overall monthly payment. However, using more cash at closing also leaves you with fewer liquid assets. Jumbo lenders may require substantial post-closing reserves, so consider both the loan category and your remaining financial cushion.

Compare your cash to close, monthly payment, mortgage insurance, and funds remaining after closing. Jumbo loans often require down payments of 10% to 20% or more, although requirements vary by lender and borrower profile, according to this jumbo loan requirements guide.

Understand What Happens When You Exceed the Limit

When your requested mortgage exceeds the applicable conforming limit, it generally becomes a jumbo loan. This does not mean approval is out of reach. It means the lender will review the application under jumbo guidelines rather than standard conforming rules.

You may need stronger credit, a lower debt-to-income ratio, additional reserves, a larger down payment, and more detailed income documentation. The lender may also conduct a closer appraisal and property review. Requirements can vary considerably between lenders, so comparing several programs may be worthwhile.

A jumbo loan can help finance a higher-priced New Jersey property when conforming financing does not cover the amount you need. When comparing options, review the complete payment, cash required at closing, reserve requirements, rate structure, and lender conditions, not just the advertised interest rate.

What Do You Need to Qualify for a Jumbo Loan?

Jumbo loans finance properties that exceed the conforming loan limits established by the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA). Because these loans do not follow standard Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac guidelines, lenders usually review the borrower, property, and loan structure more closely. Requirements can differ based on the lender, loan amount, occupancy, property type, and overall financial profile.

A strong application generally includes solid credit, manageable debt, dependable income, substantial assets, and enough cash for the down payment and closing costs. You may also need reserves, which are funds that remain available after closing to cover future mortgage payments. Before requesting a quote, review your finances and gather documents that show how you earn, save, and manage debt. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s mortgage guidance provides helpful information about the application and underwriting process.

Review Your Credit Score, Payment History, and Credit Profile

Your credit score is an important part of a jumbo loan application, but it is not the only factor lenders consider. They may also review your payment history, account balances, length of credit history, recent inquiries, collections, charge-offs, bankruptcies, and foreclosures. A consistent record of on-time payments can support your application, while recent late payments may require an explanation.

Many jumbo lenders expect stronger credit than some conforming loan programs require. However, there is no single minimum score for every jumbo loan. Lenders may consider your complete profile, including your assets, income, LTV, and property type. Before applying, check your credit reports for errors and avoid opening new accounts or making large purchases. You can request your reports through AnnualCreditReport.com.

Meet Down Payment, Loan-to-Value, and Equity Requirements

A larger down payment can strengthen a jumbo loan application by lowering your loan-to-value ratio, or LTV. This ratio compares the loan amount with the property’s value. For example, a $1 million home financed with an $800,000 loan has an 80% LTV.

Some lenders may accept a 10% down payment, while others may require 20% or more. The requirement can depend on the loan amount, credit profile, property type, occupancy, and whether you are purchasing or refinancing. A lower LTV may also affect your interest rate, reserve requirements, and overall approval terms.

Keep in mind that not every dollar in your accounts may be available for the down payment. Lenders must verify the source and availability of your funds. They may also review deposits, transfers, gift funds, and proceeds from another property. Ask how your assets will be counted before moving money between accounts.

Show You Can Afford the Full Payment

Lenders evaluate whether your income can support the proposed housing payment and your other monthly obligations. The housing payment may include principal, interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance when applicable, and homeowners association dues. In New Jersey, property taxes and insurance can have a noticeable effect on the total monthly cost.

Your lender may calculate your debt-to-income ratio, or DTI, by comparing your recurring monthly debts with your qualifying gross income. Jumbo lenders often apply stricter DTI standards or additional underwriting requirements, particularly for larger loan amounts. A mortgage calculator can help you estimate affordability, but it cannot confirm how a lender will treat your income or expenses.

Review Chase’s jumbo mortgage guidance to learn more about the financial factors lenders commonly consider. A mortgage professional can then assess your individual situation.

Document Your Employment and Income

Most borrowers must provide documentation that verifies their employment and income. Depending on your situation, this may include recent pay stubs, W-2 forms, federal tax returns, employment information, and permission for the lender to contact your employer. Lenders typically want to see stable income that is likely to continue.

If you recently changed employers, moved from salaried work to commission, or accepted a position with variable compensation, the lender may request more information. Employment gaps do not automatically prevent approval, but you should be ready to explain them clearly.

Keep your documents complete and consistent. Tell your mortgage professional about employment changes before submitting an application, rather than waiting for underwriting to discover them. Missing or conflicting information can delay the review and lead to additional document requests. If you receive income from more than one source, ask which records the lender will need for each type.

Document Self-Employed, Commission, Bonus, Rental, and Investment Income

Income that does not come from a standard salary often requires a more detailed review. Self-employed borrowers may need to provide personal and business tax returns, year-to-date profit-and-loss statements, business bank statements, and proof that the business remains active. The lender may adjust qualifying income based on business expenses, depreciation, ownership percentage, and recent profitability.

Commission, bonus, overtime, rental, dividend, interest, and investment income may also require a documented history before the lender can count it. Rental income may require leases, tax returns, or a market rent schedule. Investment income may depend on account statements and the type of assets involved.

The exact calculation varies by lender and loan program. Organize these records early, especially if your income changes from year to year. A clear record can help explain income that does not appear as a simple salary on a pay stub.

Verify Liquid Assets, Reserves, and Funding Sources

Jumbo lenders commonly review your assets to confirm that you can cover the down payment, closing costs, and required reserves. Liquid assets may include checking and savings accounts, taxable brokerage accounts, and certain vested retirement funds. The lender may request statements covering a specific period and ask you to explain large deposits or transfers.

Reserves are funds that remain available after closing to cover future mortgage payments. Requirements may be based on several months of principal, interest, taxes, and insurance. The amount can vary according to the lender, loan size, property type, and financial profile.

Gift funds, trust assets, borrowed funds, and proceeds from another property may have special documentation requirements. Before transferring money, ask how each source will be treated. Keeping funds in clearly documented accounts can make the asset review easier and reduce avoidable questions during underwriting.

Account for Existing Debts and Recurring Obligations

Your lender will review recurring obligations when calculating affordability. These may include credit card payments, auto loans, student loans, personal loans, alimony, child support, other mortgages, and co-signed debts. Even if another person makes the payments on a debt, you may need to provide documentation before the lender excludes it from your DTI calculation.

Do not leave out debts because they do not appear on a credit report or because you expect to pay them off later. Explain your plans and provide proof when requested. The lender may also ask about pending loans, new accounts, or large purchases during the application.

Avoid taking on new debt before closing unless your mortgage professional confirms that it will not affect your qualification or loan terms. A new car loan, credit card balance, or personal loan can change your monthly obligations and require the lender to reassess your application.

Meet Property, Appraisal, Condition, and Occupancy Requirements

The property must meet the lender’s standards as well as support the requested loan amount. An appraisal helps estimate the home’s market value and confirms that the property provides suitable collateral. If the appraisal is lower than the purchase price, you may need to renegotiate, bring additional cash to closing, or reconsider the transaction.

Lenders may also review the property’s condition, marketability, access, insurance availability, and intended use. Unique homes, large parcels, mixed-use properties, condos, co-ops, and homes with unusual features may receive additional scrutiny.

The lender will confirm whether the home is your primary residence, second home, or investment property. An appraisal is not a substitute for a home inspection, so consider arranging an independent inspection before completing the purchase. Understanding the property’s condition can help you make a more informed decision about the loan and the home.

Compare Requirements for Primary, Second, and Investment Homes

Occupancy affects jumbo loan eligibility, pricing, down payment requirements, and documentation. A primary residence is the home you occupy most of the time. A second home must generally meet specific use and location requirements. An investment property is purchased or held primarily to generate rental income and often receives more conservative underwriting treatment.

Second homes and investment properties may require a larger down payment, stronger reserves, lower LTV, or additional financial documentation. Rental income may not be counted in full, and projected rent may not qualify without supporting records.

Be accurate about how you plan to use the property. Misrepresenting occupancy can create serious financial and legal problems. Discuss the intended use with your mortgage broker before requesting quotes so each lender evaluates and prices the same scenario. This is especially important when comparing a vacation home with a property you plan to rent.

Meet Purchase, Rate-and-Term Refinance, and Cash-Out Requirements

The purpose of the loan affects the underwriting process. For a purchase, the lender reviews the contract, down payment, property value, and funds needed to close. You may also need to document the source of your down payment and confirm that the property meets the lender’s occupancy and condition standards.

A rate-and-term refinance replaces the existing mortgage without taking substantial cash out. The lender will still review your income, debts, assets, property value, and current loan payment history. A cash-out refinance increases the new loan balance and gives you proceeds from your home equity. It may involve stricter LTV, reserve, seasoning, and credit requirements.

Compare Fixed-Rate and Adjustable-Rate Jumbo Loans

A fixed-rate jumbo mortgage keeps the same interest rate for the entire loan term. This creates a predictable principal and interest payment, which can make long-term budgeting easier when purchasing a high-value home.

An adjustable-rate mortgage, or ARM, may start with a lower rate than a fixed-rate option. After the initial fixed period, the rate can change according to the loan’s index, margin, adjustment schedule, and rate caps. Some borrowers choose an ARM when they expect to move, refinance, or pay down the loan before the first adjustment.

An ARM may reduce short-term interest costs, but future payments could rise. Ask for the initial payment, possible adjusted payments, and maximum payment under the loan terms. A jumbo mortgage calculator can help you compare different payment structures.

Understand How Borrower, Property, and Loan Structure Affect Pricing

Your jumbo loan quote is based on several parts of your financial profile. The lender may review your credit score, payment history, debt-to-income ratio, employment history, income stability, liquid assets, and post-closing reserves. A strong application may qualify for more favorable terms, but each lender evaluates risk differently.

The property also affects pricing and approval requirements. A primary residence may receive different terms from a second home, investment property, condominium, co-op, or multi-unit home. Loan structure matters too, including the amount borrowed, down payment, term, fixed or adjustable rate, cash-out amount, and whether the loan is for a purchase or refinance.

Jumbo loans commonly require a larger down payment, stronger credit, and more detailed underwriting than many conforming loans. NerdWallet’s jumbo loan guide explains how down payment, credit, and loan-to-value can affect qualification.

Compare Interest Rate, APR, Term, and Payment Assumptions

When reviewing a quote, identify the interest rate, APR, loan term, estimated monthly payment, and date the pricing was issued. A 30-year fixed-rate loan typically has a different payment and total interest cost than a 15-year fixed-rate loan. An ARM quote should show the initial fixed period and the terms that apply after it ends.

Ask whether the estimated payment includes property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, and HOA dues. Some estimates show only principal and interest, while others include the full housing payment. The quote may also assume a particular purchase price, down payment, credit profile, or closing date.

The U.S. Bank jumbo mortgage calculator explains that estimates may rely on assumptions about the property, closing costs, lender fees, and other expenses. Use these tools for planning, then request a lender-prepared estimate for more specific figures.

Review Discount Points, Origination Fees, and Lender Credits

Discount points are upfront fees paid to reduce the interest rate. One point generally equals 1% of the loan amount, although the rate reduction may vary by lender and market conditions. Points may be worthwhile if you expect to keep the loan long enough for the monthly savings to recover the upfront cost.

Origination fees compensate the lender or broker for arranging and processing the loan. The fee may be a percentage of the loan amount or a flat charge. Lender credits work in the opposite direction. The lender may cover some closing costs in exchange for a higher interest rate.

Ask each lender to show the rate with zero points, the rate with points, and the rate with lender credits. Then calculate the break-even period for the points. This is the amount of time it takes for the monthly savings to equal the upfront cost, helping you compare options more clearly.

Account for Appraisal, Title, Escrow, Recording, and Closing Costs

Your jumbo loan quote should identify the expected costs due at closing. These may include the appraisal, title search, title insurance, settlement or attorney fees, recording charges, credit report, flood certification, survey, and other third-party services. Lender charges may include underwriting, processing, document preparation, and origination fees.

High-value properties may receive additional appraisal scrutiny. The lender uses the appraisal to confirm the property’s market value and determine whether it supports the loan amount. For an especially expensive or unusual property, a lender may request a second appraisal.

Closing costs vary by property, county, lender, and transaction type. Ask whether the estimate assumes a standard single-family purchase and whether each cost is firm or subject to change. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s Loan Estimate guide explains how to review the cost information provided by a lender.

Include Taxes, Insurance, HOA Dues, and Prepaid Expenses

Property taxes can represent a significant part of the monthly payment for a high-value New Jersey home. Your quote may also include homeowners insurance, flood insurance when applicable, and HOA or condominium dues. These costs do not reduce the loan balance, but they affect the amount you need to budget each month.

Your closing costs may include prepaid expenses as well. These can include the first homeowners insurance premium, property tax adjustments, daily interest, and deposits for an escrow account. The amount depends on the closing date, tax schedule, insurance policy, and local requirements.

Ask whether your estimated payment includes taxes and insurance or only principal and interest. Confirm whether HOA dues are included, especially for a condominium or planned community. Providing accurate tax and HOA information helps the lender prepare a more realistic monthly payment estimate.

Review Mortgage Insurance, Reserves, and Lender Overlays

Some jumbo loans do not use private mortgage insurance in the same way as many conforming loans. That does not mean every borrower can avoid additional risk requirements. A lender may require a larger down payment, stronger reserves, or a specific loan-to-value ratio instead of monthly mortgage insurance.

Reserves are funds that remain available after closing. A lender may measure reserves in months of housing payments, and the requirement can depend on the loan amount, property type, occupancy, income structure, and number of financed properties. Retirement and investment assets may receive different treatment from cash held in a bank account.

Lender overlays are requirements that go beyond standard guidelines. They may affect credit scores, reserves, debt-to-income ratios, self-employed income, or property eligibility. Ask which overlays apply to your quote and request a written list of outstanding documents. Jumbo files often require income verification, asset statements, and explanations for unusual deposits or credit activity, as described in this jumbo loan qualification guide.

Separate Cash to Close From Post-Closing Reserves

Cash to close is the amount you need to bring to closing. It may include your down payment, closing costs, prepaid expenses, escrow deposits, and adjustments or credits. This amount is different from the total assets you may need to qualify for the loan.

Post-closing reserves are funds that must remain available after you pay the down payment and closing costs. For example, a lender may require several months of mortgage payments in reserve. Eligible funds may include money in bank accounts, brokerage accounts, retirement accounts, trusts, or other approved sources.

Make sure your quote identifies both figures. A down payment that appears affordable may not satisfy the lender’s reserve requirement. Organizing bank statements, investment statements, gift documentation, and other asset records early can reduce underwriting delays. It also shows how much money will be committed at closing and how much must remain available afterward.

Understand Rate Locks, Expiration Dates, and Extension Fees

A rate lock holds specific loan pricing for a stated period, such as 30, 45, or 60 days. The lock period should match your expected closing timeline, but a longer lock may carry a higher cost. Ask when the lock begins, when it expires, and what happens if closing takes longer than expected.

If the lock expires before closing, you may need an extension. Depending on the lender and the reason for the delay, an extension may involve a fee, a new rate, or updated underwriting conditions. Some lenders offer a float-down option that may allow you to receive a lower rate if pricing improves, although this feature may have restrictions.

A preapproval or rate quote does not guarantee final loan approval. The lender must still verify your financial information, property details, appraisal, title, and other conditions. Before relying on a quote, ask for the lock confirmation and a clear explanation of expiration dates, extension costs, and any conditions that could change the final pricing.

Compare Discount Points, Lender Credits, and Origination Fees

Discount points are upfront charges paid to reduce the interest rate. Lender credits work in the opposite direction. The lender helps cover certain closing costs, usually in exchange for a higher interest rate.

Compare the rate and total cost under each option. A quote with no points may require less cash at closing but carry a higher monthly payment. Paying points may make sense if you expect to keep the mortgage long enough to recover the upfront expense.

Review origination fees separately from discount points. An origination fee compensates the lender or broker for arranging and processing the loan, while points are tied to the interest rate. Ask for the dollar amount, percentage, and purpose of every charge. The CFPB mortgage guide explains how common fees fit into the mortgage process.

Calculate Your Discount-Point Break-Even Period

To determine whether points may make financial sense, compare the upfront cost with the monthly savings. Divide the cost of the points by the monthly payment reduction. The result is the approximate number of months needed to recover the upfront expense.

For example, if points cost $8,000 and reduce the payment by $200 per month, the break-even period is about 40 months. You would generally need to keep the loan longer than that period for the rate reduction to produce savings, assuming you do not refinance or pay off the mortgage early.

Ask the lender to calculate the break-even point using your actual loan amount and proposed rate. Consider the value of keeping that cash available for reserves, repairs, investments, or other goals. The calculation should reflect how long you realistically expect to keep the loan.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a jumbo loan and a conventional mortgage?
A conventional mortgage is not insured by the federal government and may be either conforming or nonconforming. A jumbo loan exceeds the conforming limit for the property’s county, unit count, and occupancy type. The loan amount, not the home’s purchase price, determines whether jumbo financing may be required.

How much do I need to put down for a jumbo mortgage?
Down payment requirements vary by lender, loan size, property type, occupancy, credit profile, and reserves. Some programs may allow 10% down, while others require 20% or more. A larger down payment can lower your loan-to-value ratio, but you should still keep enough funds available for closing costs and post-closing reserves.

What documents are usually needed for a jumbo loan quote?
You may need pay stubs, W-2s, tax returns, bank statements, investment records, employment details, and information about your monthly debts. Self-employed borrowers may also need business tax returns, profit-and-loss statements, and business account records. The lender may request additional documentation for bonus, commission, rental, trust, or investment income.

Are jumbo loan interest rates always higher than conventional rates?
Not always. Pricing depends on market conditions, the lender, loan structure, credit profile, down payment, property type, and financial reserves. Compare the interest rate, APR, points, lender fees, payment structure, and total cash needed instead of focusing on the rate alone.
