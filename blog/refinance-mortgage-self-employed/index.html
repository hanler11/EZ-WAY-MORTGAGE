Can Self-Employed Borrowers Refinance a Mortgage?

Yes, self-employed borrowers can refinance a mortgage. The process may require more documentation than it does for someone with a traditional W-2 job because lenders need to verify how much income the business produces and whether that income is likely to continue.

Refinancing may help you lower your interest rate, change your loan term, remove mortgage insurance, or access home equity. Your options may include conventional and government-backed loans, bank-statement programs, and other solutions available through EZ Way Mortgage’s loan services. Approval depends on your income history, credit profile, existing debts, equity, property, and the lender’s guidelines.

Meet basic eligibility requirements

Self-employed borrowers must meet the same core standards as other applicants. Lenders typically review your income, credit, debts, assets, property, and payment history before deciding whether you qualify.

Requirements vary by loan type and lender. A conventional refinance may follow different guidelines from an FHA, VA, USDA, jumbo, or bank-statement program. Comparing several options can help you avoid assuming that one loan type is your only choice.

Because business income can change from month to month, lenders usually want records that show how your business operates and how much income you can reasonably use to repay the new loan. Self-employed borrowers may refinance, but careful preparation is important.

Review credit, debt-to-income, and payment history

Your credit score is only one part of the review. Lenders also calculate your debt-to-income ratio, or DTI, by comparing your monthly debt payments with your qualifying monthly income. A lower DTI generally leaves more room in your budget and may make your application easier to evaluate.

Your mortgage payment history matters, too. Recent late payments can raise concerns, especially if your business income has also declined. Before applying, review your credit reports for errors, pay every account on time, and avoid taking on new debt unless necessary.

Lenders may also consider your savings and ability to keep making payments if revenue temporarily slows. Experian’s guidance for self-employed mortgage applicants explains why consistent income, credit, debt, and payment history all matter.

Confirm equity, loan-to-value, and cash reserves

Your home equity can affect both your eligibility and the cost of refinancing. Lenders calculate your loan-to-value ratio, or LTV, by comparing the proposed loan balance with the property’s appraised value. More equity may create additional options and could reduce the need for mortgage insurance.

You may need an appraisal to confirm the home’s current value. If you have completed significant improvements, keep invoices, permits, and other records that may help support the property’s value.

Cash reserves can also matter, especially for borrowers with variable income, investment properties, jumbo loans, or larger loan balances. Some lenders may want several months of mortgage payments available after closing. The Mortgage Reports explains equity and reserve considerations, but requirements vary by lender and program.

Show business history and stable income

Most lenders prefer to see at least two years of self-employment history. They may request personal and business tax returns, year-to-date financial statements, bank records, and proof that the business is active.

Underwriting focuses on qualifying income, not simply the total revenue your business brings in. Lenders generally review tax returns, account for business expenses, and determine whether the remaining income is stable enough to support the new mortgage payment.

A strong recent year can help, but lenders may compare income across multiple years. If your income is rising, they may request documentation that explains the change. If it is falling, they may use a lower average or ask for more information before approving the refinance.

Meet property, occupancy, and loan requirements

The property must meet the requirements of the refinance program. The lender may review its type, condition, location, value, and intended use. A primary residence, second home, and investment property can each have different guidelines.

Occupancy is especially important. You must accurately state whether you live in the home, use it as a second residence, or rent it to tenants. Misrepresenting occupancy can create serious problems during underwriting and after closing.

Your current loan balance, desired cash-out amount, property value, and loan type also affect eligibility. Government programs may have specific requirements, while jumbo loans often call for stronger credit, larger reserves, and more documentation. A mortgage professional can help match your property and goals with an appropriate refinance program.

Understand why two years is not always required

Two years of self-employment is a common guideline, but it is not an automatic requirement in every situation. Some lenders may consider an applicant with one year of self-employment if the borrower has a strong employment history in the same field before starting the business.

For example, a borrower who worked as a contractor for several years before opening a related construction company may have a clearer income history than someone who recently entered an entirely new industry. The lender will review the full timeline, including work experience, education, business ownership, and earnings.

Other factors may affect the decision, including credit, cash reserves, equity, and income consistency. Requirements vary by lender and loan type, so a denial from one lender does not always mean every refinance option is unavailable.

Correct common refinancing myths

One common myth is that self-employed borrowers cannot refinance. In reality, they can, provided they document their income and meet the lender’s requirements. Another misconception is that gross business revenue automatically counts as personal income. Lenders typically evaluate net income after eligible business expenses and may apply additional program-specific calculations.

Some borrowers also assume that writing off expenses is always helpful. While deductions can reduce your tax bill, they may also reduce the income shown on your tax returns for mortgage qualification. Discuss your plans with both your CPA and mortgage professional before applying.

Finally, a bank-statement refinance is not a document-free loan. These programs may use business or personal deposits instead of traditional tax-return income, but lenders still review statements, ownership, expenses, credit, assets, and the property. Organized records and an early income review can help identify the most practical refinance option.

What Documents Do Self-Employed Borrowers Need?

Mortgage documentation can feel more involved when your income comes from a business, contract work, or several sources. That does not mean refinancing is out of reach. It means the lender needs a clear picture of how your business earns money, how much income may be used for qualification, and whether that income is likely to continue.

Your document checklist will depend on the loan program, business structure, ownership percentage, loan amount, and financial profile. A conventional refinance may rely heavily on tax returns, while a bank-statement program may place more emphasis on deposit history. Government-backed programs can have their own income and property requirements as well.

Start gathering your records before you apply. Complete, organized documents can reduce delays and make it easier to compare options. You can also ask a mortgage professional to review your situation before you submit a full application. Keep digital copies of every document, including all pages and attachments. Do not remove pages that appear blank, and avoid editing statements or financial records. If something has changed recently, such as your business structure, income, or ownership percentage, explain it early. A clear explanation with supporting records is usually more helpful than waiting for an underwriter to discover the change.

Provide personal and business tax returns

Most lenders request personal federal tax returns for the past one or two years. If you own a business, they may also request business tax returns, including every schedule and attachment. These records help the underwriter review your taxable income, business structure, ownership share, and year-to-year earnings.

Depending on your situation, the lender may need returns for a sole proprietorship, partnership, S corporation, or C corporation. Provide complete copies rather than only the first few pages. Missing schedules can lead to additional document requests and slow the review.

Some borrowers may qualify with less than two full years of self-employment history, particularly when they have related experience or strong financial records. Requirements vary by program and lender, so review the available mortgage programs before assuming a specific rule applies to you. If your returns are on extension or include an amendment, tell the lender at the beginning of the process.

Document Schedule C, 1099, K-1, and W-2 income

Self-employed income can appear across several tax forms. A sole proprietor generally reports business revenue and expenses on Schedule C, which is attached to the personal tax return. Independent contractors may provide 1099 forms, although a lender will usually review the related tax return and business expenses rather than relying on a 1099 alone.

If you own part of a partnership or corporation, the lender may request K-1 statements. These show your share of business income, but the amount listed may not equal the income available for mortgage qualification. The underwriter may also need to confirm that the business has enough funds to support distributions.

W-2 income may count when you pay yourself a salary through a corporation or work for another employer. Provide recent W-2 forms and pay stubs when requested. Keeping each income source clearly labeled and separate can make the review easier. The Mortgage Reports guide to self-employed mortgage requirements provides additional detail about common forms and supporting records.

Submit a year-to-date profit-and-loss statement

A year-to-date profit-and-loss statement, often called a P&L, shows how your business is performing after the most recent tax return was filed. It typically lists revenue, expenses, and net profit for the current year. A lender may request one when your tax returns are several months old or when your income has changed since filing.

You may prepare the statement using bookkeeping software, accounting records, or a format supplied by the lender. It should be current, accurate, and consistent with your bank deposits and other financial records. Some lenders may ask your CPA or tax professional to sign or confirm it.

Do not estimate figures simply to make your income appear stronger. If the P&L differs significantly from prior tax returns, be ready to explain why. A new contract, seasonal revenue, lost client, or one-time expense could affect the results. Supporting invoices, contracts, or accounting records may help clarify the change. Experian’s self-employed mortgage guidance also identifies a current P&L as a document lenders may request.

Provide personal and business bank statements

Bank statements help lenders review cash flow, savings, deposits, and available funds. Depending on the program, you may need 12 to 24 months of personal and business statements. Bank-statement refinance programs may use these records as a primary way to assess income, while conventional lenders may request them as supporting documentation.

Provide every page of each statement, even pages that appear blank. Large or unusual deposits may require an explanation and proof of where the money came from. Transfers between personal and business accounts should be easy to identify, and regular deposits should make sense alongside the income shown on your tax returns or P&L.

Separate accounts can make this process easier. Mixing business revenue with personal spending may make deposits and expenses harder to verify. If you use payment processors, merchant accounts, or multiple business accounts, ask which records to include. Keep statements in their original format when possible, since altered or incomplete files may lead to additional questions.

Verify business ownership and history

A lender may need to confirm how long your business has operated, what it does, and how much of it you own. Potential documents include a business license, articles of organization, partnership agreement, corporate formation records, or a current state registration.

You may also need to provide evidence that the business is active. Depending on the lender, that could include a professional website, contracts, invoices, client agreements, or a letter from your accountant. The goal is to connect your current income with an established business or line of work.

Changes in ownership, legal structure, or business activity can lead to additional questions. For example, moving from a sole proprietorship to an LLC does not automatically create a new income history, but the lender may need records explaining the transition. Be ready to describe major changes and show that your current work relates to your established experience. A CPA letter may help confirm the business timeline and ownership details.

Share mortgage, debt, asset, and property records

A refinance application includes more than business income. You will also need records for your current mortgage, other debts, assets, and the property securing the loan. Common documents include a current mortgage statement, homeowners insurance declaration page, property tax information, and details about any home equity line of credit.

Lenders may request statements for checking, savings, retirement, investment, and other accounts. These records help verify funds needed for closing and reserves. They also provide a broader view of your financial position.

You may need to document car loans, student loans, credit cards, personal loans, and business debts for which you are personally responsible. If the property is an investment or second home, provide lease agreements, rental income records, or occupancy information when requested. Complete records help the lender assess your ability to make the new payment over time.

Keep a copy of your current loan information nearby, including the interest rate, balance, monthly payment, and payoff contact details. This information can help your mortgage professional compare the proposed refinance with your existing loan.

Request CPA letters and tax transcripts

A letter from your CPA or tax professional may help verify how long your business has operated, your ownership percentage, and whether the business remains active. The letter could also explain changes in income, unusual deductions, or a recent change in business structure. The lender may provide specific wording or a form for your accountant to complete.

You may also be asked to authorize tax transcripts from the IRS. Transcripts allow the lender to compare the tax returns you submitted with records received by the government. They are not always a replacement for complete tax returns, so ask which documents are required before sending your application.

If your returns are on extension, amended, or still being processed, tell the lender early. Your CPA can help explain the status and provide supporting records. Avoid waiting until underwriting begins to request these documents, since accounting offices may need time to prepare them.

A CPA letter does not replace every other income document. It supports the file by explaining business facts that may not be obvious from a tax return. Requirements vary, so confirm the lender’s preferred format before requesting the letter.

Understand why bank-statement loans still require documents

A bank-statement refinance does not mean you can qualify without financial records. Instead of relying primarily on tax-return income, the lender reviews deposits shown in personal or business bank statements, often covering 12 or 24 months. The lender may then apply an expense factor to estimate the income available for qualification.

You will still need to document the accounts, explain large deposits, and show that the statements belong to you or your business. The lender may also request a business license, CPA letter, proof of ownership, mortgage statement, credit report, asset records, and property information.

Bank-statement programs may be helpful when business deductions reduce taxable income, but they can have different rates, fees, reserve requirements, and property rules. Compare the complete cost with a conventional refinance before choosing a program. How Do Lenders Calculate Self-Employed Income?

Self-employed income can look very different from a traditional W-2 paycheck. You may receive customer payments through your business, transfer money to a personal account, take owner draws, or leave profits in the company. Lenders look beyond the deposits in your accounts. They review your tax returns, business structure, expenses, liabilities, and income history to determine how much income may be available for your mortgage payment.

Compare net income with gross business revenue

Gross business revenue is the total amount your company earns before expenses. Net business income is what remains after eligible business expenses are deducted. For most mortgage applications, lenders focus on net income rather than gross revenue.

For example, a contractor may deposit $150,000 into a business account but report $70,000 in net income after materials, insurance, vehicle costs, subcontractors, and other expenses. The lender typically starts with the income shown on tax returns and then makes permitted adjustments based on the loan program.

This explains why strong business sales may not result in equally high qualifying income. Experian explains how lenders distinguish gross revenue from net self-employed income, although each lender applies its own guidelines.

Apply deductions and allowable add-backs

Business deductions can reduce your taxable income, but some expenses may not reduce your actual cash flow. For that reason, a lender may add certain items back when calculating qualifying income. Common examples include depreciation and some home-office expenses.

An underwriter does not automatically add back every deduction on your tax return. The expense must meet the applicable guidelines, and the calculation may differ by loan program. You may also need to provide supporting schedules or other documentation.

Large deductions can affect refinance eligibility, even when your business is profitable. Before applying, review your tax returns with your CPA and mortgage professional. Experian recommends confirming which adjustments a lender permits instead of assuming every write-off will count.

Review depreciation, home-office, and vehicle expenses

Depreciation is a non-cash expense, so some lenders may add it back to business income after reviewing the relevant tax schedules. This recognizes that depreciation can reduce taxable profit without requiring a current payment.

Home-office and vehicle expenses receive closer review. The lender may consider how the expense appears on your tax return, whether it is ordinary for your business, and whether the loan program permits an adjustment. Personal and business use may also need to be separated.

Keep records for mileage, equipment, insurance, rent, utilities, and other costs. Avoid relying on another borrower’s experience because underwriting rules vary. The Mortgage Reports explains how lenders may review depreciation and other business deductions.

Count salary, distributions, draws, and retained earnings

Your business structure affects how lenders review income. If you own an S corporation or C corporation, the lender may consider your W-2 salary, business profit, and ownership share. For partnerships and some LLCs, the review may include K-1 income, distributions, and your portion of business earnings.

An owner draw is not always additional income. It may simply be a transfer of money already included in business profit. Retained earnings may also remain in the company and may not count as personal income unless the lender’s guidelines support using them.

Underwriters may check whether distributions are consistent and whether the business has enough funds to continue operating. They may request financial statements, corporate documents, or proof that you can access the funds. LoanFlight explains why business structure and income fluctuations matter during refinancing.

Assess ownership percentage and business liabilities

Your ownership percentage can affect whether the lender treats you as self-employed and how business income or debt enters the calculation. Many mortgage guidelines use 25% ownership as an important threshold, although requirements vary by program.

If you own part of a business, the lender may review your share of profits and losses. They may also examine business debts, leases, credit lines, and other obligations. A business liability can affect your debt-to-income ratio if you are personally responsible for the payment or cannot document that the business pays it.

Prepare proof of ownership, such as an operating agreement, partnership agreement, corporate records, or business license. The Mortgage Reports discusses the 25% ownership standard often used for self-employed borrowers.

Average income and compare annual trends

Lenders often average qualifying self-employed income over one or two years. They compare tax returns, current earnings, and business performance to decide whether the income is stable and likely to continue.

For example, $80,000 of qualifying income in one year and $100,000 in the next could produce a two-year average of $90,000, subject to the lender’s calculation. However, underwriting is not always a simple average. The lender may adjust the result if income increased sharply, declined recently, or came from an unusual source.

A lender may use a shorter period when the business has limited history or current income supports a different calculation. Keep monthly records and an updated profit-and-loss statement to explain changes. The Mortgage Reports notes that lenders may use a 12-month average or the lower figure when income declines.

Address declining income, losses, and large deductions

A decline in income does not automatically prevent refinancing, but it usually requires an explanation. The lender may review why income fell, whether the decline has continued, and whether current earnings support the requested loan.

Business losses can also affect the calculation. Depending on the business structure and loan guidelines, the lender may subtract your share of a loss from other qualifying income. One-time deductions may receive additional review when they create a significant difference between revenue and taxable income.

Gather contracts, invoices, current financial statements, and a written explanation for unusual changes. A current profit-and-loss statement may help document an improvement, but it may not replace the required tax history. Discuss timing with a mortgage professional if your latest tax return shows less income than the previous year.

Analyze bank-statement deposits and expense factors

Some non-QM refinance programs use 12 to 24 months of personal or business bank statements instead of relying mainly on tax returns. These programs may calculate income from recurring deposits and then apply an expense factor or subtract documented business expenses.

Not every deposit qualifies. Transfers between accounts, loan proceeds, refunds, large one-time deposits, and unexplained cash deposits may be excluded. The lender may also request statements from all relevant accounts to confirm that the deposit history is complete.

You may need a CPA letter, business license, ownership records, and documentation showing how much of the deposits represents business revenue. Which Refinance Options Fit Self-Employed Borrowers?

Self-employed borrowers have several refinance options, but the right choice depends on your income documentation, credit profile, equity, debt, and financial goals. You may want to lower your interest rate, reduce your payment, change your loan term, access home equity, or replace an adjustable-rate mortgage with a fixed-rate loan.

Your tax returns may show less qualifying income than your business revenue because of deductions, depreciation, and other write-offs. This does not automatically prevent you from refinancing, but it may affect which programs you qualify for. Lenders may also review your business history, ownership percentage, cash reserves, and recent income trends.

Start by gathering your tax returns, year-to-date profit-and-loss statement, bank statements, current mortgage statement, and details about your debts and assets. Then compare each option based on its total cost, documentation requirements, reserve expectations, and long-term effect on your finances. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s refinance guidance can help you understand the basic tradeoffs before you apply.

Choose a conventional rate-and-term refinance

A conventional rate-and-term refinance replaces your current mortgage without providing substantial cash back. You might use one to lower your interest rate, reduce your monthly payment, change from an adjustable-rate loan to a fixed-rate loan, or shorten your repayment period.

This option may work well if you have consistent self-employment income, solid credit, manageable debt, and enough documentation to meet conventional underwriting standards. Many lenders prefer to see about two years of self-employment history. Some may consider one year of self-employment combined with two years of related work experience.

Conventional lenders generally calculate income from documented net business income rather than total revenue. Large deductions can therefore affect your qualifying income, even when your business has strong cash flow. Ask how the lender will treat depreciation, business expenses, and distributions before choosing this route. The Mortgage Reports explains self-employed mortgage requirements, including business history and income documentation.

Consider FHA, VA, USDA, and streamline programs

Government-backed refinance programs may offer another path when conventional guidelines are not the best fit. FHA refinancing can be useful for some borrowers with lower credit scores or limited equity. VA refinancing may be available to eligible veterans, active-duty service members, and surviving spouses. USDA refinancing is intended for eligible borrowers and properties in qualifying rural areas.

Streamline programs may reduce certain documentation or appraisal requirements when you meet the program’s conditions. Examples include the FHA Streamline Refinance and the VA Interest Rate Reduction Refinance Loan. Your current loan type, payment history, occupancy, loan balance, and expected financial benefit can affect eligibility.

Self-employed borrowers should not assume that a government loan requires little documentation. Lenders still need to verify income, debts, and payment history, and individual lender requirements may differ. Compare the full cost, including mortgage insurance, upfront fees, and closing costs, rather than focusing on the interest rate alone.

Explore 12- or 24-month bank-statement refinancing

A bank-statement refinance may help business owners whose tax returns show less income because of substantial deductions. Instead of relying mainly on tax returns, the lender reviews 12 or 24 months of personal or business bank statements to estimate cash flow.

The lender may separate business deposits from personal transfers, exclude unusual deposits, and apply an expense factor to estimate qualifying income. You may also need to provide business formation records, statements from multiple accounts, and a letter from a certified public accountant or another qualified professional.

Bank-statement loans can have different rate, credit, equity, and reserve requirements from conventional loans. They are not automatically cheaper or easier. Review the income calculation carefully, especially if your business has seasonal revenue or irregular deposits. The Mortgage Reports explains how bank-statement loans use 12 or 24 months of deposits.

Review other non-QM refinance programs

Non-qualified mortgage, or non-QM, programs serve borrowers who may not fit standard underwriting guidelines. Depending on the lender, these loans may consider bank statements, asset-based income, investor cash flow, or other forms of financial documentation.

A non-QM refinance may be worth reviewing if you have a newer business, complex income, significant write-offs, or a recent financial change that makes conventional approval difficult. Alternative documentation can make approval possible, but these loans may involve higher rates, larger reserve requirements, stricter property rules, or higher fees.

Ask how the lender will calculate your income and which documents support the approval. Compare the non-QM option with waiting until your income history becomes stronger or using a home equity product instead. Consider cash-out refinancing

A cash-out refinance replaces your current mortgage with a larger loan. The new mortgage pays off your existing balance, and you receive the remaining proceeds as cash, subject to equity and underwriting requirements. You might use the funds for home improvements, business expenses, debt consolidation, education, or another major financial need.

This option can provide access to more money than some second-lien products, but it also increases your mortgage balance. Your payment and total interest costs may rise, and a higher loan-to-value ratio could affect pricing or approval. If you use home equity for business purposes, remember that your home secures the loan.

Create a specific plan for the funds before applying. Compare a cash-out refinance with a HELOC or home equity loan, and include closing costs in your calculations. A lower payment does not necessarily mean the refinance saves money if you extend the loan term or borrow considerably more. Explore jumbo refinancing

A jumbo refinance may be necessary when the new loan amount exceeds the conforming loan limit for the property’s location. These loans can help homeowners refinance high-value properties, but they often involve more detailed underwriting than conforming mortgages.

Jumbo lenders may look for stronger credit, substantial cash reserves, and a lower loan-to-value ratio. They may also review your business liabilities, ownership percentage, liquidity, income trends, and the source of large deposits. Requirements can vary significantly between lenders.

Prepare organized tax returns, current financial statements, clear debt records, and documentation showing your available assets. If you own multiple properties or businesses, expect the lender to ask how those obligations affect your personal finances. LoanFlight discusses refinancing with self-employment income, including factors that may apply to jumbo borrowers.

Compare HELOC and home equity loan options

A home equity line of credit, or HELOC, allows you to borrow against your equity as needed, usually during a set draw period. This can provide flexibility when you need funds over time, but the interest rate may change. Payments can also increase when the draw period ends and repayment begins.

A home equity loan provides a lump sum with a separate repayment schedule. It may be easier to budget for when it carries a fixed rate, but you begin repaying the entire amount right away. Both products leave your existing first mortgage in place, which may help if your current mortgage rate is lower than available refinance rates.

Compare these options based on the amount you need, how quickly you need it, your repayment plan, and your ability to manage another monthly obligation. Lenders may still verify self-employment income, business records, credit, and reserves. Review the Federal Trade Commission’s home equity guidance before using your home as collateral.

Compare conventional and bank-statement qualification

The main difference between conventional and bank-statement refinancing is how the lender evaluates your income. Conventional underwriting generally relies on tax returns and standard income calculations. Bank-statement underwriting uses account deposits and an expense factor to estimate qualifying income.

A conventional refinance may offer lower rates and more repayment choices when your tax returns show sufficient, stable income. A bank-statement loan may be more practical when your business has healthy cash flow but deductions reduce your taxable income. However, bank-statement programs may require more reserves, greater equity, or a higher interest rate.

Ask for a side-by-side comparison showing the calculated income, estimated payment, cash needed to close, total fees, reserve requirement, and likely approval conditions for each option. Weigh rates, terms, fees, and reserves

The lowest advertised rate may not produce the best refinance. Compare the annual percentage rate, loan term, closing costs, discount points, lender credits, mortgage insurance, and any prepayment provisions. A loan with a slightly higher rate could make sense if it has lower upfront costs and you expect to move or refinance again soon.

Reserves deserve special attention when you are self-employed. Lenders may require several months of mortgage payments and other obligations in eligible accounts. Using too much cash for closing could leave your household or business without enough flexibility during a slow season.

Request Loan Estimates from the lenders you are seriously considering, using the same loan amount and term for each comparison. What Costs and Tradeoffs Should You Expect?

Refinancing can change your monthly payment, loan term, interest rate, or access to home equity. It can also create a new set of costs, so the right choice depends on more than the advertised rate. Self-employed borrowers should consider how the refinance will affect personal finances, business reserves, taxes, and flexibility during slower income periods.

Start by requesting a detailed estimate from each lender. Compare the interest rate, APR, closing costs, monthly payment, cash required at closing, reserve requirements, and total interest over the planned loan term. A lower payment may help your monthly budget, but a longer repayment period could increase the total cost of borrowing.

Budget for closing costs, prepaids, and escrow

Refinancing usually involves closing costs, even if you remain with your current lender. Common charges include lender fees, title services, appraisal costs, recording fees, and settlement expenses. You may also need to prepay daily interest, homeowners insurance, and property taxes at closing.

Your new mortgage may include an escrow account for property taxes and insurance. This can increase the amount you need to bring to closing, although your existing escrow balance may be refunded after the old loan is paid off. Ask for a written breakdown of every charge, including costs paid before closing and amounts added to the loan.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s explanation of mortgage closing costs can help you understand common fees. Then ask your lender which charges are required, negotiable, or eligible for lender credits.

Pay appraisal, title, credit, recording, and lender fees

Your refinance may require an appraisal to confirm the home’s current market value. The result helps determine your loan-to-value ratio, which can affect your interest rate, mortgage insurance, cash-out eligibility, and approval. Some borrowers qualify for an appraisal waiver, but you should not assume one will apply until the lender confirms it.

Other possible expenses include a credit report, title search, title insurance, underwriting, document preparation, notary services, and county recording. Ask whether each charge must be paid upfront, collected at closing, or added to the new balance.

Review the official Loan Estimate from each lender. This standardized document makes it easier to compare loan terms and identify differences in lender charges, prepaid items, projected payments, and cash needed to close.

Compare discount points, rate credits, and rate locks

Discount points allow you to pay more at closing in exchange for a lower interest rate. This may make sense if you expect to keep the new mortgage long enough for the monthly savings to recover the upfront cost. The longer you keep the loan, the more time you have to benefit from the lower payment.

Rate credits work in the opposite direction. You accept a higher interest rate, and the lender applies a credit toward some closing costs. This may help preserve cash for business reserves, taxes, or other priorities, but it can increase your monthly payment and total interest.

Compare both choices using your expected time in the loan. Also ask how long the rate lock lasts, whether the lock can be extended, and what happens if underwriting or appraisal delays closing. The CFPB’s guide to mortgage points offers more detail on this pricing decision.

Evaluate no-closing-cost refinance offers

A no-closing-cost refinance does not mean the costs disappear. The lender may cover some charges by giving you a higher interest rate, or the costs may be added to your new mortgage balance. Both approaches can increase the amount you pay over time.

Compare a no-cost offer with a loan that has a lower rate and upfront closing costs. Then estimate how long you expect to keep the mortgage. If you plan to sell the property, refinance again, or pay off the loan within a short period, reducing your upfront expense may be helpful.

If you expect to keep the loan for several years, paying some costs upfront may produce greater long-term savings. Ask the lender to show both options side by side, including the monthly payment, total interest, cash required at closing, and projected balance after several years. Avoid choosing based only on the phrase “no closing costs.”

Compare pricing and reserve requirements

Loan pricing can vary based on your credit profile, loan-to-value ratio, property type, occupancy, loan amount, and income documentation. Self-employed borrowers using bank statements or other nontraditional documentation may receive different terms than borrowers qualifying with standard tax returns.

Some programs may require more cash reserves, a larger equity position, or additional documentation. Ask how much money must remain in your personal and business accounts after closing. Reserves may be measured in months of mortgage payments, and the lender may review whether your business has enough liquidity to continue operating.

Compare the complete loan structure, not just the interest rate. Include the APR, fees, required reserves, mortgage insurance, and any restrictions. A broker such as EZ Way Mortgage Corporation can review options from multiple lenders and help you understand which requirements apply to each program.

Lower payments and improve cash flow

A refinance may lower your monthly payment by reducing the interest rate, extending the loan term, removing mortgage insurance, or changing the loan structure. For self-employed borrowers, a lower payment can leave more room for taxes, payroll, inventory, equipment, and changing business revenue.

However, a lower payment does not always mean a lower total cost. If you restart a 30-year mortgage after making payments for several years, you may reduce your monthly obligation while extending the time you pay interest. You could also pay more interest overall, even with a lower rate.

Review the full monthly payment, including principal, interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, and any association fees. Compare your current loan with the proposed loan at several points in time, not just the first month. This gives you a clearer view of both immediate cash-flow relief and long-term cost.

Shorten the term and reduce total interest

Refinancing into a shorter term, such as 15 or 20 years, can help you repay the mortgage sooner and reduce the total interest charged over the life of the loan. The tradeoff is a higher required monthly payment, which may be difficult when your business experiences seasonal or unpredictable income.

Before choosing a shorter term, test the payment against your lowest expected monthly revenue. Consider taxes, payroll, household expenses, and emergency savings rather than relying on your strongest month. You should still have enough flexibility to handle a temporary slowdown.

Another option is a longer-term loan with voluntary extra principal payments. This may give you a lower required payment while allowing faster payoff when cash flow is strong. Confirm that extra payments are applied directly to principal and ask whether the loan includes any prepayment restrictions.

Access cash while increasing the loan balance

A cash-out refinance replaces your existing mortgage with a larger loan and provides the difference in cash. Homeowners may use the funds for renovations, debt consolidation, education, investment, or business expenses. The loan can provide access to equity, but it also increases the balance secured by your home.

Consider whether the planned use of the money is likely to create lasting value or improve your financial position. Using home equity to cover recurring business expenses may create pressure if revenue declines. You should also account for the new monthly payment and the possibility of a longer repayment period.

Compare a cash-out refinance with a home equity loan or HELOC. These options differ in rates, payment structures, terms, fees, and access to funds. A mortgage professional can help you compare the risks and costs before you use home equity for personal or business purposes.

Calculate break-even timing and tax effects

Your break-even point estimates how long it will take for monthly savings to recover the refinance costs. For example, if the refinance costs $6,000 and reduces your payment by $300 per month, the simple break-even period is 20 months. This calculation becomes less useful if you sell, refinance again, or make significant extra payments before that point.

Include changes to property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, escrow payments, and interest when comparing loans. Also review how the new loan affects your principal balance over time. A refinance can lower the payment while producing limited savings if the costs are high or the loan term is extended.

Tax treatment depends on your circumstances and how you use the refinance proceeds. Review the IRS guidance on home mortgage interest, then speak with a qualified tax professional before assuming interest will be deductible, especially when funds support a business or investment.

Protect business cash flow and review prepayment terms

Closing costs and reserve requirements should not leave your business without enough working capital. Keep funds available for taxes, payroll, inventory, equipment, insurance, and ordinary operating expenses. If your income varies, base the proposed payment on a conservative cash-flow estimate rather than your strongest earning period.

Ask whether the loan includes a prepayment penalty, particularly if you are considering a nontraditional program. Confirm whether the terms affect selling the property, refinancing again, making large additional payments, or paying off the loan early.

You should also ask how extra payments are applied and whether the lender charges fees for payment changes or loan modifications. Reviewing these details before closing can prevent an expensive surprise if your business grows, revenue changes, or you decide to move. Bring these questions to your mortgage consultation so the loan supports both your home and your business plans.

How Can Self-Employed Borrowers Improve Approval Odds?

Refinancing as a business owner can require more preparation than refinancing with a traditional W-2 job. Lenders may review tax returns, business income, bank deposits, debt, credit, and cash reserves before deciding how much income you can use to qualify. A strong application tells one consistent story: your business is active, your income is dependable, and you can manage the new mortgage payment.

Start preparing before you apply. Organize your records, review your credit, and speak with a mortgage professional who understands self-employed income. These steps cannot guarantee approval, but they can reduce avoidable delays and give the lender a clearer view of your finances.

Separate business and personal finances

Use separate bank accounts and credit cards for your business and household expenses. Mixing funds can make it difficult for an underwriter to determine which deposits represent business revenue, which expenses belong to the company, and how much money is available for personal obligations.

Clear records also make your application easier to explain. Keep invoices, receipts, transfers, and owner draws organized so they can be traced to the correct account. Experian recommends separating business and personal expenses and maintaining organized financial records when applying for a mortgage.

If you have already mixed funds, do not try to correct everything with unexplained transfers before applying. Ask your accountant and loan officer how to document the accounts properly.

Keep books and tax returns consistent

Your bookkeeping, tax returns, profit-and-loss statement, and bank statements should tell the same basic story. If your books show stable income but your tax returns show a sharp decline, the lender may request more documentation or use a lower qualifying figure.

Review your records for missing expenses, unexplained deposits, and changes in business structure. Make sure reported revenue and expenses are supported by your accounting records. Lenders often focus on income remaining after eligible business expenses, not simply the gross amount your business brings in. LoanFlight explains how lenders assess self-employed income, including whether earnings appear steady and likely to continue.

Do not change figures or rush to amend returns solely to improve a refinance application. Correct errors with guidance from a qualified tax professional, and give the lender a clear explanation for legitimate changes.

Show regular, traceable income deposits

Regular deposits can help demonstrate that your business continues to generate income. Deposit business revenue into the business account, keep payment records, and be prepared to explain transfers between business and personal accounts.

A lender may review 12 to 24 months of bank statements for certain self-employed or bank-statement programs. Griffin Funding explains bank statement expectations, including the need to show consistent deposits and cash flow in some cases. Requirements vary by lender and loan type.

Large one-time deposits can create questions if you cannot document their source. Keep records for loans, gifts, asset sales, refunds, and transfers from other accounts. A steady deposit pattern is more useful when the lender can verify where the money came from and whether it reflects ongoing business activity.

Understand how write-offs affect income

Business deductions can reduce your tax bill, but they can also reduce the income used for mortgage qualification. A lender typically starts with the income reported on your tax returns, then reviews whether certain deductions can be added back under the applicable underwriting rules.

This does not mean you should stop claiming legitimate business expenses. It means you should understand how deductions may affect your refinance application before making tax decisions. Large write-offs for vehicles, equipment, depreciation, travel, or other expenses may lower your qualifying income, even when your business cash flow appears healthy.

Ask your loan officer to review a preliminary income calculation, then discuss tax decisions with your CPA. LoanFlight outlines how deductions may affect qualifying income. Mortgage planning and tax planning should work together, not happen separately.

Coordinate tax planning with a CPA

Your CPA understands your business structure, tax history, deductions, and cash flow. Your loan officer understands mortgage underwriting. Bringing both perspectives together can help you make informed choices before you file returns or submit a refinance application.

Ask your CPA to review the previous two years of returns and identify anything that may need explanation. This could include a year with unusually high deductions, a business loss, a change in ownership, or income that moved between different tax forms.

With your permission, your CPA may also communicate directly with the loan officer when clarification is needed. The CPA should not promise loan approval, but can explain how your business operates and support the accuracy of your financial records. Keep tax advice with your CPA and mortgage guidance with your lender.

Lower debt-to-income before applying

Your debt-to-income ratio compares recurring monthly debt payments with qualifying monthly income. Lowering that ratio can give the lender more room to approve the refinance, especially if self-employed income is adjusted downward after expenses.

Start by listing every recurring obligation, including your current mortgage, car loans, student loans, credit card minimum payments, personal loans, and business debts that may be counted. Paying down a credit card balance may help, but use caution before moving money from business reserves or taking on new debt.

Ask your lender which debts affect the calculation and whether paying off an account would materially change your application. Because qualifying income can differ from actual cash flow, a strategy that looks helpful on paper could create a cash shortage in your business.

Strengthen credit and avoid new debt

Review all three credit reports before applying and dispute inaccurate information as early as possible. Check payment history, balances, collection accounts, and account ownership. Experian recommends checking credit reports and reducing card balances to address errors and lower credit utilization before applying.

Continue making every payment on time, and avoid opening new credit cards, financing equipment, or co-signing a loan while your refinance is under review. A new account can change your credit score, monthly debt obligations, or available cash.

Keep credit card balances low relative to their limits, but do not close long-standing accounts without asking your lender first. Credit decisions should support both your refinance application and your broader financial needs.

Preserve equity and build cash reserves

Equity can affect your loan-to-value ratio, pricing, and access to cash-out refinancing. Avoid taking on a new lien or withdrawing substantial home equity before you understand how it may affect the refinance.

Cash reserves matter, too. They can help cover closing costs, business expenses, tax obligations, and mortgage payments during a slower month. Some lenders may ask self-employed borrowers to document several months of reserves, and requirements vary by loan program, income profile, and property type.

The Mortgage Reports discusses reserve expectations for self-employed applicants, including situations where lenders may request six to 12 months of mortgage payments. Keep personal and business reserves clearly documented, and ask which assets the lender can count.

Resolve tax and documentation issues early

Do not wait until underwriting to discover that a tax return is missing a schedule, a bank statement has an incomplete page, or a business document shows a different address. Small gaps can slow the review and create additional questions.

Before applying, collect the documents your lender is likely to request. Review your previous two years of tax returns, current-year profit-and-loss statement, bank statements, business formation records, and proof of insurance if applicable. Check that names, addresses, ownership percentages, and business identification details match across documents.

If you owe back taxes, have an active payment plan, or recently filed an amended return, disclose it early. Your lender can explain what documentation may be required. LoanFlight recommends reviewing tax returns before applying so potential issues can be addressed in advance.

Apply after business income stabilizes

A recently launched business or sharp income drop may make qualification more difficult. Many lenders prefer to see a stable self-employment history, often around two years, though exceptions may apply when you have related experience, strong documentation, or other qualifying income.

If your business has grown steadily, wait until the income is properly reflected in the records the lender can use. If the most recent year shows a decline, the lender may use a lower average or ask for additional explanations.

Timing depends on more than the business anniversary. Consider whether your tax returns are filed, your current-year income is documented, and your deposits support the numbers you report. The Mortgage Reports explains common self-employed income history requirements, while noting that program rules can differ.

Avoid major financial changes before closing

Once you apply, keep your finances as steady as possible through closing. Avoid changing business ownership, moving large sums between accounts, taking on new debt, buying expensive equipment, or making a major career change unless your loan officer knows first.

Underwriters may review updated bank statements, credit reports, and income documents during the process. A new debt payment or change in business income can affect your debt-to-income ratio and qualifying income. If your income has declined, the lender may use a lower or more recent figure.

This does not mean your business must stop operating. Continue normal business activity and keep your records current. If an unusual transaction is necessary, document it before the money moves and tell your loan team what happened. Early communication is easier than explaining an unexplained change after it appears in underwriting.

Choose a lender experienced with self-employed borrowers

Not every lender reviews business income in the same way. One may be comfortable with Schedule C income, while another may have more experience with 1099 earnings, K-1 income, bank statements, or complex business ownership.

Ask how the lender calculates income, which deductions may be added back, how it treats business debt, and what documentation it expects. You can also ask whether it offers conventional, government, bank-statement, or other refinance programs that may fit your situation.

What Is the Self-Employed Refinance Process?

The self-employed refinance process follows the same general path as other mortgage refinances, but income verification may require more detail. Instead of relying on W-2 pay stubs alone, lenders may review tax returns, business records, bank statements, ownership information, and the way money moves between your personal and business accounts.

Your refinance options depend on your goals, credit history, debt-to-income ratio, home equity, business history, and available documentation. A mortgage professional can help you compare refinance programs and identify which requirements apply to your situation. Preparing early can make the process more organized and help prevent avoidable delays.

Define your goals and review your current loan

Begin by deciding what you want the refinance to accomplish. You may want to lower your monthly payment, change from an adjustable-rate mortgage to a fixed-rate loan, shorten your repayment term, remove mortgage insurance, or access equity for a major expense. Your goal will affect which loan types and terms make sense.

Review your current mortgage statement and note the interest rate, remaining balance, loan type, monthly payment, escrow amount, and any prepayment terms. Request an official payoff statement from your current lender so you know the amount needed to pay off the loan, including accrued interest and applicable fees.

Gather recent information about your income, debts, assets, and property value before speaking with a lender. Organized records and a clear understanding of your existing loan can make it easier to discuss your choices. LoanFlight’s refinance guidance also emphasizes preparation, stable income, and complete documentation for self-employed borrowers.

Estimate payoff, equity, and break-even timing

Estimate your home’s current value and subtract your mortgage payoff to calculate your approximate equity. You will also need to account for closing costs, prepaid interest, escrow funding, and any cash you plan to receive through a cash-out refinance. Your equity helps determine your loan-to-value ratio, which can affect program eligibility, pricing, and mortgage insurance.

Next, estimate your break-even point. Divide your total refinance costs by your expected monthly savings. For example, $6,000 in costs divided by $300 in monthly savings equals a 20-month break-even period. Consider how long you expect to keep the home and loan before deciding whether the savings justify the expense.

Equity requirements vary by program, property type, credit profile, and loan purpose. Some lenders may look for at least 20% equity, while other programs permit a higher loan-to-value ratio. Griffin Funding’s self-employed refinance information provides additional context on equity and loan requirements.

Compare conventional, government, and bank-statement programs

Self-employed borrowers may qualify for several refinance options. A conventional rate-and-term refinance may be appropriate when your tax returns show enough qualifying income and you meet the lender’s credit, debt, equity, and reserve requirements. FHA, VA, or USDA refinancing may also be available if you meet the applicable program guidelines.

A bank-statement refinance uses deposits in personal or business accounts to help document income. This may be useful when business deductions reduce the income shown on your tax returns. However, bank-statement programs may have different interest rates, fees, reserve requirements, loan limits, and documentation standards.

Compare the full loan structure, not just the advertised rate. Review the payment, closing costs, points, cash-to-close amount, and any prepayment terms. The Mortgage Reports discusses refinance options for self-employed borrowers, including conventional, government, and bank-statement programs.

Complete prequalification and income review

Prequalification gives you an early estimate of your potential loan amount and the programs that may fit your situation. Depending on the lender, this review may include your credit, current mortgage, debts, assets, property details, and preliminary income information. Prequalification is not final approval, but it can help you set a realistic budget.

Be prepared to explain how your business earns money, how long it has operated, and whether your income has remained consistent. Lenders may review your work history, business ownership, recent deposits, and year-to-date earnings. They may also ask whether your business income is expected to continue.

Many lenders prefer at least two years of self-employment in the same or a related field, though exceptions may be possible. Strong credit, consistent income, relevant employment history, and substantial reserves may help support an application. Experian explains self-employed mortgage requirements in more detail.

Submit tax, business, asset, and debt documents

After you select a refinance program, the lender will request supporting documents. Common requirements include two years of personal and business tax returns, a year-to-date profit-and-loss statement, personal and business bank statements, and documentation for your current mortgage.

You may also need statements for credit cards, auto loans, student loans, personal loans, and other debts. Additional requests can include business licenses, formation documents, ownership records, proof of insurance, leases, contracts, and CPA contact information.

If your income includes Schedule C earnings, 1099 payments, K-1 distributions, W-2 wages, salary, or business draws, make sure each source is supported by matching records. Provide complete statements with all pages, even when some pages appear blank. LoanFlight lists common refinance documents that borrowers can begin collecting early.

Complete underwriting, appraisal, and title work

Underwriting is the lender’s detailed review of your ability to repay the new loan. The underwriter compares your documented income with your debts, assets, credit history, property details, and proposed loan terms. For business owners, this review may include business liabilities, ownership percentages, income trends, large deposits, and transfers between business and personal accounts.

The lender may order an appraisal to estimate the property’s current market value. The appraisal helps determine the loan-to-value ratio and the amount available for a cash-out refinance. It may also affect whether mortgage insurance is required.

Title work confirms ownership and identifies liens, judgments, or other issues that must be addressed before closing. A title company may request information about previous changes in ownership or outstanding property claims. A professional appraisal is commonly used to establish the home’s value and potential loan amount, as Griffin Funding explains.

Respond to conditions and document requests

An underwriter may issue conditions before granting final approval. A condition could involve an updated bank statement, an explanation for a large deposit, a missing tax page, a signed business document, proof that a debt was paid, or verification that your business remains active.

These requests are a normal part of the review and do not automatically mean your application has a problem. Respond as quickly as possible and send exactly what the lender requests. Avoid uploading partial statements, altered documents, or explanations that do not match your records.

If your income changed, your business received an unusual deposit, or you recently opened new credit, explain the situation clearly. Include supporting records when available. Self-employed borrowers may receive additional requests because variable income can require more evidence of repayment ability, a point also discussed by American Financing in its bank-statement refinance guidance.

Review the Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure

After you apply, you should receive a Loan Estimate showing the proposed loan amount, interest rate, monthly payment, estimated closing costs, and other terms. Review each page carefully. Pay attention to the amount due at closing, lender credits, prepaid expenses, escrow funding, points, and whether the rate is fixed or adjustable.

A lower rate does not always mean a lower overall cost. One offer may include discount points or higher lender fees, while another may have a larger credit and a higher rate. Compare official Loan Estimates instead of relying only on advertisements or verbal quotes.

Before closing, you should receive a Closing Disclosure with the final terms and charges. Compare it with the Loan Estimate and ask about changes you do not understand. Confirm the loan balance, payment, cash-to-close amount, escrow details, and first payment date before signing.

Close the refinance and observe the rescission period

At closing, you sign the final loan documents and pay any required cash to close. The new mortgage then pays off your existing loan after the lender completes its funding and recording process. Bring valid identification and review the documents before signing. If a term or fee differs from what you expected, ask the closing agent or lender for an explanation.

For many refinances secured by a borrower’s primary residence, federal law provides a three-business-day right of rescission. The period generally begins after the borrower signs the loan documents, receives the required disclosures, and receives notice of the right to cancel. It may not apply to every transaction, including certain refinances involving a different property type or the same creditor.

Ask your lender or closing professional whether rescission applies to your loan. Once the period ends, the lender can fund the refinance, subject to the transaction’s requirements. Keep copies of the closing package and confirm when your new payment schedule begins.
