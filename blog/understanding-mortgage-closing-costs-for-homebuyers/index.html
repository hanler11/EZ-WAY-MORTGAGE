Understanding Mortgage Closing Costs for Homebuyers

Understanding mortgage closing costs for homebuyers means sorting lender fees, third-party costs, prepaids, and your New Jersey Loan Estimate.

Understanding mortgage closing costs for homebuyers starts with one fact. These fees are not your down payment. They are the separate charges that finish the loan and record the deed. National guides often put that stack in a range of about 2 to 5 percent of the loan amount. But that range is a planning sketch, not a quote. Your number lives on a Loan Estimate, then again on a Closing Disclosure. This guide sorts the four buckets New Jersey buyers actually see, then shows how to read the form without treating any line as a promise from EZ Way.

What understanding mortgage closing costs for homebuyers covers

A closing cost is any charge due to finish the purchase besides the price of the house itself. Some pay the people who make the loan. Some pay the people who check the house and the title. Some prepay taxes and insurance. Some pay the county to record the deed. Mix them together and the total looks like one mysterious bill.

Split them and the bill gets readable. Use these four buckets every time a lender sends a form:

  • Lender and broker charges. Fees paid to the lender, or to the mortgage broker, for taking the application and preparing the loan.
  • Third-party services. Appraisal, credit report, title search, title insurance, survey, and the settlement agent.
  • Prepaids and the first escrow deposit. Interest from closing until the first payment, plus months of taxes and homeowners insurance set aside up front.
  • Recording and government charges. County recording fees, and transfer taxes where the contract says the buyer pays them.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau lists the same families of fees: appraisal, tax service, title insurance, government taxes, and prepaid taxes, insurance, and interest. The buyer generally pays the costs tied to the loan. The contract, or state law, can shift some of them to the seller.

Cash to close is the number that matters on closing day. It is the down payment, plus buyer closing costs, minus any deposit you already paid, minus seller credits, minus lender credits. A big closing-cost line can still leave a small check if a credit covers it. A small closing-cost line can still leave a large check if the down payment is due in cash. Read both figures before you celebrate a "low fee" offer.

Lender and broker charges on a purchase loan

Page 2 of the Loan Estimate opens with Section A, Origination Charges. This is the lender's own section. You may see an application fee, a processing fee, an underwriting fee, or a single origination charge that rolls those jobs together. Discount points, if you choose to pay them, also sit here. A point is prepaid interest you pay at closing in exchange for a lower note rate. It is optional. It is not a required tax.

Because EZ Way is a brokerage, a partner lender sets these charges. EZ Way does not publish a fee schedule, a rate, or a monthly payment. Two lenders can fund the same conventional loan and show different Section A totals. That is why the useful comparison is the whole Loan Estimate, not a single line someone circled.

Section B is services you cannot shop for. The lender picks the provider. A credit report and an appraisal are the usual names. A tax-service fee or a flood check can appear too. You can ask what the fee is for. You generally cannot swap in your own appraiser once the lender has ordered one.

Broker compensation belongs in this conversation, and it should be visible. A lender-paid broker fee is built into the rate the lender offers. A borrower-paid broker fee shows up as a charge you pay. Neither one is a secret add-on after you sign. If a form is unclear about who pays the broker, ask before you lock. Do not guess.

Lender credits are the mirror image of points. The lender gives you money toward closing costs and charges a higher rate in return. The CFPB is direct about this. A credit is not free. You pay it over time through the rate, or the loan amount grows to cover the costs. Take the credit when cash at closing is the tight constraint. Skip it when you will keep the loan long enough that the higher rate costs more than the cash you saved.

Third-party services you can shop

Section C is services you can shop for. The lender still requires the work. You may pick the company, as long as the company meets the lender's standards. Title search, lender's title insurance, owner's title insurance, a survey, and a pest inspection often land here. The settlement or escrow agent who runs the closing can land here too.

The lender must give you a written list of providers for the services it requires and lets you shop. You can use a name on that list. You can also pick someone else. That choice changes how much the fee is allowed to rise later, which the Loan Estimate section below covers. Get the list early. Shopping title insurance the week of closing is how buyers overpay.

Title work is the piece buyers skip and later wish they had read. A title search looks for liens, unpaid taxes, and breaks in the chain of ownership. Lender's title insurance protects the lender if a claim appears later. Owner's title insurance protects you. In New Jersey, the buyer's attorney often handles the closing, so an attorney fee may sit next to the title charges. Ask the attorney what is included. A quote that looks cheap can leave the title premium out.

The appraisal is easy to confuse with a home inspection. The appraisal is for the lender. It answers whether the contract price is supported by recent sales. You pay it, and you do not control the result. A home inspection is for you. It looks for a bad roof, a wet basement, or old wiring. Budget both. They answer different questions.

Program choice changes which third parties show up, not the fact that someone has to do the work. An FHA, VA, USDA, conventional, or jumbo purchase can add its own required reports. A VA appraisal is not a conventional appraisal. A jumbo file can ask for extra documentation. None of those labels removes title work or recording. If you are choosing among programs, compare the Loan Estimates side by side rather than assuming the government-backed loan is automatically cheaper to close.

Prepaids, escrow, and recording charges

Sections F and G feel like fees, and they are large, but they are mostly your own money moved forward. Prepaid interest covers the days from closing until the first mortgage payment. Close on the second of the month and you prepay almost a full month of interest. Close near the end of the month and that line shrinks. The interest rate itself did not change. The number of days did.

Homeowners insurance is due up front for the first year on most purchase loans. The lender wants a policy in force before it releases funds. Property taxes are different. You may reimburse the seller for taxes they already paid past the closing date, and you may deposit several months of taxes into escrow. Escrow is a holding account the loan servicer uses to pay the tax bill and the insurance renewal for you. It is not a tip to the lender.

Recording fees are smaller and easier to overlook. The county charges to record the deed and the mortgage so the public record shows the new owner and the new lien. Those fees are set locally. They are not a place to negotiate with the lender.

New Jersey also charges a Realty Transfer Fee when a deed is recorded. The Division of Taxation describes it as a fee on the seller, calculated from the consideration in the deed. The state posts a stepped schedule. One schedule covers prices up to a set threshold, and a higher schedule covers prices above it. Partial exemptions exist for qualifying seniors, blind or disabled sellers, and some low- and moderate-income housing. The seller claims those on Form RTF-1. Use the Division of Taxation schedule for the actual brackets. This article will not reprint them as if they were a lender fee.

A separate state fee, often called the mansion tax, used to be talked about as a buyer charge on homes over $1 million. That changed. For transfers on or after July 10, 2025, P.L. 2025, c. 69 puts the additional fee on the grantor, which is the seller. On a residential deed the added fee starts once the price clears $1 million, and the percentage steps up at higher prices. Confirm the current bracket on the Division of Taxation pages before you write an offer. Do not rely on an older blog that still says the buyer writes that check.

Even when the seller pays a transfer fee by law, the contract can still give you a seller credit toward your lender fees, title charges, or prepaids. A credit is a negotiation, not a discount the lender owes you. Sellers often trade it for a higher price. Run the cash-to-close both ways before you agree.

How to review a Loan Estimate line by line

A lender must send a Loan Estimate within three business days after it has your application. That file needs your name, income, Social Security number, the property address, an estimate of the value, and the loan amount you want. The form is an estimate. It is not an approval, and it is not a commitment to lend. It is still the best tool you have for comparing offers, because every lender uses the same boxes.

Read it in this order:

  1. Check page 1 identity. Your name, the property, the loan amount, the purpose (purchase), the product, and the loan type should match what you asked for. A 30-year fixed that arrives as a 7-year ARM is not a small typo.
  2. Check the rate lock. The top of page 1 says whether the rate is locked and, if it is, until when. An unlocked rate can move. A locked rate can still change if your application facts change or you miss the lock deadline. The CFPB explains both rules.
  3. Read the projected payment. Separate principal and interest from taxes, insurance, and mortgage insurance. A payment that looks low because taxes are left out is not the payment you will live with.
  4. Find estimated closing costs and cash to close. Closing costs are the fees. Cash to close adds the down payment and subtracts credits and the deposit you already made. Fund the second number, not the first.
  5. Open page 2 and use the buckets above. Compare Section A across lenders. Compare Section C only after you know which title company each estimate assumed.
  6. Read page 3 comparisons. In 5 years, APR, and total interest are there so you can see the cost of a lender credit or of points. Use them. Do not stop at the rate in the header.

Not every line is allowed to grow between the Loan Estimate and the Closing Disclosure. The CFPB sorts charges into three tolerance groups. Fees paid to the lender, the broker, or an affiliate for a required service cannot increase, absent a valid change of circumstance. Transfer taxes cannot increase. Recording fees, and required services when you pick a provider on the lender's written list, can rise by up to 10 percent in total. Prepaid interest, insurance premiums, initial escrow deposits, and services you shop off the lender's list can rise by any amount.

A change of circumstance is a real event, not a mood. The CFPB's examples include switching loan type, changing the down payment. An appraisal that comes in higher or lower than expected, new credit activity, or income the lender cannot document. When that happens, the lender may issue a revised Loan Estimate, and the tolerance clock can reset for the affected charges. If costs rise past the allowed limit and nothing material changed, you are owed a refund of the excess. Ask for the revised form in writing. Compare it to the one you signed.

Three business days before closing you receive a Closing Disclosure. That is the version with final figures, not another rough draft. Match Section A to the Loan Estimate. Match the rate lock. Match credits. If a new fee appears, ask which tolerance bucket it sits in and why it was not on the estimate. Bring the questions to your broker before the closing table, not during it.

New Jersey buyer choices that change the cash you need

The fee buckets stay the same across Middlesex County and the rest of the state. The cash you need does not. Down payment and credits move cash to close more than any single third-party invoice.

Program minimums are rules, not a quote of what you will put down. Conventional purchase loans can start around 3 percent down. FHA is often 3.5 percent down. Eligible VA borrowers may put $0 down. USDA Rural Housing is location- and income-based and can also be low down. Jumbo loans, for prices above conforming limits, follow the investor's own down-payment rules. EZ Way shops those programs across partner lenders. The minimum is the floor. Your file, the property, and the lender's guidelines decide the actual number.

Seller credits and gift funds are the two levers buyers underuse. A seller credit can cover part of Sections A through G when the loan program allows it. Each program caps the credit as a share of the price or of the closing costs, and a credit cannot put cash in your pocket beyond the costs. Gift funds from a family member can cover down payment or closing costs on many programs, with a gift letter and a paper trail. Plan both before you write the offer, not the week of clear-to-close.

Pre-approval is where these choices get real. A same-day pre-approval tells you a likely loan amount and which programs fit. It does not lock a rate, and it does not list your final closing costs. Those appear only after a property is under contract and a lender issues a Loan Estimate. Still, pre-approval is what keeps you from falling in love with a house whose cash to close you cannot fund. Start with pre-approval through the contact form, then compare Loan Estimates before you lock.

If you later refinance, the fee map changes. Refinance closing costs are a different article, with different credits and different tolerance stories. Do not use a refinance worksheet to budget a purchase. Use the purchase Loan Estimate only.

Start your pre-approval and bring the Loan Estimate back if you want a second set of eyes on the fees.

Frequently asked questions

How much are closing costs on a typical New Jersey purchase?

There is no honest single number. National education sites often cite a planning range of about 2 to 5 percent of the loan amount, and some go as wide as 2 to 6 percent. New Jersey recording and title practice can push a file around inside that range. Your Loan Estimate is the only figure that belongs to your loan. Treat any percent range as a budget placeholder until that form arrives.

Are closing costs the same thing as the down payment?

No. The down payment reduces the loan balance. Closing costs pay for services, prepaids, and recording. Cash to close adds them together, then subtracts credits and your earnest-money deposit. You can have a small down payment and still need several thousand dollars for prepaids, or the reverse.

Who pays the New Jersey Realty Transfer Fee?

The Division of Taxation describes the Realty Transfer Fee as a fee charged to the seller when the deed is recorded. For deeds transferred on or after July 10, 2025, the additional fee on many properties over $1 million is also imposed on the grantor, the seller. A purchase contract can still assign other costs, or a seller credit, however the two sides negotiate. Ask your closing attorney which lines the contract actually gives you.

Can the lender change the fees after I get a Loan Estimate?

Some lines can move and some cannot. Lender, broker, and affiliate charges for required services, and transfer taxes, generally cannot increase without a valid change of circumstance. Recording fees and shoppable services you buy from the lender's provider list can rise up to 10 percent in total. Prepaids, escrow deposits, insurance, and off-list shops can change by any amount. It is illegal for a lender to deliberately lowball the estimate.

What if I cannot pay closing costs in cash?

Ask about a seller credit, gift funds, or a lender credit before you assume the purchase is impossible. Each option has a tradeoff. A seller credit may raise the price. A lender credit usually raises the rate. A gift needs documentation. None of these is a fee waiver. Get the structure onto a Loan Estimate so you can see the cash to close in writing.
