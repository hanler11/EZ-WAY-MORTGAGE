Mortgage for New Construction Home

A mortgage for new construction home, explained for New Jersey buyers: one close or two, and what to ask before you build.

A mortgage for new construction home is the loan that pays to build the house and then becomes the long-term mortgage once the work is done. It is not the same as a loan on a home that is already standing. The lender has to fund the build in stages, then convert that short-term financing into the mortgage you keep. Most people picture one loan application and one closing. New construction often has two phases inside that story. First, money goes out while the house is built. Later, that balance converts into the mortgage you pay after you move in. Fannie Mae calls this construction-to-permanent financing, and it comes in a single closing or two closings. The right shape depends on the lot, the builder, the timeline, and which lenders will actually offer the product.

What a Mortgage for New Construction Home Covers

A finished-home purchase pays the seller at one closing. A new build has no finished house to buy yet. The loan has to cover the land, the construction contract, or both, and it has to do that while the house is still a plan.

Fannie Mae describes two eligible purposes for a single-closing construction-to-permanent loan. In a purchase, you do not own the lot at the first advance. Interim funds buy the lot and build the home. In a limited cash-out refinance structure, you already hold title to the lot before the first advance. Those funds pay off existing liens on the lot and pay for construction. That is a program structure, not a promise that every file qualifies.

HUD draws the same line for FHA construction-to-permanent and building-on-own-land programs. The choice is one-time close or two-step close. How long you have owned the land is not what decides which structure you can use. A New Jersey buyer who inherited a family lot and a buyer who is purchasing a lot the same week can both ask about either structure. The lender, the program, and the property still have to fit.

During construction, the lender does not hand the full amount to the builder on day one. Draws go out as stages of work are finished. The lender, or a third party the lender uses, manages those disbursements. You are not writing personal checks for the whole contract and hoping to be paid back later, unless that is a separate arrangement you made on purpose.

Interest during the build is usually charged only on the money already drawn, not on the full future mortgage. That detail matters for your cash plan. A slow build with small early draws costs less in interest than a fast start that pulls a large share of the budget up front. Ask the lender how interest is billed in the construction period and whether you pay it monthly or whether it can be included in the loan. Do not treat a sample schedule as your payment.

One Closing or Two Closings

The biggest fork in a new-construction mortgage is how many times you close.

A single closing, also called one-time close or one-time close construction-to-permanent, uses one set of documents for both the build and the permanent mortgage. You sign once. When construction is complete, the loan converts to the permanent mortgage. Fannie Mae cannot buy that loan until construction is finished and the conversion terms are final. For you, the practical point is simpler: you are not shopping for a second mortgage in the middle of the build.

Fannie Mae's selling guide limits that construction period. Any single construction period is capped at 12 months. The total, including extensions, is capped at 18 months. Acceptable patterns include three 6-month periods, a 12-month period plus a 6-month extension, or six 3-month periods. If the build would run past those limits, the loan has to be done as a two-closing transaction to be eligible for sale to Fannie Mae. Exceptions to the 12-month and 18-month caps are not granted.

A separate Fannie Mae update can extend the age of credit documents to 18 months on a single-closing loan when the loan meets stated loan-to-value limits and receives an Approve/Eligible recommendation from Desktop Underwriter. That is about how long the credit file can stay valid during a long build. It is not a promise that your file will be approved, and it is not the same rule as the construction-period cap.

A two-closing loan splits the job. You close on a construction loan first. When the house is done, you close again on the permanent mortgage. You may be able to use one lender for the build and a different lender for the mortgage you keep. That extra closing is more work, and it means a second round of documents. It can also be the only path when the build will run long, or when the construction lender and the permanent lender are not the same company.

How the Build Period and the Permanent Loan Differ

Think of the file as two jobs that share one property.

During construction, the lender is managing a project. Inspectors or the lender's draw administrator confirm that a stage is complete before the next dollars go out. Plans, the construction contract, permits, and the builder's credentials sit in the file next to your income and credit documents. If the builder and the lender do not already work together, that match has to be made before anyone breaks ground.

After conversion, the loan looks like a regular mortgage. You pay principal and interest on the finished balance. Taxes and insurance can be escrowed, the same way they are on a purchase of an existing home. The permanent term on a Fannie Mae single-closing loan cannot be longer than 30 years after conversion. The construction months are not extra years stacked on top of that term.

Rate timing is a question to ask out loud. On some single-close loans, the rate for the permanent phase is set at the first closing. On others, the rate is locked when the loan converts. Fannie Mae notes that single-closing products can include locking the rate at conversion. Ask which version the lender is offering, how long that lock lasts, and what happens if the build runs late. We will not quote a rate here. Partner lenders set pricing, and it changes.

Draws, inspections, and change orders are where budgets slip. A change order that adds a room or upgrades finishes may need lender approval before it is funded. If you pay the builder directly for an upgrade, keep the receipt and tell the lender. Money that moves outside the draw schedule can look like an unexplained deposit or an unfinished condition later.

Manufactured homes can be part of construction-to-permanent financing when they meet the program rules for manufactured housing. A site-built custom home and a manufactured home are not underwritten as the same property. Say which one you are building on the first call so the comparison starts in the right lane.

Where Standard Purchase Programs Fit

New construction is a way of funding the build. Conventional, FHA, VA, USDA, and jumbo are the programs that may sit underneath it. Not every program is offered as a construction-to-permanent loan by every lender. That is why shopping the product matters as much as shopping the payment.

Conventional construction-to-permanent loans follow Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac rules when the lender plans to sell the loan. The single-closing limits above are Fannie Mae rules. A lender that keeps the loan in its own portfolio can set a different construction period. Ask whether the loan is being built for sale to Fannie Mae or held by the lender. The answer changes the timeline you can promise a builder.

FHA construction-to-permanent financing is the government-insured version of the same idea. HUD's guidance treats one-time close and two-step close as the two funding structures, including when you build on land you already own. FHA mortgage insurance rules still apply. A low down payment does not remove the need for approved plans, a builder the lender will accept, and a property that meets FHA standards when it is finished.

Do not confuse this with an FHA 203(k) renovation loan. A 203(k) finances repairs on a home that already exists. It is a different question, and we cover refinance use of that program in a separate guide. If your goal is a new house on an empty lot, start with construction-to-permanent, not a renovation product.

VA financing can be part of the conversation for eligible Veterans, active-duty service members, and certain spouses. Eligibility, entitlement, and the builder's VA registration are separate from a conventional approval. USDA guaranteed loans are location and income specific. A new home in a rural-eligible New Jersey area is not the same file as a suburban subdivision that falls outside the USDA map. Jumbo financing is for loan amounts above conventional limits. A large custom build may land there even when the borrower wanted a conventional loan.

What to Line Up Before You Apply

Bring the project, not just your pay stubs. A clean borrower file still stalls if the lot, the contract, or the builder is vague.

  1. Write down whether you already own the lot or you still need to buy it. That single fact changes whether the file looks like a purchase or a refinance of land you hold.
  2. Get the builder's name, license, and a draft contract with a realistic schedule. Ask the builder how often they have closed one-time close loans and which lenders they already work with.
  3. Separate hard costs from wants. The construction contract, site work, permits, and contingency belong in the budget the lender sees. Finish upgrades you might add later should be labeled as optional.
  4. List your income documents the same way you would for any purchase: W-2s or tax returns, assets for the down payment and reserves, and an explanation for any large deposit. Self-employed borrowers should expect tax returns, not a one-page profit guess.
  5. Ask each lender three questions: one close or two, when the permanent rate is locked, and what the construction period cap is on this exact product.

A pre-approval on a finished-home purchase does not automatically transfer to a construction project. The lender has to underwrite the plans and the builder as well as you. Same-day pre-qualification at EZ Way is a first look at your side of the file. It is not a commitment to lend, and it is not approval of a builder.

New Jersey borrowers also need a clear view of local steps that are not set by the mortgage. Permits, inspections, and utility connections sit with the town. A lender draw schedule that expects framing in month four does not move a permit that is still in review. Build that local timeline into the 12-month and 18-month conversation before you sign a construction contract.

If you are buying from a production builder in a subdivision, the builder may already have a preferred lender and a spec or to-be-built contract. You can still compare that offer with other lenders. Preferred does not mean required. Bring the purchase agreement and the option sheet so the comparison uses the same price and the same upgrades.

Questions to Ask Before You Break Ground

Use this list in the first meeting. Short answers are a warning sign. You want the lender and the builder to name the document, the timeline, and who releases each draw.

  • Is this one closing or two, and which guide is the lender following?
  • What is the longest construction period this product allows, including extensions?
  • When is the permanent rate locked, and what happens if the build runs past that lock?
  • Who orders draw inspections, and how many days does a draw take after an inspection passes?
  • Are permits, site work, and a contingency inside the loan amount or outside it?
  • Does the builder need to be approved by this lender before the application starts?
  • If you already own the lot, is the file structured as a refinance of that land plus construction, or as something else?
  • What conditions have to be cleared again at conversion, after the house is finished?

Write the answers down and compare them across lenders. A smooth verbal promise that "we do construction loans" is not the same as a product with a known construction-period cap and a known lock.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a mortgage for a new construction home the same as a regular purchase loan?

No. A regular purchase pays for a house that already exists. A construction mortgage funds the build in draws, then converts to the mortgage you pay after the home is finished. Some finished spec homes can use a standard purchase loan instead.

What is the difference between one-time close and two closings?

One-time close uses a single set of documents, and the loan converts when construction ends. Two closings means a construction loan first and a separate mortgage after the house is built. Fannie Mae requires the two-closing path when the build would exceed a 12-month period or 18 months total.

Can I use a construction loan if I already own the land in New Jersey?

Yes, that is a common file. Fannie Mae treats a single-closing loan on land you already own as a limited cash-out refinance structure that pays off liens and builds the home. HUD also allows one-time close or two-step close when you build on your own land. The lender still has to approve you, the plans, and the builder.

Does EZ Way lend the construction money directly?

No. EZ Way Mortgage Corporation is a licensed New Jersey mortgage brokerage. We compare lender programs and guide the file. The lender funds the draws and sets the rate, terms, and fees. Nothing on this page is a commitment to lend.

Should I look at a 203(k) loan to build a new house?

Not for a ground-up build. A 203(k) loan is for renovation of an existing home. Ground-up construction belongs in a construction-to-permanent conversation. If you are repairing a house you already own, that is a different guide.
