Conventional Loan Basics

A conventional loan can be used to buy a home or refinance an existing mortgage. Its terms depend on the loan program, lender, borrower, and property, so it helps to understand the basics before comparing offers. A low advertised rate or small down payment does not tell the whole story: fees, mortgage insurance, and cash needed at closing also matter.

What makes a mortgage conventional?

A conventional mortgage is not insured or guaranteed by a federal housing program such as the FHA, VA, or USDA. It is offered by a private lender, such as a bank, credit union, or mortgage company. Lenders review factors like your credit history, income, debts, down payment, and the property you plan to buy.

Conventional loans are not all the same. Some follow guidelines that allow them to be purchased by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, while others do not. Program rules can also differ on minimum down payments, credit qualifications, property types, and mortgage insurance. As Pennymac explains, conventional financing is distinct from government-backed mortgage programs.

When you compare offers, ask which conventional loan program the lender is quoting and what requirements apply to you. Compare the interest rate alongside fees, mortgage insurance, and your estimated cash to close.

How do conventional loans compare with FHA, VA, and USDA loans?

The key difference is whether a government agency backs or insures the mortgage, and who qualifies for the program. Conventional loans are not government-backed. FHA loans are insured by the Federal Housing Administration. VA loans are available to eligible service members, veterans, and some surviving spouses. USDA loans serve qualified buyers purchasing eligible properties in rural areas.

Costs and qualification rules vary. FHA loans include mortgage insurance premiums. Eligible VA borrowers may be able to buy with no down payment, though a funding fee may apply. USDA loans have income and property-location requirements, and may include guarantee fees. Conventional loans have their own credit, down-payment, and mortgage insurance rules.

No option is best for everyone. Compare total costs and eligibility for the loan types available to you. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s overview outlines how conventional loans differ from government-backed options.

How do conforming and jumbo loans differ?

A conforming loan meets requirements that allow Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac to purchase it. These include a maximum loan amount, which is set annually and can vary by county. A loan that stays within the applicable limit may qualify as conforming if it also meets other program standards.

A jumbo loan is a conventional mortgage with a loan amount above the conforming limit for the property’s location. Because Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac do not purchase these loans, lenders set their own jumbo guidelines. Those may include higher credit standards, larger down payments, more cash reserves, or extra documentation.

If you are buying in New Jersey, check the limit for the county where the home is located, then ask lenders how your loan amount affects the terms. The Federal Housing Finance Agency’s loan limit information can help you identify the current limits.

What should you know about down payments, credit scores, and private mortgage insurance?

Some conventional programs allow eligible buyers to put down as little as 3%, but the minimum depends on the program and borrower qualifications. A smaller down payment can leave more savings available for moving costs or repairs, but it also means borrowing more. If you put down less than 20%, you will typically pay private mortgage insurance, or PMI, which protects the lender if you stop making payments.

PMI adds to your monthly cost, but it may not last for the full loan term. Ask the lender how it is calculated and when you can request cancellation. Credit requirements also vary. A score of 620 is often cited as a common minimum, but meeting a minimum does not guarantee approval or a particular rate. Review your credit and ask how it may affect your personalized offer.

When might a conventional loan fit your plans?

A conventional loan may be worth comparing if your credit, income, and savings meet a lender’s requirements and you want to look at different property or loan options. Conventional financing can serve first-time and repeat buyers, as well as homeowners refinancing. Program rules vary, so ask what choices are available for your property and intended use.

Your down payment is one part of the decision. Putting more down may reduce the amount you borrow and could lower or eliminate PMI, but keeping savings for emergencies, repairs, and other expenses may matter more for your household. Look at the payment you can manage comfortably, not just the amount a lender may approve.

Compare conventional offers with FHA, VA, or USDA loans if you qualify for those programs. Request estimates based on the same loan amount and down payment, then review rates, fees, monthly costs, and cash to close. Explore Conventional Loan Benefits and Options

Conventional loans offer choices in down payment, interest rate, and property type. The right option depends on your finances, how you plan to use the property, and the loan programs for which you qualify. Compare the full monthly payment and upfront costs before deciding.

Compare low-down-payment programs and private mortgage insurance

You may be able to get a conventional loan with less than 20% down. Eligible borrowers may qualify for a down payment as low as 3% through programs such as Fannie Mae HomeReady and Freddie Mac Home Possible. Each program has its own requirements, which may include income limits, homebuyer education, or rules about occupying the property.

With a down payment below 20%, you’ll typically pay private mortgage insurance, or PMI. This adds to your monthly payment, though you may be able to request cancellation once you meet certain equity requirements. Ask your lender how PMI affects your payment and when you could qualify to remove it. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s PMI guidance explains what to review.

Choose between fixed-rate and adjustable-rate mortgages

A fixed-rate mortgage keeps the same interest rate for the life of the loan, making your principal and interest payment predictable. This may suit you if you expect to stay in the home for many years or prefer a consistent payment. Keep in mind that property taxes and homeowners insurance can change, affecting your total monthly housing costs.

An adjustable-rate mortgage, or ARM, may begin with a lower rate for a set period. After that, the rate can change according to the loan’s terms and market conditions. Before choosing an ARM, find out when the first adjustment occurs, how often the rate can change, and what limits apply. Consider how payments might look if rates rise, rather than relying only on the introductory payment. The CFPB’s ARM guide outlines key terms to discuss with your lender.

Explore financing for purchases, refinances, second homes, and investment properties

Conventional financing may be available for a primary home purchase, a refinance, a second home, or an investment property. Eligibility, rates, down payment requirements, and documentation can vary based on the loan purpose and your financial profile. Ask lenders to evaluate your plans for the specific property, since terms for a primary residence may not apply to a rental or vacation home.

Investment properties may require a larger down payment and more cash reserves. If you’re refinancing, compare closing costs with potential savings and consider how a new loan term affects the total interest you may pay. Conventional loans can serve a range of property types, subject to program and lender requirements. It does not capture every expense, such as some third-party charges, and it may be less useful if you expect to sell or refinance soon.

Review origination charges, discount points, lender credits, and other lender fees

Avoid Conventional Loan Application Pitfalls

Once you have a conventional loan pre-approval, keep your finances and paperwork as consistent as possible until closing. Lenders may ask for updated documents or review your credit again during the process. These steps can help you avoid delays and understand what you need to do next.

Avoid new debt and major purchases before closing

Wait until after closing to finance a car, open a credit card, or make a large purchase. New debt can increase your debt-to-income ratio, while spending down savings could leave you short on funds for your down payment, closing costs, or required reserves. A lender may also review your credit before closing, so a new account could affect your approval.

Keep your spending and savings steady while your application is being reviewed. If you are considering a purchase or financial change, ask your loan team first. SouthPoint Financial Credit Union’s guide to mortgage misconceptions explains why taking on debt or using savings set aside for a home purchase can complicate the process.

Discuss job or income changes with your loan team before making them

A change in employer, pay structure, work hours, or employment status may affect how a lender verifies your income. Moving from a salaried position to commission-based work or self-employment, for example, may mean the lender needs different documents or more time to review your application.

Before accepting a new role, taking unpaid leave, reducing your hours, or changing how you are paid, contact your loan officer. Share what is changing and when, so your team can explain what documentation may be needed and whether the timing could affect your loan. It is also important to tell your lender about changes to your credit or finances. First Bank’s mortgage myth guide notes that new credit before closing may be included in your debt-to-income calculation.

Respond promptly to document requests and explain deposits and fund sources

Your lender may ask for updated pay stubs, bank statements, tax documents, or explanations about activity in your accounts. Send complete, readable documents as soon as you can. If you need more time, let your loan team know and ask what is needed to keep the review moving.

Be prepared to explain large or unusual deposits and show where the money came from. A gift, sale of an asset, or transfer between accounts may require supporting records. Keep a clear paper trail, and check with your loan team before moving funds if you are unsure what documentation to retain. This can be especially helpful if you are self-employed or using money from several sources for your purchase.

Compare written Loan Estimates instead of choosing by rate alone

An advertised interest rate does not tell you the full cost of a mortgage. Ask each lender for a written Loan Estimate, using the same loan amount, term, down payment, and loan type so you can compare offers fairly. Review the interest rate, APR, lender charges, discount points, lender credits, estimated payment, and cash to close.

APR can help you assess borrowing costs, but it does not replace a careful review of the loan’s terms and fees. Also check whether the rate is locked, how long the lock lasts, and whether an extension could cost extra. Experian’s guide to comparing mortgage rates recommends reviewing APRs and fees from multiple lenders rather than focusing on the interest rate alone.

Confirm final terms, fees, and cash needed before closing

Before closing, review your final loan terms and compare them with the offer you accepted. Confirm the loan amount, interest rate, monthly payment, closing costs, and any points or lender credits. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a conventional loan with a small down payment?
Possibly. Some programs allow eligible buyers to put down as little as 3%, but requirements vary. A smaller down payment usually means a larger loan and may require private mortgage insurance (PMI). Ask a lender to compare the payment, PMI, and cash needed at closing for different down payment amounts.

How do I know whether a conventional loan is better for me than an FHA loan?
Neither option is best for every borrower. Compare the programs you qualify for, including their interest rates, fees, mortgage insurance costs, down payment requirements, and overall monthly payments. Request estimates based on the same loan amount and term so you can review them fairly.

What documents should I prepare for a conventional mortgage application?
Lenders commonly request pay stubs, tax forms, bank statements, and permission to verify your employment. Self-employed borrowers may also need business records or additional tax documents. Your lender can provide a checklist based on your income and how you plan to use the property.

What should I compare when reviewing mortgage offers?
Review the interest rate, APR, lender fees, estimated monthly payment, cash to close, and rate-lock terms. Check whether each offer includes discount points or lender credits, and make sure the loan amount, term, and down payment match across estimates.
