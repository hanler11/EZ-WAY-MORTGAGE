VA Streamline Refinance Scams: Red Flags to Spot

Learn how to spot VA streamline refinance scams, recognize red flags in unsolicited offers, and protect your personal and financial information.

A letter promising a lower mortgage payment can feel like welcome news, especially when it mentions your VA loan by name. But an unsolicited offer isn’t proof that the sender represents the Department of Veterans Affairs or your mortgage servicer. Some companies use official-sounding language, urgent deadlines, or vague savings claims to push borrowers toward costly refinancing. Knowing the warning signs of va streamline refinance scams can help you pause, verify the lender, and review the actual terms before sharing personal information. This guide explains what to watch for, how to check an offer, and what to do if something feels wrong. New Jersey homeowners can use these steps to make a more informed choice.

Key Takeaways

  • Verify unsolicited refinance offers: Find the lender’s contact details independently, confirm its New Jersey authorization, and don’t assume an official-looking message came from the VA or your servicer.
  • Compare written loan terms and total costs: Review the rate, payment, fees, loan term, and break-even point. A lower monthly payment may still cost more over time.
  • Protect your information and respond promptly to suspected fraud: Don’t share passwords or send money under pressure. If you’ve shared details or paid, contact your bank and servicer, preserve records, and report the incident.

What Is a VA Streamline Refinance (IRRRL)?

A VA Interest Rate Reduction Refinance Loan, or IRRRL, is a refinance option for homeowners who already have a mortgage backed by the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs. It replaces the existing VA loan with a new one on the same home. Borrowers often consider an IRRRL to change their interest rate or loan terms, but “streamline” does not mean automatic approval, no fees, or guaranteed savings.

The VA’s IRRRL overview explains the program’s purpose and basic requirements. Your lender will provide details specific to the offer, including the rate, payment, fees, and loan term. Review those terms before moving forward. For New Jersey homeowners, the key question is whether the new loan offers a real financial benefit after all costs are considered.

How an IRRRL works

With an IRRRL, a lender uses a new VA-backed loan to pay off your existing VA-backed mortgage. You then make payments according to the new loan’s terms. Depending on the offer, you may be able to change your interest rate or move from an adjustable-rate mortgage to a fixed-rate mortgage.

The word “streamline” describes the refinance program, not a promise that no checks or paperwork are required. Lenders may review your application and have their own requirements. Before agreeing to proceed, compare the new rate, monthly payment, loan term, and fees with your current mortgage. The VA’s program guidance provides an overview, while your lender’s disclosures show the terms of your specific loan.

Meet eligibility, seasoning, and occupancy rules

You generally need to have an existing VA-backed home loan to qualify for an IRRRL. The loan must also meet seasoning rules: typically, at least six monthly payments must have been made, and at least 210 days must have passed since the first payment due date. Lenders can explain how these timing requirements apply to your loan. Chase’s guide to VA streamline refinances also outlines these requirements.

You must generally certify that you currently live in the home or lived there before. If you’re unsure whether your mortgage or occupancy history meets the requirements, ask the lender to confirm before paying fees or sharing sensitive information. A lender may also have additional requirements as part of its review.

What an IRRRL can, and cannot, do

An IRRRL replaces an existing VA-backed mortgage with a new VA loan. The new loan may change your interest rate, monthly payment, or loan structure. Ask the lender to show how the proposed terms compare with your current mortgage, including the costs of refinancing.

An IRRRL generally does not let you take cash out of your home equity for expenses such as renovations or debt repayment. If accessing your equity is your goal, ask about other types of refinancing and compare their costs separately. The VA’s IRRRL information explains the program’s purpose and limits. Confirm that the loan being offered is an IRRRL, rather than a different refinance described as a streamline loan.

How a lower payment can cost more over time

A lower monthly payment does not always mean you’ll pay less overall. For example, refinancing into a longer loan term may reduce your monthly bill but keep you in debt for more years. If closing costs are added to the new loan balance, you may also pay interest on those costs. Compare the full loan terms, not just the payment amount.

Ask the lender how long it would take for your monthly savings to offset the refinance costs. Also consider how long you plan to keep the home and loan. A VA and Consumer Financial Protection Bureau warning urges borrowers to look beyond advertised savings and review the actual terms. Take time to compare the written offer with your current mortgage before deciding.

Common VA Streamline Refinance Scams

A VA Interest Rate Reduction Refinance Loan, or IRRRL, lets eligible borrowers refinance an existing VA-backed mortgage, often to change the interest rate or loan terms. But “streamline” does not automatically mean cheaper, simpler, or better for your situation. A lower monthly payment could come with closing costs, a longer repayment period, or other expenses that affect what you pay over time. Review the full offer before agreeing to a refinance.

Some companies use pressure tactics or official-sounding language to make an offer seem more trustworthy than it is. Others may accurately describe a loan but leave out costs or present potential savings as guaranteed. An unsolicited offer is not automatically a scam, but you should verify who sent it and what loan they are proposing. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s guidance on VA home loan scams advises borrowers to be cautious of companies that imply they represent the VA, the government, or a loan servicer. These warning signs can help you know what to question.

Churn loans with repeated refinances and little borrower benefit

Loan churning occurs when a lender or broker encourages repeated refinances that offer little meaningful benefit. A new loan might reduce your monthly payment but add fees or extend the time you’ll spend repaying the mortgage. If you refinance again soon afterward, you may pay another round of costs before you have had much time to benefit from the earlier change.

Ask the lender to show how the new loan compares with your current mortgage, including the rate, payment, term, fees, and total cost. Find out how long it could take for any savings to offset the expenses. The VA refinance scam guidance identifies repeated refinancing and recurring fees as potential warning signs. Request the numbers in writing, and take time to review them.

Falsely claim VA or government affiliation

A private company may use official-sounding language, logos, or letter design to suggest that it represents the Department of Veterans Affairs, the federal government, or your loan servicer. A caller might say they are contacting you “on behalf of the VA,” or a letter may imply that you need to act to keep a benefit. These claims do not prove that the sender is affiliated with a government agency.

The CFPB’s consumer fraud alert warns that veterans and military families have received calls and mailers falsely claiming government or servicer affiliation. If a caller says they represent your servicer, hang up and call the number on your mortgage statement or the servicer’s official website. Don’t rely on contact details supplied in an unexpected call, text, or letter until you have verified them independently.

Mislabel cash-out refinances as IRRRLs

An IRRRL refinances an existing VA-backed loan, often to change its interest rate or terms. A VA cash-out refinance is a different loan type that may let a borrower access home equity and can involve different costs and requirements. Be cautious if someone calls an offer an IRRRL while also promising cash from your home’s equity. Ask the lender to identify the loan type and explain how any funds would be paid to you.

A confusing label can make it harder to understand the loan’s purpose and costs. Review the application and disclosures, not just the salesperson’s description. The VA Loan Network’s warning about IRRRL offers describes offers that blur the distinction between streamline and cash-out refinancing. If the documents do not match what you were told, pause and ask for an explanation before signing.

Phish for personal, financial, or mortgage account details

Phishing uses fake emails, texts, calls, websites, or QR codes to trick people into sharing private information. A message may claim you need to verify your VA benefits, confirm a mortgage payment, or act quickly to secure a refinance rate. The sender might ask for your Social Security number, bank details, mortgage login, password, or a one-time security code.

Don’t share passwords or verification codes with someone who contacts you unexpectedly. Avoid clicking links or scanning QR codes in messages you can’t verify. Instead, type the lender’s or servicer’s website address yourself, or call a number from a recent statement. The VA and CFPB warning about suspicious refinance offers explains how fraudsters may use calls and digital messages to target veterans’ personal information.

Promise “skipped” payments, escrow refunds, or no-cost loans

Promises that you can skip mortgage payments, receive an automatic escrow refund, or refinance at no cost deserve a closer look. The timing of your first payment may change when you refinance, but that does not mean you avoid the cost of interest or other loan expenses. Likewise, how your current escrow balance is handled depends on the transaction and the terms of your loan.

Ask for an itemized estimate that lists lender fees, other closing costs, prepaid expenses, and any amount added to the loan balance. Ask what will happen to your current escrow account and when a refund might be issued. The Yahoo Finance explanation of VA IRRRLs notes that claims such as skipped payments or unusually attractive terms may be misleading. Check that every promise appears in the written documents.

Use public loan records to create official-looking mailers

Some mortgage and property details may appear in public records after a loan closes, depending on local recordkeeping and access. A company may use details such as your name, property address, or lender to send a personalized refinance offer. Accurate information about your loan does not mean the sender is your servicer, the VA, or a government agency.

Treat phrases like “final notice,” “benefit deadline,” or “respond immediately” as reasons to verify the sender, not as proof that you must act. Contact your servicer using the number on your mortgage statement and ask whether it sent the notice. The VA Loan Network’s scam warning describes how public loan records may be used to create official-looking refinance offers. Before sharing information, confirm who sent the mailer and what loan they are proposing.

Spot Red Flags in Unsolicited Refinance Offers

An unexpected call, text, email, or letter about refinancing your VA mortgage deserves a careful review. It may come from a legitimate lender, but an offer arriving in your mailbox does not mean you need to respond. A professional-looking message, or one that mentions your VA loan, does not prove the sender works for the VA or your current mortgage company.

Pay attention to the sender’s approach as well as the offer itself. Pressure to act immediately, promises of guaranteed savings, requests for passwords, and unclear company details are all reasons to pause. One warning sign does not confirm fraud, but several together should prompt extra care.

Don’t verify a message by using its phone number or links. Look up the company’s contact details independently, then ask for the terms in writing. Review the rate, fees, loan term, and payment before sharing documents or agreeing to proceed. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s guidance on VA home loan scams offers more advice on spotting pressure tactics and questionable requests.

Demand quick action through calls, texts, emails, or letters

Be wary of messages saying you must refinance today, respond before a deadline, or act immediately to keep a special rate. Scammers use urgency to discourage you from checking the company, comparing offers, or reading the terms. Unsolicited pitches may arrive by robocall, text, email, or official-looking mail, sometimes with details about your VA loan. Those details may come from public records, so they don’t prove the sender is affiliated with the VA or your servicer.

A legitimate lender should give you time to ask questions and review written information. Don’t click a link or call a number in a message you suspect is suspicious. Find the company’s contact information independently and ask whether the offer is genuine. This VA IRRRL scam warning describes how urgent language and official-looking materials can be used in unsolicited pitches.

Promise guaranteed approval, unusually low rates, or vague savings

Treat claims such as “everyone qualifies,” “guaranteed approval,” or a rate far below other lenders’ quotes with skepticism. An advertised rate may depend on your credit, loan terms, points, and other conditions. It may also be a teaser rate meant to prompt you to call. Ask for the rate and fees that would apply to your situation, in writing.

Be cautious of broad promises to save money without a clear explanation of the numbers. A lower monthly payment could come from extending your loan term or adding closing costs to the balance, which may increase the total amount you pay. The VA and CFPB warning about misleading refinance offers advises borrowers to look beyond advertised rates and consider whether a refinance offers a real benefit.

Pressure you to sign before reviewing written terms

A lender should give you a chance to read documents and ask questions before you sign. Be cautious if a representative says the offer will disappear unless you act immediately, discourages you from getting another quote, or asks you to sign papers you haven’t seen in full. Before agreeing to refinance, make sure you understand the loan amount, interest rate, monthly payment, term, and fees.

If a document is unclear, ask the lender to explain it in plain language and provide a copy for you to review. Don’t sign blank forms or rely on a verbal promise that a fee will be removed or a payment skipped. The CFPB’s consumer fraud alert identifies pressure to sign unread or confusing documents as a warning sign. If the representative won’t give you time to review, step away.

Request passwords, gift cards, or unusual wire transfers

Never share passwords, one-time security codes, or login credentials for your bank, mortgage, or email accounts with someone who contacts you unexpectedly. A mortgage professional may need financial documents to assess an application, but that is different from asking you to share your password or letting them log in as you. Send sensitive documents only through a secure channel you have verified with the company.

Be especially cautious if someone asks you to pay a mortgage bill with gift cards or a money order, or directs you to wire money to an unfamiliar account. Before sending funds related to a refinance, confirm the payment instructions with the lender or closing professional using a trusted phone number. The CFPB’s scam guidance warns about mortgage payment requests involving gift cards or money orders. If you’ve shared login details, contact your financial institution promptly and change affected passwords.

Hide company details or request upfront payments

Before sharing personal information, ask for the company’s full legal name, business address, phone number, and the name of the person handling your loan. Be cautious if a representative avoids these questions, uses inconsistent company names, or won’t provide written details about the offer. Verify the company independently instead of relying only on a website or phone number included in an unsolicited message.

Question requests for fees paid upfront, especially if the lender hasn’t explained what the charge covers or provided written terms. Some costs may arise during a legitimate mortgage process, so ask for an itemized explanation and confirm when each fee is due. Don’t pay by gift card, cryptocurrency, or an unexpected wire transfer. The CFPB’s tips for avoiding VA home loan scams identify upfront fee demands as a reason to pause and investigate.

Tell a Legitimate IRRRL Offer From a Scam

Remember: unsolicited offers aren’t automatically fraudulent

An unexpected letter, call, or email isn’t automatically a scam. Mortgage companies may contact homeowners about refinancing, and some may use public loan records to identify potential customers. Still, an offer deserves a careful review, especially if it promises a dramatic payment drop or urges you to act immediately.

Treat the message as a reason to check the details, not as a reason to share personal information or make a quick decision. Ask who the lender is, what type of refinance they’re offering, and how the new loan could affect your costs. The VA and CFPB’s guidance on refinance offers recommends caution with claims that sound too good to be true.

Verify the sender using independently found contact details

Don’t rely on the phone number, email address, or website included in an unsolicited message. Search for the company yourself and use the contact details listed on its official website to ask whether the offer is genuine. You can also check the company and loan officer through NMLS Consumer Access, the public database for mortgage professionals and companies.

Scammers may use details from public loan records to make a mailer or call seem specific to you. So even if a message includes your name, lender, or loan balance, verify it independently. Watch for misspelled website names and email addresses, too. If someone says they represent the VA or your current servicer, contact that organization using a number from its official website or a recent account statement.

Confirm the loan is an IRRRL, not a cash-out refinance

An IRRRL, or Interest Rate Reduction Refinance Loan, refinances an existing VA-backed loan. It may help you change your interest rate or loan terms, but it isn’t the same as a VA cash-out refinance, which may let you borrow against your home equity and change your loan balance.

Ask the lender to identify the exact loan program in writing. If the offer mentions “cash back,” “equity payout,” or “debt consolidation,” ask whether it’s actually a cash-out refinance and how that option would affect your balance and costs. Review the VA’s IRRRL requirements to confirm the program’s purpose and basic rules. Don’t proceed until you understand which loan the lender is proposing.

Get the rate, payment, fees, and term in writing

Before you agree to move forward, request written details about the interest rate, estimated monthly payment, loan term, closing costs, and any VA funding fee. Check whether the payment includes property taxes, homeowners insurance, and escrow, or covers principal and interest only. A lower monthly payment may come with a longer repayment period, so compare the new term and total interest, too.

Ask for a Loan Estimate and take time to review it. Don’t rely on verbal promises that a loan is “free” or that you’ll “skip” payments. Ask which costs you’ll pay upfront, which may be added to the loan, and whether a higher rate covers any fees. The VA and CFPB’s consumer warning advises borrowers to examine refinance claims and loan terms closely.

Know that the VA doesn’t endorse lenders or guarantee savings

A lender may offer VA-backed loans, but that doesn’t mean the VA recommends the company or has approved its specific offer. Be cautious if someone claims the VA selected you for a special deal, guarantees you’ll save money, or requires you to refinance with a particular lender. Ask for the claim in writing and confirm it through an official VA channel.

The VA sets program rules, while lenders review applications and set their own rates and fees. Your eligibility and costs depend on your circumstances and the loan terms, so no lender can promise savings without reviewing the numbers. Compare written offers from multiple lenders, and ask a licensed mortgage professional to explain anything that’s unclear. For questions about VA home loan benefits, consult the VA’s official home loan resources.

Verify Lenders and Compare Full Costs

A lower rate or monthly payment can make a refinance offer sound appealing, but neither tells you the full cost of the loan. Before you share documents or agree to move forward, verify the lender, request written terms, and compare the costs over time. A reputable mortgage professional should answer your questions clearly and give you time to review the details.

Check the company and loan officer through NMLS Consumer Access

Search for both the mortgage company and the individual loan officer in NMLS Consumer Access, the public database for mortgage licensing information. Check that the names and license details match the offer, and confirm the lender is authorized to work with borrowers in New Jersey.

Don’t rely on the phone number or website in an unexpected email, text, or letter. Scammers can impersonate real companies and professionals. Find the lender’s contact information independently, then call to confirm that the person contacting you works there and is handling your application. If you can’t verify the connection, don’t send personal or financial information until you’ve spoken with the company through a trusted channel.

Confirm the lender can do business in New Jersey

A lender must be authorized to conduct mortgage business in the state where the property is located. Use the New Jersey Department of Banking and Insurance to find state licensing information, and ask which licensed company will handle your application. Check the legal business name, not only a marketing name or website.

An out-of-state lender isn’t automatically a scam, but it should be able to confirm that it is authorized to work with New Jersey borrowers. If the company avoids questions about its license, gives you conflicting names, or asks you to proceed before you can verify its status, pause and investigate before sharing documents.

Ask who will originate, close, and service the loan

Ask which company will take your application, which entity will close the loan, and who will collect your payments afterward. These responsibilities may belong to different companies, but the lender should explain each role and provide the names in writing.

This information helps you verify future communications, especially if you receive new payment instructions. Never send money based only on wire details in an email or text. Call the lender or settlement agent using a phone number you found independently to confirm where funds should go. If the lender can’t clearly explain who is responsible at each stage, ask for clarification before signing.

Compare Loan Estimates from multiple lenders

Request a written Loan Estimate from more than one lender. This standardized form outlines key loan terms and estimated costs, so you can compare offers beyond the advertised rate. The CFPB’s guide to Loan Estimates explains where to find important details.

For a useful comparison, ask lenders to quote the same loan amount and term. Review the interest rate, estimated payment, closing costs, and cash needed at closing. A lower rate may come with discount points or higher fees, so compare the overall offer. Ask each lender to explain any charge or difference you don’t understand, and take time to review the estimates before making a decision.

Review APR, points, lender fees, and any VA funding fee

Compare the annual percentage rate (APR), interest rate, discount points, origination charges, and other lender fees. APR includes the interest rate and certain loan costs, which can help you assess the overall cost, but it doesn’t replace a careful review of each fee. Ask what each charge covers and whether it is optional.

Check whether a VA funding fee applies to your situation and confirm the amount in writing. Some borrowers are exempt, so don’t assume the fee applies or that it has been waived. Compare these costs across lenders, and ask for an explanation if the APR is notably higher than the interest rate or if a fee appears on one estimate but not another.

Compare the new term, total interest, escrow, and prepaid costs

Look beyond the monthly payment. A lower payment may come from extending repayment over a longer term, which could mean paying more interest over the life of the loan. Compare the proposed term with the time left on your current mortgage, and ask the lender to estimate total interest for both loans.

Review escrow amounts for property taxes and homeowners insurance, as well as prepaid interest and other costs due at closing. These expenses can change the cash you need upfront or the amount added to the loan. The VA’s IRRRL overview explains the purpose and basic requirements of this refinance option. Ask your lender to show how the proposed loan affects your costs in plain language.

Calculate your break-even point and check payment due dates

Estimate how long it may take to recover refinance costs by dividing those costs by your expected monthly savings. For example, if the costs are $3,000 and your payment decreases by $100 a month, the estimated break-even point is 30 months. This calculation is only useful if you compare the same types of payments and include relevant costs. Ask the lender which fees are included and whether they’ll be paid upfront or added to the loan.

Confirm when your first payment on the new mortgage is due and how the change from your current loan will work. A month without a payment due doesn’t mean a free month; interest and other costs may still apply. Check the payment schedule in writing and make sure you understand any timing changes before signing.

Protect Yourself Before Signing

Before accepting a VA Streamline Refinance offer, take time to confirm who you’re dealing with and what the new loan will cost. A few careful checks can help you catch misleading terms before they become part of your mortgage.

Contact your current servicer at a verified number

If an unsolicited call, text, email, or letter claims to come from your mortgage servicer, don’t use its phone number or link to check whether the offer is legitimate. Find the servicer’s contact details on a recent mortgage statement or its official website, then call and ask whether it contacted you and whether a refinance request is on file.

This step can help protect you from callers who use public loan information to sound convincing. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s guidance on VA loan scams recommends caution with companies that claim an affiliation with the government or a loan servicer. Your current servicer can confirm details about your existing loan, but you don’t have to refinance with it.

Request an itemized estimate and time to review it

Ask for a written estimate that lists the proposed interest rate, monthly payment, loan term, and each fee. Request details about discount points, the VA funding fee, title and appraisal costs, prepaid expenses, and any costs added to the new loan balance. Confirm how much cash you would need at closing and whether the quoted payment includes taxes, homeowners insurance, and escrow.

Compare the estimate with your current loan and other offers before you decide. A low advertised rate may come with significant fees or a longer repayment term. The VA and CFPB warn about refinance offers that provide limited benefits while adding costs to a borrower’s balance. A lender should give you time to review the numbers without pressuring you to act immediately.

Read the Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure carefully

Review the Loan Estimate when you receive it. Confirm that the loan type is an IRRRL, then compare the interest rate, projected payment, loan term, estimated closing costs, and cash needed to close with what the lender discussed. Ask about figures you don’t understand or costs that differ from the initial estimate. The CFPB’s guide to the Loan Estimate explains what to look for in this document.

Before closing, compare the Closing Disclosure with the Loan Estimate and ask the lender to explain any changes. Check the amount financed, lender fees, prepaid costs, escrow, and the date your first payment is due. Don’t rely on verbal assurances if the written terms say something different. Request clarification or corrections before signing.

Don’t sign blank forms or share passwords and account credentials

Never sign a blank form, a document with missing information, or paperwork you haven’t had enough time to read. Ask for a complete copy and a plain-language explanation of any section that is unclear. You can pause the process until you understand what you’re agreeing to. Pressure to sign immediately or before reviewing documents is a reason to step back.

A lender may ask for documents such as pay stubs, tax returns, or account statements to review your application. Send them through a secure channel you’ve verified, such as the lender’s official online portal. Don’t share your email, bank, mortgage, or other account passwords, and never give anyone a one-time security code. The CFPB’s VA loan scam guidance flags pressure to sign unread or unclear papers as a warning sign.

Keep copies of offers, disclosures, messages, and payment records

Save each version of the offer, your Loan Estimate, Closing Disclosure, signed documents, and messages from the lender or broker. Keep a note of calls that includes the date, the caller’s name, the company, and what you discussed. Save envelopes and record phone numbers and email addresses linked to unsolicited offers. These details can help you compare what you were told with the final loan terms.

After closing, retain your closing package and proof of any payments or fees. Store digital copies somewhere secure and keep paper records in a safe place. If a dispute arises, an organized record can help your servicer, bank, or regulator understand what happened. Keep the evidence if you suspect a scam, and share it with the appropriate agency when you report the offer.

Respond to a Suspected VA Refinance Scam

If a refinance offer seems suspicious, pause before taking another step. You don’t have to make a decision while someone is pressuring you. These steps can help protect your money and personal information, and give you a clear record of what happened.

Stop communicating; don’t send money or more information

End the call or stop replying. Don’t click links, open attachments, sign documents, or share more personal or financial details, even if the sender says the offer will expire soon. Don’t send money to secure a rate, release funds, or cover an unexpected fee.

Some predatory offers encourage repeated refinances that add closing costs without providing a clear benefit. If you’re unsure whether an offer is legitimate, contact the lender or servicer using a number from its official website, a recent statement, or your loan documents. Don’t rely on contact details in the suspicious message. A legitimate mortgage professional should give you time to review the written terms.

Contact your bank and servicer if you shared details or paid

If you shared bank or card information, call your financial institution using the number on your card or its official website. Explain what happened, ask whether the account should be locked or monitored, and ask how to dispute unfamiliar charges. If you sent money, report the transaction promptly. Your bank can explain what steps may be available, though recovering funds isn’t guaranteed.

If you shared mortgage account details or received suspicious loan paperwork, call your current servicer using a verified number. Ask whether anyone has requested changes to your account or payment instructions. The CFPB’s guidance on VA home loan scams warns borrowers about callers and mailers that falsely claim ties to the government, the VA, or a loan servicer.

Consider a credit freeze or fraud alert if your identity may be at risk

If you shared your Social Security number, birth date, or other identity details, consider placing a credit freeze or fraud alert. A freeze can make it harder for someone to open new credit accounts in your name. To place one, contact each of the three nationwide credit bureaus. A fraud alert asks lenders to take extra steps to verify your identity before opening an account.

Neither option prevents every type of identity misuse, but each may help while you investigate. The FTC explains how credit freezes and fraud alerts work, including how to place or remove them. If you suspect someone has already used your information, visit IdentityTheft.gov for a recovery plan.

Report suspected fraud to the VA Office of Inspector General and FTC

Report suspected VA-related fraud to the VA Office of Inspector General. Describe what the person claimed, how they contacted you, and whether they asked for money or personal information. You can report suspicious activity even if you’re unsure whether a crime occurred. Include the company’s name and any details that could help identify the offer.

You can also submit a report through the FTC’s fraud reporting site. Include dates, phone numbers, email addresses, website links, and the amount of any payment. Reporting doesn’t guarantee that you’ll recover money or receive an individual response, but your information may help authorities identify patterns and warn others.

File a complaint with the CFPB or New Jersey’s banking regulator

If your concern involves a mortgage lender, loan officer, or servicer, consider filing a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. The CFPB accepts mortgage-related complaints and lets you include supporting documents. Explain what happened, what steps you’ve taken, and what response or resolution you’re seeking.

New Jersey residents can also contact the New Jersey Department of Banking and Insurance with concerns about a financial services company. Gather the company’s name, any license or registration details, and copies of relevant messages or loan paperwork before submitting your complaint. A complaint doesn’t replace contacting your bank or servicer about urgent account security issues. If you shared account details or sent money, contact them as soon as possible.

Save messages, documents, phone numbers, and transaction records

Keep copies of every text, email, letter, voicemail, and document related to the offer. Save the sender’s phone number, email address, website, mailing address, and the date and time of each contact. If you made a payment, keep the receipt, transfer confirmation, or bank statement. These records can help your bank, servicer, or investigators understand what happened.

Don’t alter the original messages or documents. Store copies somewhere secure, and write down a brief timeline while the details are fresh. Note what the sender promised, what information you shared, and what steps you took afterward. If the person contacts you again, preserve the new message and verify their identity through an official channel before responding.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an unsolicited VA IRRRL offer be legitimate?
Yes. Lenders may contact homeowners about refinancing, but an unexpected message does not confirm the sender’s identity or the value of the offer. Verify the company using contact details you find independently, then review the loan terms before sharing information or moving forward.

Does a lower monthly payment mean an IRRRL will save me money?
Not necessarily. A smaller payment could result from extending the loan term or adding costs to the new balance. Compare the interest rate, loan term, fees, and total cost with your current mortgage, and ask how long it may take to recover the refinance costs.

How can I tell whether a lender is offering an IRRRL or a cash-out refinance?
Ask the lender to name the loan program in writing and explain whether you’ll receive cash from your home equity. An IRRRL refinances an existing VA-backed mortgage, while a cash-out refinance is a separate loan type with different terms and costs. Review the Loan Estimate and other disclosures to confirm the offer matches what you were told.

What should I do if I shared personal information with a suspicious company?
Contact your bank or mortgage servicer using a verified phone number if you shared account details or payment information. If you provided identity information, consider a credit freeze or fraud alert. Save messages and documents, then report suspected fraud to the appropriate authorities.
