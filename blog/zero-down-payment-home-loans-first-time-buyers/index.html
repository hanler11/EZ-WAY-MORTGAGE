Zero down payment home loans for first time buyers

See which zero down payment home loans for first time buyers fit in New Jersey, and what cash you still need at closing.

Zero down payment home loans for first time buyers are real, but they are not a blank check. In New Jersey, two paths can finance the full purchase price. One is a VA loan for an eligible service member, Veteran, or surviving spouse. The other is a USDA guaranteed loan when the home sits in an eligible rural area and household income fits the limit.

A state down-payment loan can cover cash you would otherwise bring, but it sits on top of a first mortgage that still has its own rules. None of these is a promise that you will be approved, and none of them erases closing costs.

What zero down payment home loans for first time buyers actually cover.

People search this phrase because they want to buy without emptying a savings account. The honest answer is narrower than the ads. A zero-down loan means the lender can finance the purchase price, up to the appraised value, when you and the property qualify. It does not mean the lender pays your taxes, insurance, title work, or the program fee that keeps the guarantee in place.

For a first-time buyer in New Jersey, three ideas get mixed together:

  • True zero down. VA purchase loans and USDA Section 502 guaranteed loans can finance the full purchase price for borrowers who meet the program rules. The VA purchase loan page says eligible buyers often need no down payment, up to the appraised value.
  • Low down, not zero. FHA loans and many conventional first-time programs still ask for a down payment. On this site, FHA is described as a 3.5 percent down option, and conventional purchase loans start at 3 percent down. Those are useful, but they are a different question.
  • Help with the cash you do owe. New Jersey's housing agency can lend money toward down payment and closing costs. That help is a second loan paired with a first mortgage. It is not, by itself, a zero-down first mortgage.

Credit unions and local banks sometimes advertise their own 100 percent financing. Those products come and go, and the terms belong to that lender. VA loans: zero down for eligible service, not for every buyer.

A VA-backed purchase loan is the clearest zero-down path, and it is closed to most civilians. You need a valid Certificate of Eligibility, and you must plan to live in the home. You still have to meet credit and income standards from both VA and the lender. VA's own eligibility page is the source for service rules.

Minimum service depends on when you served. For many Gulf War-era Veterans, that can mean 24 continuous months, or at least 90 days in some cases, with shorter service allowed after a service-connected disability discharge. A lender can request the certificate, or you can request it yourself.

If you qualify, the loan can cover the purchase price up to the appraised value with no down payment, and there is no monthly private mortgage insurance. The home can be a single-family house, a one-to-four unit property you will live in, a condo in a VA-approved project, or certain manufactured homes. The benefit can be used more than once after you sell or refinance, if you still meet the rules.

The funding fee is not a down payment.

Most VA borrowers pay a one-time funding fee. It keeps the program going because VA does not charge monthly mortgage insurance. VA News describes the fee for first-time use as typically 2.15 percent of the loan, and higher on a later use with no down payment. A larger down payment can lower the percentage. You can pay the fee in cash at closing or roll it into the loan. Rolling it in means you finance more than the purchase price, so "zero down" can still increase what you owe.

You do not pay the fee in a few cases listed by VA. Those include receiving compensation for a service-connected disability, taking retirement or active-duty pay instead of that compensation, or receiving Dependency and Indemnity Compensation as a surviving spouse. A few pre-discharge and Purple Heart cases are exempt too. A later disability award can sometimes produce a refund when the effective date reaches back before closing. That decision belongs to VA, not to the broker.

The full certificate walkthrough, current rate talk, and streamline refinance warnings live on other pages. This article only uses the purchase rules that decide whether a first-time buyer can skip the down payment.

USDA guaranteed loans: zero down only in eligible rural areas.

The other nationwide zero-down program is the USDA Single Family Housing Guaranteed Loan, often called a Section 502 guaranteed loan. USDA gives approved lenders a 90 percent loan note guarantee so they can offer 100 percent financing to low- and moderate-income buyers. Qualified borrowers pay no down payment. USDA confirms the current upfront and annual fees in its fiscal year 2026 fee notice. The loan is a 30-year fixed term, and the home must be your primary residence in an eligible rural area.

Income may not exceed 115 percent of the area median. You must be a U.S. citizen, a U.S. non-citizen national, or a Qualified Alien. USDA does not publish a single minimum credit score. You still have to show you can manage debt, and the lender sets its own credit overlay. Household income, not just your paycheck, is what gets tested against the county limit.

Fiscal year 2026 runs from October 1, 2025 through September 30, 2026. USDA's fee notice sets an upfront guarantee fee of 1 percent and an annual fee of 0.35 percent on purchase and refinance loans. The upfront fee can be financed. The annual fee is paid with the monthly payment.

Those percentages can change in a later fiscal year. The fee that applies is tied to the conditional commitment USDA issues, not to the day you close. Check the current notice before you rely on a number from an old article.

New Jersey still has USDA-eligible pockets outside the big urban cores. Perth Amboy and much of the New York and Philadelphia suburbs will not qualify. A house twenty minutes away might. The only reliable check is USDA's property and income eligibility tool, run on the actual address, before you fall in love with a listing. Do not guess from a town name.

Lender shopping, streamline refinance costs, and a side-by-side with FHA are separate topics. Here, USDA matters only as a zero-down purchase option when the map and the income limit both say yes.

New Jersey help that is not the same as zero down.

Many first-time buyers in this state are not Veterans and are not buying in a USDA area. For them, "no money down" usually means help with the cash the first mortgage still requires.

The New Jersey Housing and Mortgage Finance Agency offers a First-Time Homebuyer Mortgage Program. It is a 30-year fixed government-insured loan, which can be FHA, VA, or USDA, for a New Jersey home you will occupy. Its consumer fact sheet says the paired Down Payment Assistance Program can provide up to fifteen thousand dollars toward down payment and closing costs, based on the county.

That assistance is a no-interest second loan with no monthly payment. It is forgiven if you live in the home as your principal residence for five years and you do not refinance or otherwise convey the first mortgage. You can use it once.

Read the limits before you count on it:

  • NJHMFA treats you as a first-time buyer if you have not owned a primary home in the past three years. Buyers in a designated Urban Target Area, and qualified Veterans, can be exempt from that three-year test, but you still cannot own another primary home at closing.
  • Household income limits and purchase-price limits apply, and they differ by county. Urban Target Areas can have higher income limits.
  • The down payment itself still follows the insurer. FHA's minimum, VA's zero-down rule, or USDA's zero-down rule does not change just because the state helps with cash.
  • Assistance cannot cover costs the seller customarily pays, and it cannot cover an appraisal shortfall when the home appraises below the price.
  • You apply through an NJHMFA participating lender. A broker can help you see whether the first mortgage fits, but the agency pairs the assistance with its own lender network.

County and nonprofit grants sometimes appear on top of these programs. Treat any grant as real only after you read who funds it, whether it is a loan or a gift, and what happens if you sell early. A blog post cannot lock a dollar amount that a county can change.

Zero down is not zero cash at the closing table.

This is the line that surprises people. Even when the loan covers the price, you can still need cash for items the program does not finance.

Typical items, set by the lender, the title company, or the county, include:

  • Lender and third-party closing costs, such as origination, appraisal, title insurance, and recording charges.
  • Prepaid property taxes and homeowners insurance, often collected so the lender can start an escrow account.
  • The VA funding fee, if you are not exempt and you choose not to finance it.
  • The USDA upfront guarantee fee, if you do not roll it into the loan.
  • Repairs the appraisal requires before the loan can close.
  • Moving costs, utility deposits, and a small reserve some lenders want to see left in the bank after closing.

Sellers can agree to pay some of the buyer's closing costs. VA, USDA, and FHA each cap how much the seller can contribute, and a contract that asks for more than the cap has to be rewritten. Seller help is negotiated. It is not a right, and it is not a reason to skip reading the Loan Estimate.

An appraisal gap is a separate problem. If the home appraises below the price, a zero-down program generally will not finance the difference. You would need to renegotiate, bring cash, or walk away if your contract allows it. NJHMFA assistance cannot fill that gap either.

If you want the line-by-line version of lender fees, prepaids, and the Loan Estimate, ask for it on your own file. Use the pre-approval form rather than treating this page as a fee sheet. The point here is simpler: budget for cash even when the down payment line says zero.

A practical order for New Jersey first-time buyers.

Start with the gate that can disqualify you, not with a rate screenshot. A same-day pre-approval conversation is useful only after you know which program can even look at the file.

  1. Name your service history. If you, your spouse, or a surviving spouse may have VA eligibility, get the Certificate of Eligibility question answered before you tour homes. A civilian buyer should not plan around a VA loan.
  2. Drop the address into USDA's eligibility map. If the town fails, stop building a USDA budget. If it passes, check household income against the county limit, including income from people who will live in the home.
  3. If both of those are closed, price a low-down loan plus help. FHA at 3.5 percent down, or a conventional option at 3 percent down, is the realistic path for many Middlesex County buyers. Add NJHMFA assistance or a documented gift if you need help with the cash. That is low cash, not zero down.
  4. Add closing cash on purpose. Ask the broker to show the cash-to-close range in words, including the program fee, prepaids, and any seller credit you hope to negotiate. Do not treat a pre-approval letter as a promise that those items are covered.
  5. Match the house to the program before you write the offer. VA condos need project approval. USDA homes need the rural map. NJHMFA homes must be in New Jersey and must fit the price and income caps. A house that fails the rule wastes the inspection clock.
  6. Keep the occupancy promise. These zero-down and assistance programs are for a home you will live in. A rental you will not occupy belongs in a different conversation, often a conventional or DSCR loan with its own down payment.

Frequently asked questions.

Can a first-time buyer in New Jersey get a home loan with no down payment?

Yes, if you qualify for a VA purchase loan or a USDA guaranteed loan on an eligible property. Everyone else should expect a down payment, then look at gifts or New Jersey down-payment assistance to reduce the cash they bring. Approval still depends on credit, income, the appraisal, and the lender's guidelines.

Does zero down mean I need no cash at closing?

No. You can still owe closing costs, prepaid taxes and insurance, and a program fee such as the VA funding fee or the USDA upfront guarantee fee. Some of those fees can be financed or paid by the seller, within program caps. Ask for the cash-to-close figure before you write an offer.

Is an FHA loan a zero-down option?

No. FHA is a low-down loan. On EZ Way's program list it is a 3.5 percent down option, and gift funds are allowed. It becomes part of a zero-cash plan only if assistance or a gift covers that 3.5 percent and your closing costs, and the lender accepts that structure.

Do I have to be a New Jersey resident to use state down-payment help?

NJHMFA says you do not have to already live in New Jersey. The home must be in New Jersey, and you must occupy it as your principal residence. You also have to meet the agency's income, purchase-price, and first-time or target-area rules. The assistance has to be paired with an NJHMFA first mortgage.

Will a zero-down loan hurt me later?

You start with no equity from a down payment, so a price drop or an appraisal gap has fewer cushions. Financing the VA or USDA fee raises the balance you repay. The tradeoff is that you keep savings for repairs and moving. Whether that tradeoff fits is a credit and budget question, not a slogan.
