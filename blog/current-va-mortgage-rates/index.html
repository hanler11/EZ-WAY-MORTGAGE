What Are Current VA Mortgage Rates?

VA mortgage rates change frequently, so published figures are useful for market context, but they are not guaranteed offers. The rate available to a New Jersey borrower depends on several details, including credit history, loan purpose, loan amount, property type, occupancy, income, and whether the borrower pays discount points.

Published rates can still help you compare loan terms and understand how VA financing is priced. 30-Year Fixed VA Rates: 6.09%–6.44%

Published national averages for 30-year fixed VA mortgages range from approximately 6.09% to 6.44%. The exact figure may vary by lender and by whether the advertised offer includes discount points. With a fixed-rate mortgage, the interest rate remains the same throughout the loan term, making the principal and interest payment easier to plan.

A 30-year term generally provides a lower monthly payment than a 15-year term, although it may result in more interest paid over the life of the loan. Your personalized quote may differ from the published range because lenders evaluate your complete financial profile.

Use the latest VA mortgage rate information from Veterans United for general market context. Then ask a lender or broker to price the loan based on the New Jersey property, purchase price, and financial information in your application.

15-Year Fixed VA Rates: 5.50%–5.99%

Published 15-year fixed VA rates range from approximately 5.50% to 5.99%. Since the loan is paid off in half the time of a 30-year mortgage, the monthly principal and interest payment is usually higher. However, the shorter term may significantly reduce the total interest paid over the life of the loan.

A 15-year mortgage may suit borrowers with stable income and enough room in their budgets for a larger payment. It also allows homeowners to build equity more quickly. Before choosing this term, review the complete monthly housing cost, including property taxes, homeowners insurance, funding fees, and any applicable homeowners association dues.

Ask for 15-year and 30-year quotes based on the same loan amount and property. Comparing both options can show whether the potential interest savings are worth the higher monthly payment.

VA ARM Rates, Introductory Terms, and Caps

A VA Adjustable Rate Mortgage, or ARM, may begin with a lower introductory rate than certain fixed-rate loans. After the introductory period, the rate can change based on the loan’s index and margin. The initial period, adjustment schedule, annual caps, and lifetime cap depend on the specific program and lender.

An ARM may be worth considering if you expect to sell or refinance before the first adjustment. Still, you should understand how the payment could change if you keep the mortgage longer than planned. Ask the lender for the highest possible rate and monthly payment under the loan’s adjustment caps.

The Department of Veterans Affairs purchase loan guidance provides general information about VA-backed financing. Your lender can explain the ARM terms available for your purchase and help you compare them with fixed-rate alternatives.

Purchase, IRRRL, Cash-Out, and Jumbo VA Rates

VA rates can vary based on the purpose of the loan. A purchase loan helps finance a home you plan to buy. An Interest Rate Reduction Refinance Loan, or IRRRL, replaces an existing VA mortgage, usually with the goal of obtaining a lower rate or payment. A cash-out refinance may allow you to access home equity, but it can involve different pricing, fees, and underwriting requirements.

Published figures for a 30-year VA cash-out refinance are approximately 6.375%, while a 30-year VA IRRRL rate is about 6.125%. Jumbo VA loans may have different rates because they involve larger loan amounts and may require additional lender review, reserves, or underwriting documentation.

These figures should not be compared as if they were identical products. Request quotes for the same term, loan amount, points, and closing-cost structure before deciding which option is more affordable.

Compare Interest Rates, APR, Points, and Credits

The interest rate affects the principal and interest portion of your monthly payment, but it does not show every cost of borrowing. The Annual Percentage Rate, or APR, includes certain loan fees and can provide a broader view of the mortgage’s cost. Compare the interest rate and APR together rather than focusing on one number.

Discount points are upfront charges that may reduce your interest rate. Lender credits can help cover some closing costs, but they generally come with a higher rate. The better choice depends on how long you expect to keep the mortgage, how much cash you have available, and whether a lower payment matters more than a smaller amount due at closing.

How Do VA Rates Compare With Conventional and FHA Rates?

A VA mortgage may offer a lower interest rate than a comparable conventional or FHA loan, but the rate alone does not determine which option costs less. Your credit profile, loan amount, down payment, occupancy, property type, and lender pricing all affect the offer you receive. Compare the full payment and closing costs, not just the headline percentage.

VA loans also have features that can change how much cash you need upfront. No down payment and no monthly private mortgage insurance can make a meaningful difference for New Jersey buyers managing moving expenses, repairs, prepaid taxes, and other costs. An experienced mortgage broker can compare loan programs using the same assumptions, making it easier to review your options fairly.

Compare VA’s No-Down-Payment Option and No Monthly PMI

Eligible borrowers can use a VA loan to purchase a primary residence with no down payment. You can still make a down payment if you want to reduce the loan balance, lower the funding fee, or build equity sooner. However, putting money down is not required simply because you are using VA financing. Veterans United explains the VA no-down-payment benefit, which many eligible Veterans may not realize they can use.

VA loans also do not require monthly private mortgage insurance, even when you finance the entire purchase price. This can result in a lower monthly payment than a conventional loan with a similar balance and less than 20% down. You may still pay a one-time VA funding fee unless you qualify for an exemption, so review both the monthly payment and the complete loan cost.

Compare Conventional Rates, Down Payments, and PMI

Conventional loans generally allow a down payment as low as 3%, although requirements vary by program and borrower profile. If you put less than 20% down, the lender will typically require private mortgage insurance. PMI protects the lender, but it adds to your monthly payment until you meet the applicable cancellation requirements. Bankrate’s VA rate comparison explains how conventional down payments and PMI differ from VA financing.

A conventional loan may suit borrowers with excellent credit, substantial savings, or plans to make a larger down payment. It can also work for a wider range of property uses. Still, a slightly lower conventional rate may not produce a lower overall payment after PMI, closing costs, and the larger upfront cash requirement are included. Ask each lender to show the estimated monthly payment with and without PMI when possible.

Compare FHA Rates, MIP, and Upfront Costs

FHA loans typically require a minimum down payment of 3.5% for borrowers who meet the applicable credit requirements. They also include an upfront mortgage insurance premium and ongoing monthly mortgage insurance payments. The upfront premium may be financed into the loan, but doing so increases the balance and the interest paid over time. Veterans United’s VA rate guidance offers useful context when comparing VA and FHA financing.

FHA financing may help buyers who have limited savings or a lower credit score, but its mortgage insurance structure can increase the long-term cost. A VA loan may reduce monthly expenses because it does not carry monthly mortgage insurance. Compare the required cash, principal and interest, mortgage insurance, taxes, homeowners insurance, and total interest for both options.

The Department of Veterans Affairs does not set a universal minimum credit score for VA loans. Individual lenders establish their own requirements, often called overlays. Many lenders prefer a credit score of 620 or higher, although some may consider other factors when reviewing the complete application. VA rate guidance from Veterans United discusses the difference between VA guidelines and lender standards.

Credit score is only one part of the review. Lenders may also assess payment history, debt-to-income ratio, employment, assets, and residual income. Two borrowers with similar scores may receive different offers because their debts, reserves, income history, or loan amounts differ. A mortgage broker can help identify lenders whose requirements fit your financial profile instead of assuming every lender applies the same standards.

VA financing can reduce this amount because no down payment is required. Your credit history, debt, income, reserves, loan purpose, and rate-lock timing can all affect the pricing you receive.

Review Credit, Payment History, and Lender Overlays

Your credit profile helps lenders assess how you have managed borrowed money. They may review your credit scores, payment history, collection accounts, recent inquiries, and outstanding balances. Consistent, on-time payments and manageable balances can support stronger pricing. Late payments, unresolved debts, or recent financial problems may affect your approval, rate, or documentation requirements.

VA guidelines provide a general framework, but lenders can add requirements called overlays. One lender may accept your credit profile while another asks for a higher score, additional reserves, or explanations for past issues. The VA credit and underwriting guidance explains the broader review process.

Before applying, check your credit reports for errors and avoid opening unnecessary accounts. If your credit needs attention, ask a mortgage professional which changes may have the greatest effect before requesting rate quotes.

Assess Debt-to-Income Ratio, Residual Income, and Reserves

Your debt-to-income ratio, or DTI, compares your monthly debt payments with your gross monthly income. Lenders may include auto loans, student loans, credit card minimums, child support, and the proposed housing payment. A lower DTI can make an application easier to approve, but VA underwriting considers more than this one number.

Lenders also review residual income, the money left after major obligations are paid. The required amount depends on household size, loan amount, and property location. The VA residual income guidelines help explain how this standard is applied.

Cash reserves may also support an application, particularly for jumbo loans, self-employed borrowers, or applicants with variable income. Keep bank statements and asset records organized so your lender can verify funds without unnecessary delays.

Consider Loan Amount, Entitlement, Down Payment, and LTV

The amount you borrow affects your payment, loan structure, and possibly your rate. A standard VA loan may receive different pricing from a larger jumbo loan, which can require additional review. Your available VA entitlement also matters because it determines how much of your benefit you can use for a new mortgage.

Eligible borrowers may qualify for a VA loan with no down payment when they have sufficient entitlement and meet lender requirements. However, a down payment can reduce the loan balance and may lower the funding fee in certain cases. The VA funding fee guidance explains how down payment amounts affect funding-fee tiers.

Loan-to-value, or LTV, compares the loan amount with the property value. A lower LTV may reduce lender risk, although VA pricing depends on several factors. Ask how different down payment amounts would change your rate, fees, payment, and cash needed at closing.

Choose a Loan Purpose, Term, and Rate Type

Your reason for borrowing can affect your available options and pricing. Purchase loans, Interest Rate Reduction Refinance Loans (IRRRLs), cash-out refinances, and jumbo VA loans may have different rates. A refinance may also be evaluated based on whether it lowers your payment, changes your term, or provides access to home equity.

The loan term matters as well. A 30-year fixed mortgage generally provides a lower monthly payment, while a 15-year mortgage may have a different rate and a higher payment. A fixed-rate loan keeps the principal and interest payment consistent. An adjustable-rate mortgage can change after its initial period. The VA adjustable-rate mortgage information explains how these loans work.

Compare the complete cost of each option, not just the advertised rate. A lower rate may include discount points, higher closing costs, or a shorter term that does not fit your budget.

Review Property Type, Occupancy, Appraisal, and Condition

VA loans are generally intended for a home you will occupy as your primary residence. Eligible properties may include single-family homes, certain condominiums, manufactured homes, and some multi-unit properties, as long as they meet VA and lender requirements. A vacation home or typical investment property generally does not qualify for standard VA purchase financing.

The appraisal estimates the property’s market value and checks whether it meets VA minimum property requirements. Safety, sanitation, structural, or utility issues may need to be corrected before closing. The VA appraisal process provides more detail about what borrowers can expect.

Property type can affect lender requirements and pricing. Condominiums may need project approval, while manufactured homes, older properties, and multi-unit residences may require additional documentation. If the appraisal is lower than the purchase price, you may need to renegotiate, pay the difference, or reconsider the property.

Document Employment and Self-Employed Income

Lenders need reliable evidence that you can repay the mortgage. Employees may be asked for recent pay stubs, W-2 forms, tax returns, and employment verification. Bonuses, commissions, overtime, and other variable income may require additional documentation to show that the income is stable and likely to continue.

Self-employed borrowers often provide personal and business tax returns, year-to-date profit and loss statements, business licenses, and recent bank statements. Lenders may review your ownership percentage, income trends, business expenses, write-offs, and time in business. The VA underwriting guidelines outline income factors lenders may consider.

Keep your records consistent and respond quickly to document requests. Large deposits, job changes, new business debt, or unexplained transfers can lead to follow-up questions. Ask for a document checklist early so you can prepare before underwriting begins.

Track Market Conditions, Lender Pricing, and Rate Locks

Mortgage rates change as financial markets and economic conditions shift. Lenders may update pricing during the day, so a quote usually applies to a specific loan scenario and time. An online rate may not match your final offer after the lender reviews your credit, income, property, and loan purpose.

A rate lock holds your interest rate for a set period, often through the expected closing date. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s rate-lock guidance explains why borrowers should confirm the lock period, cost, and process for delays.

Ask whether your quote is locked or floating, how long the lock lasts, and whether an extension fee applies. You can also ask about a float-down option, which may allow you to receive a lower rate if pricing improves. Compare quotes on the same day using identical loan details.

Weigh Discount Points Against Lender Credits

Discount points are upfront charges paid to reduce your interest rate. Lender credits work in the opposite way: the lender covers some closing costs in exchange for a higher rate. Neither option is always better. The right choice depends on your available cash, expected time in the home, and plans for the mortgage.

To estimate the breakeven point, divide the cost of the points by your expected monthly savings. If the savings take eight years to recover and you expect to move or refinance sooner, paying points may not be worthwhile.

Lender credits may be helpful when you want to preserve cash for moving costs, repairs, or reserves. Review the CFPB guidance on discount points and lender credits with your Loan Estimate. Compare the rate, APR, closing costs, monthly payment, and cash to close before making a decision.

Understand Funding-Fee Tiers, Exemptions, and Financing

The VA funding fee helps support the VA home loan program. Its amount depends on several factors, including your military category, loan purpose, down payment, and whether you have used your VA benefit before. A larger down payment may reduce the fee, while some borrowers qualify for an exemption.

Veterans receiving qualifying disability compensation may not have to pay the funding fee. Certain surviving spouses and other eligible borrowers may also qualify for an exemption. The VA funding fee guidance explains the current rules and qualifying circumstances.

You may pay the funding fee at closing or finance it into the loan. Financing keeps more money in your bank account upfront, but it increases the loan balance and the interest paid over time. Ask your lender to show both choices in writing, including the revised payment and total loan cost.

Review Origination, Underwriting, Processing, and Lender Fees

Your lender may charge an origination fee for preparing and arranging the mortgage. Other charges can include underwriting, processing, administration, document preparation, rate-lock, or technology fees. Lenders do not always use the same names for these services, so compare the dollar amounts and ask what each charge covers.

VA rules limit certain charges and require lenders to follow specific guidelines. A lender may charge a reasonable flat origination fee or itemize permitted services, depending on the loan structure. Ask whether each fee is paid to the lender, a third-party provider, or a government office.

Review the loan costs section when you receive your Loan Estimate. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s Loan Estimate guide explains where to find lender charges, services you can shop for, and lender credits. If a fee changes later, ask why and request an updated explanation.

Account for Appraisal, Title, Recording, Credit, and Third-Party Costs

A VA-approved appraiser estimates the property’s market value and checks whether it meets VA minimum property requirements. The borrower generally pays the appraisal fee, although the timing and amount can vary. A home inspection is separate from the VA appraisal and may be helpful, particularly when buying an older home or a property with visible maintenance concerns.

Other costs may include a title search, lender’s title insurance, owner’s title insurance, attorney or settlement services, credit reports, flood certification, surveys, recording fees, and required inspections. Some charges apply to nearly every transaction, while others depend on the property, lender, location, or settlement provider.

New Jersey title and attorney fees may vary by county and transaction. Request an itemized estimate and ask which services you can shop for. The VA purchase-loan guidance explains the general process and property requirements.

Budget for Taxes, Insurance, and Escrow

Prepaid and escrow items can make your cash to close higher than expected, even though they are not lender profits. These charges may include the initial homeowners insurance premium, property tax reserves, daily interest from closing through the end of the month, and future escrow deposits.

New Jersey property taxes are an important part of your housing budget. They can vary significantly by municipality, school district, assessment, and tax year. If the seller has already paid taxes, the closing statement may include a credit or adjustment between the buyer and seller.

Your lender estimates these costs before closing, but the final figures may change after your insurance policy, tax information, and closing date are confirmed. Calculate the Breakeven Point for Points and Credits

Discount points allow you to pay more upfront in exchange for a lower interest rate. Lender credits work in the opposite direction, reducing some closing costs in exchange for a higher rate. Either choice can be useful, depending on your available cash and how long you expect to keep the loan.

To estimate the breakeven period, divide the cost of the points by the monthly payment savings. For example, if points cost $4,000 and lower the payment by $100 per month, the breakeven point is 40 months. If you keep the loan longer than that, the payment savings may outweigh the upfront cost, assuming the loan terms do not change.

A lender credit may help if you need cash for moving expenses, repairs, or financial reserves. Ask for side-by-side quotes with zero points, discount points, and lender credits. Comparing all three options can show which structure best fits your plans.

Apply VA Closing-Cost Rules and Seller Contributions

VA borrowers generally pay allowable closing costs, but the seller may agree to cover some expenses through the purchase contract. Seller-paid closing costs can reduce the money you need at closing, but the agreement must comply with VA guidelines and the terms of the contract.

VA rules distinguish between ordinary closing costs and seller concessions. Ordinary costs customarily paid by the seller are treated differently from concessions, which may include paying the buyer’s debts, prepaid taxes or insurance, or other benefits. Seller concessions are generally subject to a 4% limit based on the property’s reasonable value. Customary closing costs paid by the seller do not count toward that same limit.

Have your lender and real estate attorney review the contract before you rely on a seller contribution. The VA Lenders Handbook provides official underwriting guidance. Your mortgage professional can also explain how the rules apply to your purchase price, loan structure, and closing costs.

Account for Fees Even Without Monthly PMI

VA loans do not require monthly private mortgage insurance (PMI), which may result in a lower payment than a comparable conventional loan with a small down payment. However, avoiding PMI does not remove the other costs associated with borrowing and owning a home.

You still need to account for the funding fee, lender charges, appraisal, title services, taxes, homeowners insurance, escrow deposits, and interest. Depending on the structure, a VA loan may have a higher cash-to-close amount or a larger financed balance even without a monthly PMI charge.

Compare the full monthly payment, not just principal and interest. Include property taxes, homeowners insurance, and homeowners association dues when applicable. Then compare the total cost over the period you expect to own the home. Review IRRRL Rates and Lower-Payment Goals

An Interest Rate Reduction Refinance Loan, commonly called an IRRRL or VA Streamline refinance, helps eligible borrowers replace an existing VA loan. The usual goals are to reduce the interest rate, lower the payment, or change from an adjustable-rate loan to a fixed-rate loan. Because the process may require less documentation than some other refinance options, IRRRL pricing can be competitive.

One published example listed a 30-year fixed VA Streamline refinance rate at 6.125%. Treat that number as a reference point, not a personal quote. Your offer may differ based on the lender, loan balance, term, points, credit profile, and other pricing factors.

A lower payment does not always mean a lower overall cost. If you restart a 30-year loan, you may reduce the monthly payment while extending repayment and paying interest for more years. Compare your current balance, remaining term, proposed term, monthly payment, APR, closing costs, and total interest. The VA’s IRRRL guidance explains the program’s basic requirements.

Meet IRRRL Seasoning, Net-Benefit, and Recoupment Rules

An IRRRL has eligibility requirements beyond obtaining a lower advertised rate. Generally, you must have made at least six consecutive monthly payments on your existing VA loan, and at least 210 days must have passed since the first payment was made. Your lender will verify your payment history and review the loan’s timing before moving forward.

The new loan must also provide a net tangible benefit. This may involve a lower interest rate or payment, or a change from an ARM to a fixed-rate mortgage. The lender must document that the refinance offers a meaningful financial or stability benefit under applicable VA rules.

Recoupment is another important factor. Eligible refinance costs generally must be recovered through monthly savings within the applicable period, commonly 36 months. Ask which charges the lender includes in the calculation and whether costs are paid upfront or added to the loan balance. If the savings take too long to recover the closing costs, refinancing may not fit your goals.

Compare Cash-Out Rates, Equity, and Appraisal Requirements

A VA cash-out refinance replaces your current mortgage and lets you access part of your home equity. Eligible borrowers may use the proceeds for home improvements, debt consolidation, education expenses, or other permitted purposes. A cash-out refinance may also be available when the existing mortgage is not a VA loan, provided you meet VA and lender requirements.

Cash-out rates can be higher than purchase or IRRRL rates because the new loan may increase the balance and reduce the equity remaining in the property. Your rate may vary based on equity, credit, occupancy, loan size, and lender guidelines.

Compare VA Refinancing With a HELOC or Equity Loan

Refinancing is not the only way to access home equity. A home equity line of credit, or HELOC, lets you borrow funds as needed, while a home equity loan generally provides a lump sum with a separate repayment schedule. Both options may allow you to keep your existing first mortgage, which can be helpful if it has a particularly favorable rate.

A HELOC commonly carries a variable interest rate, so the payment may change over time. What VA Mortgage Rate Misconceptions Should You Know?

VA loans can offer competitive rates, flexible qualifying guidelines, and the option to purchase a primary residence with no down payment. However, several details are easy to misunderstand. Your rate and terms may depend on your credit history, income, debts, loan purpose, property type, loan amount, and the lender’s pricing. Closing costs also affect the overall expense, even though VA loans typically do not require monthly private mortgage insurance. The VA home loan program establishes important guidelines, while lenders apply those guidelines during underwriting and may add their own requirements.

The VA Does Not Set or Guarantee Mortgage Rates

The Department of Veterans Affairs guarantees part of an eligible borrower’s loan, but it does not lend the money directly or set one rate for every VA borrower. Banks, credit unions, and mortgage brokers determine their own pricing based on market conditions, loan details, and the borrower’s qualifications.

As a result, two eligible NJ borrowers may receive different offers from different lenders. Rates can also change as financial markets move. The VA guarantee may reduce a lender’s risk, but it does not guarantee approval or ensure that you receive the lowest available rate.

VA Eligibility Does Not Guarantee Approval

Qualifying military service is an important first step, but it is not the same as mortgage approval. A lender must also review your income, credit, debts, assets, employment history, and the property you plan to purchase.

The VA does not set one universal minimum credit score for every borrower. Individual lenders may establish their own score requirements and other overlays. They may also review your payment history, recent credit activity, debt-to-income ratio, and income stability. Self-employed applicants may need additional tax returns and business records.
