Learn how an investment property cash-out refinance works, what you need to qualify, and how to use your rental property equity for your financial goals.

What Is an Investment Property Cash-Out Refinance?

An investment property cash-out refinance lets you borrow against equity in a rental or other income-producing property. You replace the current mortgage with a new one, use it to pay off the existing loan, and may receive remaining funds at closing. The amount available depends on the property’s appraised value, your mortgage payoff, lender limits, and your financial profile.

Replace Your Mortgage With a Larger Loan

A cash-out refinance replaces your current mortgage with a new loan secured by the same property. The new loan pays off the old balance, and you may receive some of the difference as a lump sum after closing costs and other required payoffs are deducted. As Freedom Mortgage explains, the amount you can take out depends on the new loan and what remains after the existing mortgage is paid.

The new mortgage comes with its own rate, repayment term, and monthly payment. Review all three, not just the cash you might receive, and remember that financing for an investment property may have different requirements from a mortgage on your primary home. A mortgage professional can help you compare available loan programs and assess which options fit your property and plans.

Calculate Cash Proceeds From Appraised Value, Loan-to-Value Limits, and Payoff

Lenders typically use an appraisal to estimate the property’s current value, then apply a maximum loan-to-value (LTV) limit. LTV compares the loan amount with the appraised value. Some investment property lenders set cash-out limits around 70% to 75% LTV, though requirements vary by lender, loan program, property, and borrower. LendingOne’s overview describes how LTV limits can affect the equity available to borrow.

For a rough estimate, multiply the appraised value by the applicable LTV limit. Subtract the existing mortgage payoff, closing costs, and any other debts that must be paid from the loan. What remains is an estimate, not a guaranteed payout. The appraisal, underwriting review, and lender rules determine the final loan amount and proceeds.

Compare It With a HELOC or Home Equity Loan

A cash-out refinance provides funds as a lump sum and replaces your existing mortgage. The new loan may have a fixed rate and payment, depending on the program, but refinancing means changing the terms on the full mortgage balance. If your current loan has a lower rate, the new rate could apply to a larger balance than the amount of cash you need.

A home equity loan also provides a lump sum, while a home equity line of credit (HELOC) lets you draw funds as needed up to an approved limit. HELOC rates are often variable, and availability for rental properties depends on the lender. Navy Federal’s comparison outlines how lump-sum loans differ from revolving credit. Compare rates, fees, repayment terms, and access to funds before choosing.

What Are the Typical Eligibility Requirements?

Lenders review several parts of your application before approving a cash-out refinance on an investment property. They’ll look at the property’s value and condition, the equity you have, your credit and income, and whether you can handle the new payment. They may also consider rental income, the number of units, your cash reserves, and how long you’ve owned the property.

Requirements vary by lender and loan program. The figures below are common examples, not a promise of approval. A mortgage professional can help you understand which criteria apply to your situation and what documents to prepare.

Equity and Maximum Loan-to-Value Limits

Your equity helps determine the maximum loan amount a lender may approve. Many lenders limit cash-out refinances on investment properties to about 70% to 75% loan-to-value, or LTV. That generally means you’ll need to retain 25% to 30% equity after closing, though the limit depends on the lender, property, and loan program.

For example, if your property appraises for four hundred thousand dollars and the maximum LTV is 75%, the new mortgage may be capped at three hundred thousand dollars. Your existing mortgage payoff and closing costs still need to come out of that amount, so the cash you receive will be lower. Review common LTV guidelines, then ask lenders how they calculate the limit for your property.

Credit Score, Debt-to-Income Ratio, and Income Documents

Lenders assess your credit history, income, and monthly debt payments to decide whether you can manage the new mortgage. Cash-out refinancing for an investment property may call for stronger credit and a lower debt-to-income ratio than a refinance on a primary home. Some lenders cite credit score ranges of about 640 to 680 for one-unit properties and 680 to 700 for two-to-four-unit properties. These are examples, not universal minimums.

Gather documents that show your income, assets, and debts. Depending on your circumstances, a lender may request pay stubs, W-2 forms, tax returns, bank statements, and details about other mortgages. Self-employed borrowers may need business records as well. Investment property refinance guidelines can give you a sense of the financial factors lenders may review.

Rental Income, Property Type, Unit Count, and Condition

Rental income may help show that the property can support its mortgage payment. Lenders decide how much of that income they’ll count, and may ask for lease agreements, rental history, or tax returns. They may also consider whether the property generates positive cash flow. Check how lenders assess rental income, since the calculation can differ by loan program.

The property’s unit count and condition can also affect eligibility. A single-family rental may be assessed differently from a two-to-four-unit property, and the appraisal helps establish its value and condition. Significant repairs could raise questions about the property’s marketability or eligibility. If the rental is vacant, recently renovated, or has multiple units, tell your lender early and ask which records or inspections they’ll require.

Cash Reserves and Ownership Period

Some lenders require cash reserves after closing, in addition to funds needed to complete the refinance. A common guideline is six to 12 months of mortgage payments. The required amount can depend on your credit, the number of properties you finance, and the loan program. Ask whether reserves must be held in a particular type of account and how the lender calculates the required balance.

You may also need to own the property for a certain length of time before taking cash out. Some lenders require a six-month to one-year ownership period, sometimes called a seasoning requirement. Rules can vary, so confirm the timeline before relying on the proceeds for a purchase or project. Refinance requirements for investment properties can differ by lender and borrower.

Compare Lender and Loan Program Requirements

Lenders don’t all use the same eligibility standards. One may permit a higher LTV, while another may focus more heavily on your credit, rental income, property type, or cash reserves. Loan programs can have different criteria, too, so compare the full set of terms rather than choosing based on a single rate or requirement.

Ask each lender about its maximum LTV, credit score guidelines, debt-to-income limits, reserve requirements, ownership rules, fees, and document checklist. How Much Cash Could You Receive?

The amount you receive from an investment property cash-out refinance depends on the property’s appraised value, your lender’s loan-to-value limit, your current mortgage payoff, and the costs of the new loan. The maximum you can borrow is not the same as the cash you’ll take home: part of the new loan pays off your existing mortgage, and fees or other debts can further reduce the proceeds.

To build a useful estimate, start with the property’s likely value and the lender’s maximum loan amount. Then subtract the mortgage payoff and any costs or debts that will be paid from the new loan. The result is an estimate, not a promise. The appraised value, final payoff amount, program rules, and underwriting decision can all affect the amount available at closing.

For example, if a rental property appraises for four hundred thousand dollars and the lender allows a 70% LTV, the loan limit based on that calculation would be two hundred eighty thousand dollars. Subtract the Mortgage Payoff, Closing Costs, and Other Debts

Once you have an estimated new loan amount, subtract the current mortgage payoff. Use a recent payoff statement if possible, because the amount may include interest through a specific date or other charges that differ from the balance on your latest statement. Then subtract closing costs, prepaid expenses, and any other debts you plan to pay with the refinance.

Closing costs can vary by lender, property, and loan terms. Some may be rolled into the new loan if the program permits it, but that increases the loan balance and leaves less cash available to you.
